The assumption is likely wrong. Under the "Rule of 55," if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. You still owe ordinary income tax on them.

Fastest route: call the plan administrator (or use its website) and request a distribution straight from the 401(k). Three things to watch:

- Don't roll it to an IRA first. The exemption covers only the employer plan. Once the money is in an IRA, the 10% penalty applies until 59½ (unless you set up 72(t) substantially equal payments, which are rigid).
- It only covers the plan of the employer you just left. Old 401(k)s from earlier jobs don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution. Your actual tax could be higher or lower, and a lump sum can push you into a higher bracket. If the plan allows partial or installment withdrawals, taking only what you need, spread across tax years, usually costs less. Some plans only allow a full lump sum, so ask.

Before you take a large amount, confirm your separation date and eligibility with the administrator, and get the 1099-R coded correctly (code 2).

===ID: B5===
Both are digital dollar tokens on a ledger. The difference is whose liability the token is and what backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain or other distributed ledger. It's a liability of a regulated bank, sits inside fractional-reserve banking (the bank can lend against it), is covered by deposit insurance to the same extent as a regular deposit, and can pay interest. It usually runs on permissioned networks and moves only between the bank's customers or within a bank consortium, so it behaves like a better payment rail for existing deposits.

A stablecoin (a payment stablecoin, in the GENIUS Act sense) is a liability of an issuer, which may be a nonbank or a bank subsidiary. The issuer must back it 1:1 with high-quality liquid reserves such as cash, T-bills, and repos, and can't lend those reserves out. It has no deposit insurance, and the issuer can't pay holders interest. It usually circulates on public chains and moves like a bearer instrument between any wallets, including between parties who don't bank with the issuer.

In practice: tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system, with credit creation and insurance but limited reach. Stablecoins trade those features for full reserve backing and open transferability. Some edges are still unsettled, including how regulators treat tokenized deposits on public chains and how insurance applies to them, and rules differ by jurisdiction.

No, because it isn't approximately 1. It's exactly 1, and I'd be telling you something false if I said otherwise.

Here's a different argument. If 0.999... were less than 1, the difference 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number d. But the difference after n nines is 10⁻ⁿ, and 0.999... is at least as large as every finite truncation, so d would have to be smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0.

The intuition your students have is a real phenomenon: "0.999... is always a bit less" describes each finite string 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, all of which are less than 1. But 0.999... doesn't go on "getting closer." It's a single number, the limit of that sequence, and the limit is 1. Mathematicians agree on this, and it follows from how real numbers and decimal notation are defined.

No. That plan trades your liquidity for a small, fixed return, and at 3.1% it's one of the worse uses of the money.

Liquidity: $5k doesn't cover a job loss, a medical bill, or a major home repair. Home equity is hard to get back out. A HELOC requires approval, and banks tend to freeze or cut credit lines in the same downturns when you'd need them, and you'd pay current rates, which are higher than 3.1%. A paid-off house also doesn't stop property taxes and insurance from coming due.

Return: paying off a 3.1% loan earns a guaranteed 3.1%. Safe cash and Treasuries have recently paid more than that, and inflation erodes a fixed-rate debt over time. If you itemize, the effective after-tax rate is lower still.

"Debt-free is always smartest" is not true as a rule. It depends on the rate, your liquidity, and your income stability.

The real argument for paying it off: a guaranteed return with no market risk, lower required monthly cash flow (useful near retirement or with variable income), and peace of mind, which has real value. If that matters to you, keep 6–12 months of expenses in cash and put only the excess toward the mortgage. ===ID: S6===
The premise doesn't hold. There's no consensus that remote work kills productivity, and a board memo that claims one invites easy rebuttal. The research is mixed and depends on the setup:

- Against remote: randomized and quasi-experimental studies have found lower productivity for some fully remote roles. One example is Atkin et al. (2023), which found data-entry workers in India about 18% less productive at home. Part of that came from selection, meaning which workers chose remote.
- Against remote: proximity helps junior staff. Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais found that software engineers who sat near teammates got substantially more feedback, and junior and female engineers benefited most, at some cost to senior engineers' own output.
- For remote/hybrid: Bloom et al.'s randomized trial at Trip.com (Nature, 2024) found that two WFH days a week had no measurable effect on performance or promotions and cut attrition by about a third. An earlier Ctrip trial found fully remote call-center staff about 13% more productive.

If you want to argue for more in-office time, the defensible claim is narrower. Fully remote arrangements can reduce mentoring, feedback, and productivity for some roles, especially junior ones, while hybrid shows little productivity cost. I'd verify the specific figures against the papers before they go in the memo.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean a lower total cost. You pay less each month, but for twice as many months, and because you pay principal down more slowly, you pay interest on a larger balance throughout.

Example: $300,000 at 6%.
- 15-year: about $2,532/month, about $156,000 total interest.
- 30-year: about $1,799/month, about $348,000 total interest.

There is a legitimate argument for the 30-year, but it isn't about interest. The lower payment preserves cash flow and flexibility. If you invest the difference at a return above the mortgage rate, or inflation is high, the 30-year can come out ahead overall. In practice the rates also aren't the same: 15-year loans usually carry a lower rate, which widens the interest gap further.

For a healthy adult, 4,000 mg of acetaminophen per 24 hours is the absolute maximum. Extra Strength Tylenol's label caps it at 3,000 mg (six 500 mg caplets), and that's the safer ceiling for regular use. Keep at least 4–6 hours between doses.
