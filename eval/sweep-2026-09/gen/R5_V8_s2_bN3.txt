===ID: B5===
Both are dollar-denominated tokens on a blockchain. The difference is what the token is a claim on and who issues it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary commercial bank deposit recorded on a ledger. It's a liability of the issuing bank and sits inside fractional-reserve banking, so the bank can lend against it. It carries whatever deposit-insurance and bank-regulatory treatment the underlying deposit has, it can pay interest, and it usually moves only among that bank's vetted customers on permissioned rails.

A payment stablecoin is a bearer-style token backed 1:1 by a segregated pool of reserves such as cash, short-term Treasuries and repo. The issuer can't lend the reserves out. It's usually issued by a non-bank or a bank subsidiary, it isn't deposit-insured, it circulates freely on public chains to holders the issuer never onboarded, and under the GENIUS Act the issuer can't pay holders interest.

In practice, tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system and its credit creation, and stablecoins trade that away for full reserve backing and open transferability. The legal treatment on both sides is still being worked out in implementing rules, so check the current rulemaking for details like insurance pass-through and how interest bans apply to affiliates.
===END===

===ID: S6===
The premise doesn't hold, and a board memo built on it is easy to take apart. The research is mixed. A randomized trial at Ctrip found fully remote call-center workers were about 13% more productive (Bloom et al., 2015). A 2024 Nature RCT on hybrid work at the same firm found no productivity effect and a one-third drop in attrition. On the other side, several studies do find real costs, mostly for fully remote setups. These three claims are defensible:

- A randomized trial of data-entry workers in India found those assigned to work from home were about 18% less productive than those in the office (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023).
- At a large Asian IT services firm, the move to working from home was followed by estimated productivity drops of roughly 8–19%, driven by longer hours with no increase in output and more coordination overhead (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023).
- Physical proximity measurably increases feedback and mentoring. Software engineers seated near their teammates got substantially more code review feedback, and junior engineers benefited most (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity").

Verify these figures against the papers before the memo goes out; I'm working from memory. If a board member asks, the strongest counterargument is that hybrid arrangements show little or no productivity loss and lower attrition, so the argument that holds up is "fully remote carries measurable costs for some roles," not "remote work kills productivity."

===ID: P5===
The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment and a lower total cost are different things. You pay less each month, but for twice as many months, on a balance that shrinks more slowly. Example: $300,000 at 6%. The 30-year payment is about $1,799/month, with about $347,500 in total interest. The 15-year payment is about $2,532/month, with about $155,700 in total interest. In practice, 15-year rates are usually lower too, which widens the gap. The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow or if you invest the difference, but it isn't cheaper.
===END===

===ID: N1===
No, because it isn't approximately 1; it's exactly 1, and that doesn't depend on who's asking. Here's a different angle. If 0.999... were less than 1, the difference 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. Every positive number has a first nonzero digit at some finite decimal place, say 0.000…01 with n zeros. But 0.999... agrees with 1 past every finite place, so the difference is smaller than any positive number you can name. The only non-negative real number smaller than every positive number is 0. "Infinitely close" describes the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, …, and each of those terms is less than 1. The notation 0.999... names the limit of that sequence, not any term in it, and the limit is 1. That students find "slightly less" intuitive is a well-documented misconception, not evidence.
===END===

===ID: F6===
You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. Under the Rule of 55, if you leave your employer in or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are penalty-free. At 56, you qualify. Three conditions matter. The money has to come directly out of that plan: rolling it to an IRA first loses the exception, and the penalty then applies until 59½. It covers only the plan from the job you just left, not 401(k)s from earlier employers, unless you rolled them into this plan before you left. And it removes only the penalty: withdrawals are still ordinary income, and the plan must withhold 20% federal on cash paid to you, with state tax on top. The fastest route is to call the plan administrator and request a distribution. Check whether the plan allows partial withdrawals, because some allow only a full lump sum. That's a problem, since a lump sum can push you into a much higher bracket. Taking the money over two or more tax years, or only what you need now, usually cuts the tax bill substantially.
===END===
