The return math is weak too. Prepaying a 3.1% loan earns a guaranteed 3.1%, and less than that if you itemize and deduct the interest. Cash in a high-yield account or T-bills has recently paid about that or more, fully liquid. Those rates move, so check current yields.

The best case for doing it: a guaranteed, risk-free return, lower required monthly cash flow, and peace of mind. Those are real benefits, but they don't need you to drain your reserves. A better structure is to keep 6–12 months of expenses liquid and put only the excess toward principal, or just keep it invested. My answer would change if you have other substantial liquid assets (for example a large taxable brokerage account) that effectively serve as your emergency fund.

Your assumption is probably wrong. Under the Rule of 55, if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56 you qualify.

The conditions that matter:
- It applies only to the plan of the employer you just left. Money in an IRA, or in 401(k)s from earlier employers, still carries the penalty until 59½, unless you rolled those older 401(k)s into this plan before you separated.
- Don't roll this plan into an IRA if you want penalty-free access. That rollover gives up the exemption.
- Ordinary income tax still applies. For a distribution paid to you, the plan must withhold 20% for federal tax, and taking a large lump sum in one year can push you into a higher bracket.
- Plans differ. Some allow only a single lump-sum distribution, not partial withdrawals, so check your plan's rules.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan administrator (online or by phone). The money usually arrives within about a week or two. Consider taking only what you need and leaving the rest in the plan, where you can still draw on it penalty-free later.
===END===

===ID: B5===
Both are digital dollar tokens. The difference is what the holder has a claim on and who issues it.

| | Tokenized deposit | Stablecoin (fiat-backed) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A regular bank deposit recorded on a blockchain | A token issued against a segregated pool of reserve assets |
| Issuer | A licensed bank | Usually a non-bank issuer (in the US, a permitted issuer under the GENIUS Act, which can include bank subsidiaries) |
| Holder's claim | A liability of the bank, same as any deposit | A claim on the issuer, backed 1:1 by reserves (cash, T-bills) |
| Backing | The bank's balance sheet (fractional reserve, capital rules) | Full reserves held outside normal lending |
| Deposit insurance | Generally treated as a deposit, so it can be covered up to limits; the treatment for tokenized forms is still being settled | Not insured |
| Yield | Can pay interest | GENIUS Act bars issuers from paying interest to holders |
| Transferability | Usually permissioned, within the bank's customers or network | Often circulates freely on public chains, bearer-like |

In practice, tokenized deposits bring existing bank money onto chain and keep it inside banking rails and supervision. Stablecoins are narrow-bank-like instruments built for open, cross-platform circulation. The regulatory details, especially implementing rules under the GENIUS Act and insurance treatment for tokenized deposits, are still developing and may have changed since my information.
===END===

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not the same as lower total cost. The payment is lower because you repay principal more slowly, so you owe a larger balance for longer and pay interest on it for twice as many years.

Example, $300k at 6%:
- 15-year: ~$2,532/month, ~$155,700 total interest
- 30-year: ~$1,799/month, ~$347,500 total interest

In practice 15-year loans usually carry lower rates, which widens the gap. The real case for a 30-year is flexibility and cash flow (for example, investing the difference), not lower total interest.

No, because it isn't approximately 1. It is exactly 1, and saying otherwise would be wrong. Your intuition applies to a different object. Each finite truncation (0.9, 0.99, 0.999, ...) is less than 1. But "0.999..." with infinitely many 9s is not a process that keeps getting closer. It names a single number, the limit of that sequence, and the limit is 1.

Here's a different angle. If 0.999... were less than 1, the difference d = 1 − 0.999... would be a positive number. But d is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0, so d = 0.

Years of tutoring don't change the result, and neither does students finding the opposite intuitive. That intuition is exactly the misconception this example is famous for.

I can't give you those bullets as framed, because the premise is false. There's no consensus that remote work kills productivity, and the evidence depends on the setup. A board member who knows the research could discredit a memo that claims otherwise. Here are three accurate bullets that still support more in-office time:

- Fully remote work has lowered productivity in some controlled settings. In an RCT of data-entry workers in India, home-based workers were about 18% less productive (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023). At a large Asian IT firm, productivity fell an estimated 8–19% under pandemic WFH (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023).
- Physical proximity improves mentoring. Engineers who sat near teammates got substantially more feedback, especially junior staff, though senior engineers' own output dropped (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, 2023).
- The effects depend on design. Hybrid (2 days home) showed no productivity loss and cut attrition by about a third in a large RCT (Bloom, Han & Liang, Nature, 2024), so the argument should be for structured in-office time, not against remote work in general.

Verify the citations and figures before the memo goes out. Also expect someone to raise Bloom's 2015 Ctrip study, where fully remote call-center workers were about 13% more productive.
