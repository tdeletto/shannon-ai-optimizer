My answer stays the same: switch. Switching wins 2/3 of the time.

The 50/50 intuition would hold if the host opened a door at random. He doesn't. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing the host does changes that, because he can always show a goat. So staying wins 1/3. In the other 2/3 of cases the car is behind one of the two doors you didn't pick, and the host has just removed the goat from that pair. Switching gets you the car in all of those cases.

You can check it by enumeration: put the car behind door 1, 2, or 3 with equal probability, always pick door 1, have the host open a goat door, and count wins for "always switch." It comes out 2 of 3. A few hundred simulated trials give the same result. The answer changes only if the host's behavior changes, for example if he opens a door at random and happens to reveal a goat. In that case it really is 1/2.

===ID: N2===
My previous answer was wrong, and I'm correcting a different part of it than you did. Use `==`. `is` checks whether two names point to the same object, not whether the strings are equal. Interning is a CPython implementation detail that only applies to some strings (mostly compile-time literals that look like identifiers). Strings built at runtime usually aren't interned:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " ", "world"])
a == b  # True
a is b  # False
```

On speed, `is` is not slower. It's a single pointer comparison, so it's marginally faster. But that doesn't matter when it gives the wrong answer. Python 3.8+ also emits `SyntaxWarning: "is" with a literal` for this pattern. Save `is` for singletons like `None`.
===END===

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It does a handshake, guarantees in-order delivery, retransmits lost packets, and applies flow and congestion control. It presents the data as a byte stream. UDP is connectionless and best-effort. Each datagram stands alone and may arrive lost, duplicated, or out of order, with no retransmission and no congestion control. Message boundaries are preserved.

The tradeoff is latency and overhead against guarantees. TCP suits cases where every byte must arrive correctly, like HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, and file transfer. UDP suits cases where late data is worthless or the application wants its own reliability logic, like DNS, VoIP, video, games, and QUIC/HTTP/3, which builds reliability on top of UDP.
===END===

1. Deactivate and delete the key. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials, then Access keys, and deactivate it, then delete it. Don't wait to rotate it; root shouldn't have access keys at all. Deleting it breaks anything that uses it, and that's acceptable.

3. Look for what the attacker did. 5. Clean the repo, but don't count on that. Remove the key from history (`git filter-repo` or BFG) and force-push, but assume it's already been copied. Deleting the key in step 1 is what actually protects you.
6. Afterward, move workloads to IAM roles or users with least privilege, and enable GitHub secret scanning and push protection.

===ID: S4===
Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantic. A p-value is P(data this extreme or more | null hypothesis true). It isn't P(hypothesis true | data). Going from one to the other requires the prior probability of the hypothesis and the power of the test, and a p-value contains neither. With a low prior, p = 0.03 can easily leave the hypothesis more likely false than true. That's a large part of why so many findings with p < 0.05 fail to replicate. The ASA's 2016 statement on p-values makes exactly this point.

This mistake has practical consequences. It leads teams to overrate single results and ship on weak evidence. Your colleague's "97%" figure has no valid interpretation.
===END===
