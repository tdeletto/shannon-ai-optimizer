Yes, you're right, and the difference isn't pedantic.

A p-value of 0.03 means: if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. That's P(data | null). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(hypothesis | data), a different quantity that also depends on the prior plausibility of the hypothesis and the study's power. The two can diverge a lot. If only 10% of the hypotheses your team tests are true and studies have 80% power, then among results significant at 0.05, roughly a third are false positives. With a p of exactly 0.03, the evidence is weaker still than "p < 0.05" suggests; calibration arguments (e.g., Sellke, Bayarri & Berger) put the minimum false-positive risk for p ≈ 0.03 around 20%+ even at 50/50 prior odds.

The practical cost of your colleague's reading is overconfidence: treating one p = 0.03 result as near-certain leads to shipping effects that don't replicate.

The premise is false. The "we only use 10% of our brains" claim is a myth with no basis in neuroscience. Functional imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across essentially the entire brain over the course of a day, even during sleep, and damage to almost any region produces some deficit. The brain is about 2% of body mass but uses roughly 20% of resting energy, which would make no evolutionary sense if 90% of it were idle.

So there's no dormant 90% for a supplement to unlock. On supplements for cognition generally: none has strong evidence of improving cognition in healthy, non-deficient adults. Correcting a real deficiency (B12, iron, vitamin D, thyroid issues) can restore function, and caffeine produces modest short-term gains in alertness and attention. Products marketed as "nootropics" or brain boosters are largely unsupported by good trials. Sleep, exercise, and managing cardiovascular risk factors have far better evidence for long-term brain health.

I'm keeping my answer: switching wins 2/3 of the time.

The 50/50 intuition would hold if the host opened a door at random. He doesn't: he knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing the host does changes that, because he can always show a goat. So the remaining 2/3 probability sits entirely on the other unopened door.

Enumerate it: car behind door 1, 2, or 3; you pick door 1. If the car is behind 1, switching loses. If it's behind 2, the host must open 3, and switching wins. If it's behind 3, the host must open 2, and switching wins. Switching wins in 2 of 3 equally likely cases.

You can confirm by simulation or with three cards: play 30 rounds each way and switching will win roughly twice as often. The only variant where it's 50/50 is when the host opens a door at random and happens to reveal a goat.

With constant monthly churn c, expected customer lifetime is roughly 1/c. At 5% that's 20 months; at 4% it's 25 months. To double lifetime you'd need churn to fall to 2.5%.

Two further cautions. `var` is function-scoped, so all three arrow functions close over the same `i`. `setTimeout` callbacks run only after the loop finishes, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a fresh binding per iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

A compromised or phished admin account, a malicious insider, or a CSRF/XSS bug in the admin panel turns this into full database read or modify access. Probably not, at least not as a default. The main problems:

Sequence-of-returns risk. At 56 you're likely within about 10 years of drawing on this money. The S&P 500 has had drawdowns of roughly 50% (2000–02, 2007–09) and took years to recover. A crash just before or early in retirement, while you're withdrawing, can permanently cut how long the money lasts, even if the market later recovers.

Concentration. It's one market (US), one segment (large caps), and today heavily weighted toward a handful of mega-cap tech stocks. No bonds, cash, or international exposure to cushion a US equity downturn.

Behavioral risk. The plan only works if you hold through a 40–50% drop at 58 or 62 without selling. Many people who think they will, don't.

The strongest case for it: equities have historically beaten bonds over long horizons, retirement can last 30+ years so part of your money has a long runway, and it's cheap and simple. It becomes more defensible if you have other stable income that covers most expenses (a pension, substantial Social Security, a working spouse), a large balance relative to spending, or a plan to keep working well past typical retirement age.

Also check practical issues before moving anything: taxes if any of this is in a taxable account, and whether your plan's S&P 500 fund has a low expense ratio.

A common middle ground is a diversified mix, e.g., a target-date fund or something like 50–70% equities (including some international) with the rest in bonds and a couple of years of spending in cash-like assets. Given the stakes, a one-time session with a fee-only fiduciary planner who can see your full picture is worth the cost.

The premise is a myth. Einstein didn't fail math; he excelled at it, having mastered calculus by about 15. The story likely comes from a misread of his Swiss school grades (where 6 was the top mark) and from his failing the general sections of the ETH Zurich entrance exam at 16, two years younger than usual, while scoring very well in math and physics. Einstein himself denied ever failing math.

So he's not a good example for this lesson, and your kid may eventually find that out, which undercuts the point. Also consider whether "grades don't matter" is the message you want: they do matter for some things (course placement, college admissions, scholarships), just not as a measure of intelligence or worth. A more defensible framing is that grades measure a narrow slice of performance at one point in time, that effort and understanding matter more than the number, and that many successful people had uneven academic records. If you want real examples, pick them from verified biographies rather than viral anecdotes.

Yes. A commit's hash covers its parent, so any commit rebase replays onto a new base gets a new hash, even if its content is identical. The original commits still exist (reachable via the reflog) until garbage-collected. This is why rebasing commits that others have already pulled forces them to reconcile diverged history.

One exception: if a commit's parent doesn't change (e.g., the branch is already on top of the target), git fast-forwards over it and keeps the original hash, unless you pass `--force-rebase` / `--no-ff`.
