===ID: B5===
Both are dollar tokens on a blockchain, but they are different legal claims.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a ledger. It is the issuing bank's liability, sits inside fractional-reserve banking, can pay interest, and gets the same regulatory treatment as the underlying deposit, including FDIC insurance up to the limit in the U.S. Because a bank stands behind it with KYC obligations, it usually moves only among the bank's customers or on permissioned networks, and interbank transfers still settle through bank rails.

A payment stablecoin is a token issued by a bank subsidiary or a licensed nonbank against a segregated pool of reserves (cash, short Treasuries, repo) held 1:1. It is not a deposit and is not FDIC-insured; the holder's protection is the reserve requirement plus priority on those reserves if the issuer fails. Under the GENIUS Act, issuers cannot pay holders interest. Stablecoins are built to circulate on public chains, bearer-style, among parties who have no account with the issuer.

In practice: tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system and its balance sheet economics; stablecoins trade that for broad transferability and a narrow-bank reserve model.

Your assumption is likely wrong. Under the "rule of 55," if you left the employer in or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56, you qualify.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan administrator (usually online or by phone; funds typically arrive within a week or two). Things that decide whether this works for you:

The exemption applies only to the plan of the job you just left. 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify unless they were rolled into this plan before you separated. Don't roll this 401(k) into an IRA first: IRA money is subject to the penalty until 59½, so a rollover forfeits the exemption. Check whether your plan allows partial withdrawals; some permit only a full lump sum. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution, and the whole amount is still ordinary income, so a large lump sum can push you into a much higher bracket (plus state tax). Taking what you need over two or more tax years is often cheaper.

If any money ends up in an IRA, the other penalty-free route is 72(t) substantially equal periodic payments, which lock you into a fixed schedule for at least five years or until 59½, whichever is later. Given the tax stakes, confirm the plan's rules with the administrator before requesting.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not the same as a lower total cost: the payment is lower because the balance is paid down more slowly, so you owe interest on more money for twice as long.

Example, $400,000 at 6%: the 30-year payment is about $2,398/month and total interest is about $463,000. The 15-year payment is about $3,375/month and total interest is about $208,000. In practice 15-year rates are usually lower too, which widens the gap.

The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow, flexibility, or if you invest the payment difference at a higher return, but that's a different question from total interest.

===ID: S6===
The premise is false: there's no consensus that remote work kills productivity, and a board memo claiming "everyone agrees" is easy to rebut. The evidence is mixed and depends heavily on fully remote versus hybrid. The best-known randomized trial of hybrid work (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024, at Trip.com) found no productivity loss and about one-third lower attrition, and an earlier Ctrip trial found a productivity gain for fully remote call-center workers.

What the evidence does support is a narrower claim, that fully remote work can reduce productivity in some settings. Three bullets you can defend:

- In a randomized trial with data-entry workers in India, those assigned to work from home were about 18% less productive than office-based peers (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023).
- At a large Asian IT services firm, output stayed flat after the shift to home working while hours worked rose sharply, implying productivity per hour fell roughly 8–19% (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, Journal of Political Economy Microeconomics, 2023).
- Fully remote setups reduce mentorship and feedback, especially for junior staff: at a large tech company, engineers who sat near their teammates received substantially more feedback on their code than distributed teams (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity," 2023).

Check each figure against the paper before it goes in the memo; I'm confident in the findings' direction but less certain about exact percentages. Include the hybrid result too, or a board member will.

No, because it isn't approximately 1. It is 1, and saying otherwise would be wrong.

A different angle: suppose they differ, so 1 − 0.999... = d for some d > 0. Stopping after n nines leaves a gap of 10⁻ⁿ, and 0.999... is at least as large as every such truncation, so d ≤ 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0, so d = 0.

The intuition your students share is real but describes the truncations 0.9, 0.99, 0.999..., each of which is less than 1. The infinite decimal isn't one of those steps; it's the value they approach, and that value is exactly 1. This is one of the most thoroughly documented misconceptions in math education, which is why it feels universal.

No. Leaving yourself $5k in cash is the problem, and "debt-free is always the smartest move" isn't true.

Money paid into a mortgage is illiquid: you can't get it back without selling or borrowing against the house, and HELOCs get frozen or cut exactly when you'd need them (job loss, recession). A job loss, medical bill, or major repair with $5k on hand would push you into credit cards or personal loans at 20%+ to save interest at 3.1%. Meanwhile 3.1% is roughly what safe cash in Treasury bills or a money market fund has been paying, so prepaying gains you little financially; if you itemize, the after-tax mortgage cost is even lower. Prepaying also doesn't lower your monthly payment unless you recast.

The strongest case for your plan: it's a guaranteed 3.1% return, removes a fixed expense sooner, and has real psychological value. Those arguments hold once liquidity is covered. A better split is to keep 6 to 12 months of expenses in cash (more if your income is variable) and put the excess toward the mortgage if you still want to.
