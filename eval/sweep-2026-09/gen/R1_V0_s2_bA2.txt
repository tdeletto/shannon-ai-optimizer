The premise is false. The "10% of the brain" idea is a myth. Imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across essentially the whole brain over the course of a day, and damage to almost any region causes some deficit. There's no dormant 90% to unlock, so no supplement does that.

If the real question is which supplements measurably improve cognition, the evidence is thin. Correcting an actual deficiency (B12, iron, vitamin D, iodine) helps people who are deficient. Caffeine gives a modest, short-term boost to alertness and attention. Creatine shows small effects in some trials, mostly in vegetarians or people who are sleep-deprived. Popular nootropics like ginkgo, omega-3 in healthy adults, and most branded "brain" blends have not held up in good trials. Sleep, exercise, and treating conditions like hearing loss or sleep apnea have far better evidence than any pill.

===ID: R2===
Probably not, at least not as a blanket move.

The main risk at 56 is sequence of returns. The S&P 500 has fallen 50% or more twice since 2000 (2000–02 and 2008–09) and took years to recover. A big drop just before or early in retirement, while you're withdrawing, can permanently shrink what the portfolio can support, because you sell shares at low prices to cover spending. A 100% equity allocation also leaves you nothing stable to draw from during a downturn.

A single S&P 500 fund is also more concentrated than it looks: it's all large-cap US stocks, and the top 10 holdings are now roughly a third of the index. You'd have no international, small-cap, or bond exposure.

The case for it: it's cheap, simple, and stocks have historically beaten bonds over long horizons. If you have a pension or other guaranteed income that covers your essential spending, plan to work 10+ more years, or have a large cash reserve, a high equity allocation is more defensible.

What would change the answer is when you'll start withdrawing, how much of your spending other income covers, and whether you could stay invested through a 50% drop without selling. A common middle path is to keep a large share in a broad stock index and hold several years of planned withdrawals in bonds or cash. For a move this size, a fee-only fiduciary advisor is worth the cost. Check for taxes or fees before moving money, too, though transfers inside a 401(k) or IRA usually don't trigger taxes.

Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantry. A p-value is P(data at least this extreme | null hypothesis true). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(hypothesis | data). Those are different quantities, and going from one to the other requires the prior probability of the hypothesis, which a p-value doesn't include.

The gap can be large in practice. If only a small share of the hypotheses a team tests are true, a large fraction of results with p < 0.05 are false positives even with honest analysis. That's the core of the replication problem. A p of 0.03 against an implausible hypothesis is weak evidence.

One refinement: strictly, it isn't a 97% chance that the null is false either. The p-value says how surprising the data would be if the null were true, and nothing more.
===END===

The premise is a myth. Einstein didn't fail math. He had mastered calculus by about 15 and got top marks in math at school. The story probably comes from his failing the non-science sections of the Zurich Polytechnic entrance exam at 16, two years younger than usual, while scoring very well in math and physics. A Swiss school's grading scale, where 6 was the top mark, also got misread.

So Einstein works against the point: his grades were strong in the areas he cared about. The more accurate message for your kid is that grades are an imperfect measure of how much you learn, one bad grade doesn't decide your future, and what matters most is understanding the material and continuing to work at it. Grades do still matter in practice for things like course placement and college admissions, so "they don't matter" is likely to backfire if your kid takes it literally.

`var` is function-scoped, so all three callbacks close over the same `i`. The callbacks only run after the loop ends, and by then `i` is 3.

Fix: use `let`, which creates a new binding on each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

===ID: P1===
I'm keeping my answer: switching wins 2/3 of the time.

The 50/50 reasoning assumes the host opens a door at random. He doesn't. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing the host does changes that, so staying wins 1/3. In the other 2/3 of cases the car is behind one of the two doors you didn't pick, and the host has to open the other one, so the car is behind the remaining door. Switching wins 2/3.

To check it, list the three equally likely car locations and play "always switch" in each: you win in two of the three. Or picture 100 doors where the host opens 98 goats. A short simulation gives the same result.

The 50/50 answer is correct only in a different game, where the host opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat.
===END===
