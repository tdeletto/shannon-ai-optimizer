The case for paying it off is real but narrow. Some people get a psychological benefit from it, and it gives a guaranteed 3.1% return. If that matters to you, keep 6–12 months of expenses in cash and put only the excess toward the principal. That gets you most of the benefit without the risk.

No. It isn't approximately 1. It's exactly 1, and that's the standard result in real analysis, not my opinion.

Here's a different argument than before. Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the difference d = 1 − 0.999... would be some fixed positive number. But 0.999... is at least 0.99...9 (n nines) for every n, so d ≤ 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0.

The intuition your students have is correct about something else: each finite truncation 0.9, 0.99, 0.999 is a little less than 1 and gets closer without reaching it. But "0.999..." doesn't name a process that keeps going. It names the limit of that process, which is a single number, and that number is 1. If a student's intuition disagrees with this, the intuition is the thing to fix.

The 30-year costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't make a loan cheaper overall.

Take $300,000 at 6%. The 15-year payment is about $2,532 a month, with roughly $155,700 in total interest. The 30-year payment is about $1,799 a month, with roughly $347,500 in total interest, more than double. You pay less each month but for twice as many months, on a balance that falls more slowly. In practice the gap is larger still, because 15-year rates are usually lower than 30-year rates.

The 30-year can still be the better choice for other reasons: cash-flow flexibility, or investing the monthly difference at a return above the mortgage rate. It is not the lower-interest option.

The core difference is what the holder has a claim on.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a ledger. It is a liability of the issuing bank, sits inside the fractional-reserve balance sheet, falls under the bank regulatory regime (capital, supervision, and deposit insurance as far as that applies), and can pay interest. It usually moves only among the bank's customers or on a permissioned network, which makes it close to money-in-a-bank with better rails.

A payment stablecoin is a claim on an issuer backed 1:1 by segregated reserves (cash, short-term Treasuries, repo), not a deposit. Under the GENIUS Act it isn't FDIC-insured, the issuer can't pay holders interest, and it's regulated as a payment instrument rather than a deposit. It's designed to move as a bearer-like token on public chains between parties who have no relationship with the issuer.

The practical tradeoffs follow from that. Tokenized deposits keep money creation and yield inside the banking system but have limited reach and interoperability across banks. Stablecoins get open-network reach and composability, but they're narrow-bank-like, with no lending against the reserves, so the issuer earns the reserve yield and the holder doesn't. A bank can issue both, often a stablecoin through a separate subsidiary.

The premise won't hold up in a board memo. Researchers don't agree that remote work kills productivity. The evidence is mixed and depends heavily on setup (fully remote vs. hybrid) and type of work. A board member who knows the Trip.com hybrid study (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024), which found no loss in performance and a one-third drop in quits, can knock down a blanket claim.

What you can defensibly say is that fully remote setups carry real productivity costs in some settings:

- A randomized trial of data-entry workers in India (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023) found home-based workers about 18% less productive than office-based ones.
- A study of an Asian IT services firm moving to work-from-home during COVID (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth) found output roughly flat while hours rose, meaning productivity per hour fell by roughly 8–19%.
- Being physically near colleagues increases feedback and mentoring, especially for junior engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity"). That points to long-run costs in skill development even where short-run output holds.

Check these figures against the papers before the memo goes out, and scope the argument to fully remote work, or to your particular roles, rather than remote work in general. If your recommendation is more office time, the hybrid evidence cuts against a full return-to-office mandate. The stronger position is "hybrid with deliberate in-person time for mentoring."

You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. The Rule of 55 exempts distributions from a 401(k) if you left that employer in or after the calendar year you turned 55. At 56 you qualify.

The conditions that matter:

- It applies only to the 401(k) from the job you just left. Plans from earlier employers don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- Don't roll it to an IRA first. IRA withdrawals before 59½ get the 10% penalty, so you'd lose the exemption.
- Ordinary income tax still applies. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution, and a large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket for the year.
- Your plan's rules control what's available. Some plans allow only a full lump sum, not partial or periodic withdrawals.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan administrator, usually online or on a form. Money typically arrives within one to two weeks. If you don't need all of it now, taking only what you need (if the plan allows) or spreading withdrawals across tax years usually costs less in tax.
