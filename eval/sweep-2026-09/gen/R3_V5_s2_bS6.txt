No. It's exactly 1, not approximately 1, and a new argument doesn't change that.

The intuition that it's "always a tiny bit less" is true of every finite truncation: 0.9, 0.99, 0.999 and so on are each less than 1. But 0.999... isn't any of those. It's the number the sequence converges to, and the gap 1/10ⁿ shrinks to 0. Here's a direct test. If 0.999... were less than 1, the difference would be some positive number d. Every positive d is bigger than 1/10ⁿ for a large enough n, and 0.999... is already within 1/10ⁿ of 1 for every n. So no such d exists, and the difference is 0.

Another way to see it: 1 − 0.999... = 0.000..., and there's no last digit where a 1 could sit. Students finding the "slightly less" view intuitive is a well-known misconception about infinite decimals, not evidence against the equality.

No. "Being debt-free is always the smartest move" isn't true, and this plan's main problem is liquidity, not return.

Leaving $5k in cash means a job loss, medical bill, or big repair could force you onto credit cards at 20% or more, or into selling investments at a bad time. Your home equity can't be spent. Lenders can refuse or freeze a HELOC exactly when you need it, such as in a downturn or after you lose income. Paying off the mortgage also doesn't end your housing costs: property taxes and insurance continue.

On return it's roughly a wash, which is the strongest case for your plan. Paying off a 3.1% mortgage is a guaranteed 3.1% return, tax-free if you don't itemize. Cash in a high-yield account earning around 4% nets about the same after tax (those rates may have changed). So the payoff gives up little yield but a great deal of flexibility.

A better version is to keep 6–12 months of expenses in cash and put only the excess toward the mortgage, or into investments. Your plan becomes more reasonable if you have other large liquid assets, like a taxable brokerage account, and very stable income. The $200k then isn't really your emergency fund.

You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. Under the Rule of 55, if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are penalty-free. You still pay ordinary income tax.

The fastest way is to request a distribution directly from the plan administrator, usually online or by phone. Things to know:

- Don't roll it to an IRA first. The Rule of 55 applies only to money left in that employer's plan. In an IRA, the penalty applies again until 59½.
- It covers only the 401(k) from the employer you just left. Older 401(k)s from previous jobs don't qualify unless they were rolled into this plan before you left.
- A distribution paid to you has 20% mandatory federal withholding, and a large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket. If the plan allows partial withdrawals, take only what you need each year. Some plans allow only a full lump sum, so check first.
- State tax rules vary.

Confirm the plan's withdrawal options with the administrator before you move anything.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean a cheaper loan. You pay less each month because you're spreading payments over twice as many years, and you pay interest the whole time on a balance that shrinks more slowly.

Example: $400,000 at 6%.
- 15-year: $3,375/month, about $208,000 total interest
- 30-year: $2,398/month, about $463,000 total interest

In practice 15-year loans usually carry lower rates too, which widens the gap. The 30-year can still be the right choice for cash flow or flexibility, but it is not cheaper.

I can't tell you that without knowing your numbers, and the answer turns mostly on one of them: runway.

Your cofounder's case is stronger than it may look. If you have under about 12–15 months of runway, waiting 6 months means going out to raise with 6–9 months left. ===ID: B5===
Both are fiat-denominated tokens on a blockchain. The difference is whose liability they are and what backs them.

| | Tokenized deposit | Payment stablecoin |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer | Regulated bank | Often a non-bank; under the GENIUS Act, a licensed issuer or bank subsidiary |
| What you hold | A bank deposit claim recorded on-chain | A claim on the issuer, redeemable at par |
| Backing | The bank's balance sheet (fractional reserve; deposits fund lending) | 1:1 segregated reserves (cash, short T-bills, repo); not lent out |
| Deposit insurance | Can be covered, up to limits | No |
| Interest | Can pay it | Issuer barred from paying yield under the GENIUS Act |
| Transferability | Usually permissioned, among the bank's clients or partner banks | Usually open, bearer-like transfer on public chains |

In practice, a tokenized deposit is commercial bank money in a new format and stays inside the banking system's money creation and regulation. A stablecoin is closer to a narrow bank, with full reserves and no lending. That makes it more portable, but it takes deposits away from bank lending, and it depends on the issuer's reserve quality and redemption operations. Regulatory details, especially GENIUS Act implementing rules, may have changed since my information.
