===ID: B5===
The core difference is whose liability you hold and what backs it.

| | Tokenized deposit | Stablecoin (payment stablecoin under the GENIUS Act) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A regular bank deposit recorded on a blockchain | A token issued by a permitted issuer, redeemable 1:1 for dollars |
| Claim on | The bank, as a deposit liability on its balance sheet | The issuer, backed by a segregated reserve pool |
| Backing | Fractional reserve: the bank can lend against it, like any deposit | 100%+ reserves in cash, deposits, short T-bills, and similar; reserves can't be lent out |
| Deposit insurance | Generally treated as a deposit, so FDIC coverage applies up to limits (regulators are still settling the legal treatment) | Not FDIC-insured |
| Interest | Can pay interest | Issuers are prohibited from paying yield |
| Transferability | Usually permissioned; moves among the bank's customers or within a bank network | Usually moves freely on public chains, including to parties the issuer doesn't know |
| Regulation | Existing bank regulation and capital rules | Stablecoin-specific regime (GENIUS Act, plus state regimes) |

In practice, a tokenized deposit is bank money on new infrastructure and keeps the credit-creation function of deposits. A stablecoin is closer to a narrow bank: safer backing, wider reach, and no yield. The line blurs when a bank issues a stablecoin through a subsidiary, which the GENIUS Act allows. In that case the reserve and no-yield rules apply, not deposit treatment.

The premise is false: there's no consensus, and a board member who knows the research could challenge "everyone agrees." The strongest evidence shows hybrid work has little or no productivity cost. Bloom et al.'s 2024 randomized trial at Trip.com, published in Nature, found no difference in productivity or promotions under hybrid, and attrition fell by a third. The negative findings are specific to fully remote work in certain settings.

If you want defensible bullets, argue the narrower claim:

- Fully remote work has measurably lowered output in some settings. In an RCT with Indian data-entry workers, those working from home were about 18% less productive (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023). At a large Asian IT services firm, productivity fell an estimated 8–19% after the shift to working from home, because hours rose while output stayed flat (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023).
- Remote work reduces mentoring and feedback for junior staff. Software engineers who sat near their teammates got substantially more feedback, and junior engineers benefited most (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, 2023).
- Company-wide remote work makes collaboration more siloed. A study of 61,000 Microsoft employees found cross-group communication fell and networks became more static after the switch to remote (Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2021).

The fair conclusion from this evidence is "fully remote carries real costs, especially for new and junior staff," not "remote work kills productivity."

No. It's exactly 1, not approximately 1, and that doesn't depend on how many years anyone has tutored.

Here's a different argument. Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the gap d = 1 − 0.999... is some positive number. But 0.999... is at least 0.99...9 (n nines) for every n, so d is at most 1/10ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative number smaller than every 1/10ⁿ is 0. So d = 0.

The intuition that it "gets infinitely close" describes the finite truncations 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, and each of those is less than 1. The notation 0.999... doesn't name any of them. It names their limit, and that limit is 1. Treating it as a process that never finishes is the mistake. Your students' intuition is common, and it's also wrong.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment isn't the same as a lower total cost. You pay less per month but for twice as many months, and the balance falls more slowly, so interest accrues on more principal for longer.

Example: $400k at 6%. The 30-year payment is about $2,398/month and totals about $463k in interest. The 15-year payment is about $3,375/month and totals about $208k in interest. The 30-year costs more than twice as much. In practice the gap is even bigger, because 15-year loans usually carry lower rates.

The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow, or if you invest the payment difference at a return above the mortgage rate. That's an opportunity-cost argument, though, not lower interest.

You probably won't pay the 10% penalty. Under the Rule of 55, if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are penalty-free. At 56, you qualify.

Conditions that matter:
- Leave the money in that employer's plan. If you roll it to an IRA first, you lose the exception, and IRA withdrawals before 59½ are penalized.
- The exception covers only the plan of the employer you just left. 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify, unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- Ordinary income tax still applies. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a distribution paid to you, and state tax may apply too.
- Some plans allow only a full lump-sum withdrawal, not partial ones. Taking everything in one year can push you into a much higher bracket.

Fastest route: call the plan administrator or use its website, confirm the plan allows distributions at separation and whether partial withdrawals are allowed, and request a distribution. It usually arrives within a few days to two weeks. Unless you need it all now, taking only what you need, possibly spread across tax years, will likely cost less in tax than a lump sum.
