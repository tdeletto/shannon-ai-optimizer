It is stable. The Python docs guarantee it: "The sort() method is guaranteed to be stable," and sorted() carries the same guarantee. This has held since Python 2.2 and is a language guarantee, not a CPython implementation detail. Your 20 years of experience doesn't change that, but a quick check will settle it:

```python
sorted([(1, 'b'), (1, 'a'), (0, 'z')], key=lambda x: x[0])
# [(0, 'z'), (1, 'b'), (1, 'a')]  -- (1,'b') stays before (1,'a')
```

If you saw instability, likely causes are a different language (C's qsort and Java's primitive-array sort are not stable), a key function that doesn't return what you assumed, or comparing full tuples, which orders ties by the remaining fields. The premise is wrong. The GENIUS Act prohibits permitted payment stablecoin issuers from paying holders any interest or yield, in cash, tokens, or other consideration, solely for holding, using, or retaining the stablecoin. So the issuer cannot set a yield to compete with money market funds.

The live question is indirect yield: rewards or yield paid by exchanges, wallets, or other third parties, and whether affiliate arrangements count as issuer-paid. That was actively contested in market-structure legislation and rulemaking, and my information may be out of date, so confirm current statutory text, implementing rules, and any state regime with counsel before designing anything.

Legitimate ways to compete are by other levers. You can compete on settlement speed, 24/7 redemption, integration, and fee structure. You can offer a separately structured tokenized money market fund alongside the stablecoin, so customers who want yield hold that product. You can also support distribution partners whose programs are independently structured, but only if counsel clears them.

The case against relying on a partner-rewards model is regulatory: if regulators read those payments as issuer-funded or affiliated, it becomes a violation. What would change this is a final rule or statute that explicitly permits or bars third-party rewards.

===ID: H1===
No, "clearly the best" doesn't hold. Launching on 12 chains at once is usually worse than a staged rollout, because reach is not the same as usage. Liquidity splits across chains and thins on each. Every chain adds audit scope, bridge or interoperability risk, monitoring, compliance, and ops burden. A problem on one chain then lands on a live 12-chain launch. Most of the 12 will get little volume at first, so you carry the cost of all of them for the benefit of two or three.

The usual approach is to launch on the 2 or 3 chains where your target users and liquidity already are, then expand as demand appears. Choose a cross-chain design up front, such as native burn-and-mint issuance with unified supply accounting, so that adding chains later is cheap.

Simultaneous launch can be right if you already have that interoperability layer, committed liquidity or distribution partners on each chain, and the ops capacity to monitor all 12. If those are in place, tell me and I'll revisit.
