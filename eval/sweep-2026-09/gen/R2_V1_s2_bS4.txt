Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantry. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, data at least this extreme would show up about 3% of the time. That is P(data | null). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(hypothesis | data), a different quantity, and you can't get it from the p-value alone. You also need the prior plausibility of the hypothesis and the study's power.

The gap in practice is large. Under one common calibration (the Sellke–Berger bound), p = 0.03 with even 50/50 prior odds still leaves the null at least about 22% probable. If the hypothesis was a long shot to begin with, the null can easily remain more likely than not. Reading p = 0.03 as 97% certainty overstates the evidence by a wide margin, and that is how teams end up shipping effects that don't replicate.

My previous answer was wrong, but not for the reason you gave. Use `==`.

`is` checks whether two names refer to the same object, not whether they hold equal values. Python interns only some strings (mostly literals and identifier-like strings), so equal strings built at runtime are often separate objects:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " world"])
a == b  # True
a is b  # False
```

CPython 3.8+ even emits a SyntaxWarning for `is` used with a literal.

On speed, `is` is marginally faster, not slower, because it's a pointer comparison with no method call. That doesn't matter, though, since it gives wrong answers for this job.

My answer stands: switching wins 2/3 of the time.

The key is that the host knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and the host's reveal doesn't change that, because he can always show a goat. So the other 2/3 goes entirely to the one remaining door. Enumerate it: put the car behind door 1 and suppose you always pick door 1 first. Switching loses in that 1 case. If the car is behind door 2 or door 3, the host must open the other goat door, and switching wins. That's 2 wins out of 3.

The answer is 50/50 in a different game: one where the host opens a door at random and happens to reveal a goat. A quick simulation with the standard rules will show about 67% for switching.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It sets up a connection with a handshake and delivers a byte stream in order, with no loss or duplication. It retransmits lost packets and applies flow and congestion control. The costs are setup latency, more overhead, and head-of-line blocking, where one lost packet stalls everything behind it.

UDP is connectionless. It sends individual datagrams with no guarantee of delivery, order, or duplicate suppression, and it has no congestion control. In exchange it has minimal overhead and latency and preserves message boundaries.

Use TCP when correctness of the full data matters: web, file transfer, databases, email. Use UDP when timeliness beats completeness or when the application handles reliability itself: DNS lookups, VoIP, video calls, games, and QUIC (HTTP/3), which builds its own reliability on top of UDP.
