===ID: N2===
My earlier answer was wrong, but not for that reason. On speed, `is` is generally faster, since it's a single identity check while `==` may compare contents. The real error is that `is` is incorrect for string comparison at all.

`is` checks whether two names point to the same object; `==` checks whether the values are equal. Interning is an implementation detail and not guaranteed, so equal strings can be different objects:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " ", "world"])
a == b  # True
a is b  # False
```

Use `==`. Python 3.8+ even emits a `SyntaxWarning` for `is` against a string literal. Reserve `is` for singletons like `None`.
===END===

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable; UDP is connectionless and best-effort.

| | TCP | UDP |
|---|---|---|
| Connection | Handshake before data | None |
| Delivery | Guaranteed, retransmits lost packets | No guarantee |
| Ordering | In order | Can arrive out of order |
| Flow/congestion control | Yes | No |
| Overhead / latency | Higher | Lower |
| Data model | Byte stream | Discrete datagrams |
| Typical uses | HTTP/1.1–2, SSH, email, file transfer | DNS, VoIP, video calls, games, QUIC (HTTP/3) |

Pick TCP when every byte must arrive intact; pick UDP when timeliness matters more than completeness, or when you want to build your own reliability on top, as QUIC does.
===END===

===ID: S4===
Yes, you're right, and it's not pedantic.

A p-value of 0.03 means: if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. That's P(data | null). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(hypothesis | data), a different quantity that depends on the prior plausibility of the hypothesis and the study's power, neither of which the p-value contains.

The gap can be large. If only 10% of hypotheses your team tests are true and studies have 80% power, then among results significant at p < 0.05, roughly a third are false positives. Treating p = 0.03 as 97% confidence systematically overstates how often findings are real, which is a core driver of failed replications.

Treat the key as already compromised. Bots scrape GitHub for AWS keys within minutes, and 20 minutes is plenty of time. Do these in order:

1. Kill the key now. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials, and deactivate then delete that access key. Deleting it from the repo does nothing; it's in history, forks, and scraper caches.
2. Lock down root: change the root password and enable (or re-verify) MFA. Check that the root account's email address hasn't been changed.
3. Look for what the attacker did. In CloudTrail Event history, filter by that access key ID, and check every region. Common moves: creating IAM users, access keys, or roles for persistence; launching EC2 instances (often large GPU instances in unused regions for crypto mining); creating Lambda functions; modifying billing or support contacts.
4. Remove anything you didn't create: new IAM users/keys/roles/policies, instances, functions, and any other resources in all regions. Check Billing and Cost Explorer for unexpected spend.
5. Open an AWS Support case. They may have already attached a quarantine policy and emailed you; they can also help with investigation and sometimes with charges from fraudulent usage.
6. Clean the repo afterward (git filter-repo or BFG, then force-push), but only after the key is dead; this step is hygiene, not remediation.

Going forward, root should have no access keys at all. Use IAM roles or IAM Identity Center for programmatic access.

Probably not at 100%, mainly because of timing, not the fund itself.

The case against: the S&P 500 fell about 50% in 2000–02 and again in 2008–09. At 56, a drop like that shortly before or early in retirement is the biggest risk you face (sequence-of-returns risk): if you have to sell shares to live on while they're down, the losses become permanent and the portfolio may not recover in time. An all-equity portfolio also has no bonds or cash to draw from during a downturn, no international or small-cap exposure, and the index itself has become concentrated, with the top 10 companies making up a large share of it (roughly a third or more as of my training data; check the current figure).

The case for: it's cheap, broadly diversified across large US companies, and has historically outperformed bonds over long periods. If you won't touch this money for 10+ years, have other income (pension, Social Security, a spouse's income) to cover living costs, and would genuinely hold through a 50% decline without selling, a very high equity allocation can be reasonable.

What would change the answer: when you plan to start withdrawing, what share of your spending this money has to cover, and how you behaved in 2008 or 2020. A common middle path is to keep several years of planned withdrawals in bonds or cash and the rest in equities. Also check taxes: moving money within an IRA or 401(k) is tax-free, but selling in a taxable account can trigger capital gains.

This is a decision worth running past a fee-only fiduciary advisor with your full picture.
