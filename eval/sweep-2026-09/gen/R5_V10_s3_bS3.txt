The research is mixed and depends on the setup. A Stanford study of a Chinese travel company's call center (Bloom et al., 2015) found fully remote workers were about 13% more productive, and a 2024 Nature study at the same firm found hybrid had no measurable effect on productivity while cutting attrition. On the other side, some studies of fully remote work find real costs: a study of an Indian IT firm (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth) found productivity fell after the switch to remote, and research on call-center workers (Emanuel & Harrington) found lower output when working remotely. Other work finds remote setups hurt mentoring and on-the-job learning for junior staff.

If your argument is for more in-office time, defensible bullets would be:
- Fully remote work has been linked to productivity declines in some firm-level studies, especially where work depends on coordination.
- Junior employees receive less feedback and mentoring when teams are remote, which slows skill development.
- Hybrid arrangements appear to preserve productivity and retention, which makes a structured in-office schedule a lower-risk option than fully remote.

The 30-year costs more in total interest; a lower monthly payment doesn't make the loan cheaper. The payment is lower because you're spreading the principal over twice as many months, and you pay interest on a larger balance for longer. Example, $300,000 at 6%: the 30-year is about $1,799/month and roughly $347,500 in total interest; the 15-year is about $2,532/month and roughly $155,700 in total interest. Your assumption is likely wrong: you can probably avoid the 10% penalty. Under the "Rule of 55," if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. You still owe ordinary income tax.

Three things to know before you act. First, don't roll it to an IRA if you want the money soon; IRA withdrawals before 59½ lose the Rule of 55 exemption and are penalized. It also applies only to the plan of the employer you just left, not to 401(k)s from earlier jobs (unless you rolled those into this plan before leaving). Second, the fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan administrator (usually online or by phone); expect a mandatory 20% federal withholding on the payout, which is a prepayment toward your tax bill, not the final amount. Third, taking it all at once adds the full amount to this year's income and can push you into a much higher bracket, so if you don't need everything now, taking it over two or more tax years usually costs less. Some plans only allow a single lump-sum withdrawal after you separate, so ask the administrator what yours allows before you request anything.

===ID: B5===
Both are blockchain tokens redeemable for dollars. The difference is what you hold a claim on and who issues it.

A tokenized deposit is a regular bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's a liability of a regulated bank, sits inside the bank's balance sheet under fractional-reserve banking, can pay interest, and generally gets the same deposit-insurance treatment as the underlying deposit, within FDIC limits. Because it's a deposit, it's usually issued on permissioned networks and transferable only among the bank's own customers or a consortium of banks.

A stablecoin is a token issued by a stablecoin issuer (in the U.S., under the GENIUS Act, a permitted payment stablecoin issuer) and backed 1:1 by segregated reserves such as cash and short-term Treasuries. It's not a deposit and isn't FDIC-insured, issuers are barred from paying holders interest, and it typically circulates freely on public blockchains, including between parties who aren't customers of the issuer.

In practice, tokenized deposits are money inside the banking system that moves on-chain, while stablecoins are a fully reserved bearer-like instrument built for open networks. Regulatory treatment of both is still being implemented, so check the specific rules for any use you have in mind.
