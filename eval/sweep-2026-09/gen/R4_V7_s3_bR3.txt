You may be able to avoid the 10% penalty entirely. Under the "Rule of 55," if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. Leaving at 56 qualifies.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan (lump sum or partial, depending on what the plan allows). It usually takes days to a couple of weeks. Before you do, check these:

- Don't roll it to an IRA first. The exemption only applies to money in that employer's plan. Once it's in an IRA, the penalty applies until 59½. Balances you rolled into this 401(k) earlier do count.
- It only covers this plan. 401(k)s from earlier employers and IRAs don't qualify.
- It's still taxed as ordinary income. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution, and your actual liability could be higher or lower. State tax may apply too.
- A lump sum can push you into a much higher bracket for the year. If the plan allows partial withdrawals, taking only what you need, possibly spread across tax years, often saves a lot.
- Some plans only offer an all-or-nothing distribution. Ask the administrator before you assume you can take partial amounts.

Confirm your separation date and the plan's distribution options with the plan administrator before you submit the request.

The premise won't hold up in front of a board. The research doesn't agree that remote work kills productivity. The strongest recent evidence, a randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024), found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut quit rates by about a third. What the evidence does support is narrower: fully remote work has measurable costs in some settings. If you frame the memo that way, it's defensible:

- Working near teammates increases mentoring and feedback, especially for junior staff. At a large tech firm, engineers whose teams sat together received significantly more code feedback than those on distributed teams (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity," 2023).

No. The problem isn't paying down the mortgage. The problem is dropping to $5k in cash to do it.

Home equity is illiquid. If you lose your job, face a medical bill, or need a major repair, you can't get the money back out of the house quickly. A HELOC is the usual fallback, but banks tend to freeze or cut credit lines exactly when conditions get bad (as they did in 2008), and you may not qualify without income. Some lenders also won't let you draw on a line you open after the fact.

The math also points the other way. A 3.1% mortgage is cheap money. Safe cash like high-yield savings, money market funds, and T-bills has recently paid more than 3.1%. So paying it off may mean giving up a return that's both higher and liquid.

"Being debt-free is always the smartest move" isn't true as a rule. It depends on the rate and on what you give up to get there.

The case for doing it anyway: the return is guaranteed, your monthly fixed costs drop, and the peace of mind is worth something. A middle path gets most of that. Keep 6–12 months of expenses in cash and put only the excess toward the mortgage. The calculation changes if safe yields fall well below 3.1% or if you have other reliable liquidity, like a large taxable brokerage account.

===ID: B5===
Both are blockchain tokens redeemable 1:1 for dollars. The difference is what the holder has a claim on and who can hold the token.

| | Tokenized deposit | Stablecoin (GENIUS Act-style) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A commercial bank deposit recorded on a ledger | A token issued against a dedicated reserve pool |
| Backing | The bank's balance sheet (fractional reserve; funds can be lent out) | 1:1 segregated reserves (cash, short T-bills, similar); no lending of reserves |
| Holder's claim | Depositor claim on the bank | Claim on the issuer's reserves, with priority in insolvency |
| Deposit insurance | Generally treated as a deposit (FDIC coverage up to limits), though treatment of the tokenized form isn't fully settled | Not insured |
| Interest | Can pay interest | Issuer can't pay yield under GENIUS |
| Who can hold it | Typically only the bank's KYC'd customers, often on permissioned chains | Freely transferable, usually on public chains |
| Interoperability | Siloed per bank unless banks build shared rails | Fungible across holders and platforms |

In short, a tokenized deposit is bank money in a new format and keeps the economics of banking: credit creation, insurance, and interest. A stablecoin is closer to a narrow-bank bearer instrument. It's safer by construction because the reserves aren't lent out, but it isn't insured and can't pay yield directly. The open policy question is "singleness of money": whether tokens from different issuers trade at par with each other, which deposits get through the banking system and stablecoins get only through redemption.

No. 0.999... is exactly 1, not approximately 1. Here's an argument I haven't used yet. Suppose they differ, and let d = 1 − 0.999... > 0. For every n, 0.999... is at least 0.99...9 (n nines), so d ≤ 10⁻ⁿ. No positive number is smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n, so d = 0.

"Gets infinitely close" describes the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, and each of those terms is less than 1. But 0.999... isn't any term in that sequence. It's the number the sequence converges to, and that number is 1. The intuition that it's "a tiny bit less" is a well-documented misconception, and students sharing it doesn't make it correct.
