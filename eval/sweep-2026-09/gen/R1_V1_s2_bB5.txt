The difference is who issues it and what you hold a claim on.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. The issuer is a regulated bank, and the token is that bank's deposit liability, so it sits inside the normal banking framework: fractional-reserve lending against it, capital and supervision rules, and generally the same deposit-insurance treatment as the underlying deposit (though how insurance applies to tokenized forms is still being clarified). It can pay interest. It usually circulates on permissioned rails among the bank's own customers or a bank consortium, so it moves less freely than a stablecoin.

A stablecoin is a token issued by an entity, often a non-bank, that holds a separate pool of reserve assets (cash, short-term Treasuries, repo) meant to back it 1:1. The holder has a redemption claim on the issuer, not a bank deposit, and there is no deposit insurance. In the US, the GENIUS Act (July 2025) requires payment stablecoins to be fully reserved with high-quality liquid assets and bars issuers from paying interest to holders. Stablecoins are typically bearer-style and transferable across public chains to anyone, which is their main advantage for open, 24/7 settlement.

In short: a tokenized deposit is bank money on new rails, with lending, insurance, and yield but narrower reach; a stablecoin is fully reserved narrow money with broad interoperability but no insurance or yield. Details on insurance treatment and implementing rules are evolving, so check current regulator guidance before relying on specifics.

I can't write those bullets honestly, because the premise is wrong: there is no consensus that remote work kills productivity. The research is mixed and depends heavily on the type of work and whether it's fully remote or hybrid. A board memo asserting a consensus that doesn't exist is easy to rebut.

What the evidence does support, if your concern is fully remote work:

- Some controlled studies find fully remote work lowers output in certain roles, e.g., randomized or quasi-experimental studies of data-entry workers and IT staff in India that found meaningful productivity drops for home-based workers.
- Remote setups can weaken mentoring, informal feedback, and collaboration, with some evidence that junior employees receive less coaching when not co-located.
- Hybrid arrangements, by contrast, have been found in a large randomized trial (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024) to have no measurable productivity loss and lower attrition, so the stronger case is usually for structured in-office time, not against remote work entirely.

Other studies, such as Bloom's earlier Ctrip call-center experiment, found remote work raised productivity. Verify the specific studies and figures before they go in the memo; I'm citing from memory, not a checked source.

Home equity is illiquid. Once the $195k is in the house, the only ways to get it back are selling or borrowing against it, and lenders tighten or freeze HELOCs exactly when you'd need one, such as a job loss or recession. $5k covers very little: a roof, a medical bill, or a few months without income would force you into credit cards at 20%+ to protect an asset earning you 3.1%. The case for your plan is real but narrower: a guaranteed 3.1% after-tax return, lower monthly outflow, and peace of mind. It makes more sense if you have other substantial liquid assets outside this $200k, very stable income, and plans to stay in the home.

You likely won't pay the 10% penalty. Under the "rule of 55," if you leave an employer in or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56, you qualify.

The conditions that matter: it applies only to the 401(k) of the job you just left, not to IRAs or plans from earlier employers (unless you rolled them into this plan before leaving). If you roll this 401(k) into an IRA, you lose the exemption, so leave the money in the plan while you withdraw. Ordinary income tax still applies, and the plan must withhold 20% federal tax on cash distributions; your actual bill may be higher or lower.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan administrator. Check what the plan allows: some permit only a full lump sum, not partial withdrawals. Taking everything at once can push much of it into a higher tax bracket, so if you don't need it all now, spreading withdrawals across tax years usually costs less. Confirm with the plan administrator or a tax advisor, since rules and plan terms can change.
===END===

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean a cheaper loan; it means you repay principal more slowly, so you pay interest on a larger balance for longer.

At $300,000 and 6%, the 30-year payment is about $1,799 a month with roughly $347,500 in total interest; the 15-year payment is about $2,532 a month with roughly $155,700 in total interest. In practice 15-year rates are usually lower, which widens the gap.

The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow, or if you invest the payment difference at a return above the mortgage rate. That's a different question from total interest.
