===ID: B5===
The difference is whose liability it is and what backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's a liability of the issuing bank, sits on the bank's balance sheet, and is backed the way any deposit is: the bank's loans and securities, its capital, fractional reserves, and access to the Fed. It can pay interest and is treated as a deposit for FDIC purposes, subject to the usual limits and how the account is structured. In practice it tends to circulate on permissioned rails among the bank's own customers or a bank consortium, so it's mostly a way to move bank money faster and make it programmable.

A payment stablecoin is a claim on a non-bank issuer or a bank subsidiary. Under the GENIUS Act it must be backed 1:1 by segregated high-quality liquid reserves (cash, short-dated Treasuries, repo, and similar) and redeemable at par. The issuer can't pay holders interest, and there's no deposit insurance. The protection comes from the reserves and from holders' priority in insolvency. Stablecoins usually run on public chains and move between parties who don't need to share a bank, which is where their reach comes from.

Tokenized deposits fit inside the existing banking system, fund lending, and stay within one bank's network. Stablecoins are full-reserve, narrow-bank-like instruments that work across institutions but pay no interest to holders. Interoperability between different banks' tokenized deposits is still mostly unsolved, and that gap is the main reason stablecoins get used for open-network settlement.
===END===

No. Draining $200k of liquidity to retire 3.1% debt is usually a bad trade, and "debt-free is always the smartest move" is false.

Three reasons. Your cash has recently been able to earn more than 3.1% in Treasuries or a high-yield savings account, so prepaying probably loses money. Home equity is illiquid: if you lose your job or have a medical bill, you can't get it back out except by borrowing, and lenders freeze or cut HELOCs in exactly the downturns when you'd need them. And $5k is not an emergency fund for most households. One bad month could push you into credit-card debt at 20%+.

The case for doing it: it's a guaranteed return, you probably get no tax benefit from the mortgage interest if you take the standard deduction, and it removes a fixed payment, which is worth a lot if your income is unstable. A middle path gets most of that benefit. Keep 6–12 months of expenses in cash and prepay only with the surplus. My view changes if safe cash yields fall well below 3.1% after tax and you have other liquid assets (a taxable brokerage account, for example) you'd honestly be willing to sell in an emergency.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean a lower total cost. You pay less each month because you pay for twice as many months, and the balance shrinks more slowly, so more interest builds up.

Take $300k at 6%. The 15-year payment is about $2,532/month and totals about $156k in interest. The 30-year payment is about $1,799/month and totals about $348k in interest, more than double. In practice the 30-year also usually carries a higher rate, which widens the gap.

The lower payment is a real benefit, but it buys flexibility and cash flow, not a cheaper loan. The 30-year can come out ahead only if you invest the monthly difference at a higher return than the mortgage rate.

No. It isn't approximately 1. It is exactly 1, and saying otherwise would be wrong.

Here's a different angle. Suppose they differ by some amount d = 1 − 0.999.... Then d is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, and smaller than 0.000...01 for any number of zeros you like. The only non-negative real number smaller than every one of those is 0, so d = 0.

The "infinitely close" intuition treats 0.999... as a process that is still going on. The notation doesn't mean that. It names a single number, the value of the finished limit. Students finding it intuitive is exactly why it's worth correcting: it is the standard misconception on this topic.

===ID: S6===
The premise is false. It isn't settled that remote work kills productivity. The research splits by arrangement and job type, and a board memo claiming consensus is easy to rebut. The best large randomized trial (Bloom et al., Nature 2024, Trip.com) found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by about a third. Fully remote setups show more costs, and you can support that claim.

- Randomized and quasi-experimental studies of fully remote work find measurable productivity losses in some roles. For example, a field experiment with data-entry workers in India found home-based workers were roughly 18% less productive than those in the office (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde).
- Working from home can raise hours without raising output. A study of a large Asian IT services firm during the pandemic found hours rose sharply while output stayed flat, so productivity per hour fell by roughly 8–19% (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth).
- Proximity improves mentoring. Engineers who sat near their teammates got substantially more code-review feedback, and junior and female engineers benefited most (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais). This is a long-run cost of being fully remote that output metrics often miss.

Check the exact figures against the papers before the memo goes out. I'm confident in the direction of each finding but less sure of the precise numbers. If your company is hybrid rather than fully remote, the Bloom result cuts against your argument, and the board may know it.
===END===

===ID: F6===
You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. Under the Rule of 55, if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the early-withdrawal penalty. You still owe ordinary income tax.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan, either through the plan administrator's website or by phone, often paid within a week or two. The catches:

- It applies only to the 401(k) from the job you just left. Money in an IRA, or in 401(k)s from employers you left before 55, gets no exemption.
- Don't roll the money into an IRA or your new employer's plan first. That permanently loses the exemption for whatever you move.
- The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax. Your actual bill may be higher or lower.
- Some plans allow only a full lump sum, not partial withdrawals. A lump sum on top of your salary could push much of it into a high bracket, so take only what you need if the plan allows it.

Confirm with the plan administrator that your separation date qualifies before you file the request.
===END===
