`var` is function-scoped, so the loop has one `i` and all three arrow functions close over it. `setTimeout` callbacks run only after the loop has finished, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`. It creates a new binding on each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
```

Probably not as a blanket move at 56. The main risk is timing, not long-run return.

The S&P 500 has fallen roughly 50% twice this century (2000–2002 and 2007–2009) and went nearly flat from 2000 to 2009. If a drop like that comes just before or early in retirement and you're selling shares to live on, you lock in the losses, and the portfolio may never get back to where it would have been. This is sequence-of-returns risk, and it matters most in the 5–10 years around retirement. A 100% index fund also means 100% US large-cap stocks, with a large share in a handful of tech companies and no bonds, cash, small caps or international holdings.

The strongest case for doing it: fees are very low, US stocks have had the best long-run returns, and a 56-year-old could plausibly live another 30+ years. It becomes more defensible if a pension, Social Security or other income will cover most of your spending, if you won't touch this money for 10+ years, and if you're sure you wouldn't sell during a 50% drop. Most people who say they wouldn't sell do sell.

A more common setup is to hold several years of expected withdrawals in bonds or cash and keep the rest in broad equities. The right split depends on your retirement date, other income and spending needs. For a decision this size, it's worth running the numbers with a fee-only fiduciary advisor.

Yes, you're right, and it's not pedantic. A p-value of 0.03 is the probability of getting data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis is true: P(data | H0). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is a claim about P(H1 | data). Getting from one to the other requires the prior plausibility of the hypothesis and the study's power, and the p-value contains neither.

The gap is large in practice. Under fairly generous assumptions (the Sellke–Berger bound, with 50/50 prior odds), p = 0.03 still leaves at least about a 22% chance that the null is true. When the hypothesis was unlikely to begin with, or the study is underpowered, that chance is much higher. This is why so many "significant" findings fail to replicate. Treating 1 − p as the probability the hypothesis is true reliably overstates the evidence.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It uses a handshake to set up a connection, then delivers a byte stream in order with no losses or duplicates, retransmitting whatever gets lost, and it applies flow and congestion control. That costs setup latency and overhead, and one lost packet stalls everything behind it (head-of-line blocking).

UDP is connectionless. It sends individual datagrams with no guarantee of delivery or order and no retransmission or congestion control, but it keeps message boundaries and adds minimal latency and overhead. Any reliability you need, you build yourself.

Use TCP when every byte has to arrive intact: HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer. Use UDP when fresh data matters more than complete data, or when you want to design your own reliability: DNS, VoIP, video calls, games, and QUIC, which HTTP/3 runs on.
===END===

Yes. Rebase replays your commits onto a new base, and a commit's hash covers its parent, tree, author and committer metadata. The rebased commits are therefore new commits with new hashes, even when their diffs are identical. The originals stay reachable through the reflog until garbage collection.

In practice, rebasing commits you've already pushed means you have to force-push (use `--force-with-lease`), and anyone who built on the old commits has to reconcile. My earlier answer was wrong, but not about speed. Use `==` for strings.

`is` checks whether two names point to the same object, not whether the values are equal. CPython interns some strings, such as short identifier-like literals, but that's an implementation detail and not guaranteed. Strings built at runtime (from `input()`, `.join()`, slicing, or file reads) are usually separate objects, so `a is b` can be `False` when `a == b`. Since 3.8, Python even raises a SyntaxWarning for `is` against a literal.

On speed, you have it backwards. `is` is a pointer comparison and can't be slower than `==`. `==` on strings checks identity first anyway, then compares length and content. The difference doesn't matter, because `is` gives wrong answers.

The premise is a myth. Einstein didn't fail math. He was excellent at it and had taught himself calculus by about 15. The story probably comes from two things. What matters most is whether they're learning and putting in real effort, and a bad grade is information about what to work on, not a verdict.
