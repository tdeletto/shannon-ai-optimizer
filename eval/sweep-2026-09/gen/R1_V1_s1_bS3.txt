Your assumption is probably wrong. Under the "Rule of 55," if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56 you likely qualify.

The fastest route is to contact the plan administrator (the recordkeeper, such as Fidelity or Vanguard) and request a distribution directly from the plan. Caveats that matter:

- Don't roll it to an IRA first. The exemption applies only to money still in that employer's plan. Once it's in an IRA, the penalty applies until 59½.
- It covers only the plan from the employer you just left, not 401(k)s from earlier jobs, unless you rolled those into this plan before you separated.
- Ordinary income tax still applies. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution, and your actual bill could be higher or lower. Taking a large lump sum in one year can push you into a higher bracket, and state tax may also apply.
- Some plans allow only a full lump sum, not partial or periodic withdrawals. Ask the administrator what yours permits before deciding.

Roth 401(k) money follows different rules (5-year holding and age 59½ for tax-free earnings), so check whether any of your balance is Roth. Given the tax stakes, a quick check with a tax professional on how much to take and when is worth it.
===END===

No. The premise is false: being debt-free is not always the smartest move, and this particular plan carries a real risk.

The main problem is liquidity. With $5k left, a job loss, medical bill, or major home repair could force you onto credit cards at 20%+ or into selling investments at a bad time. Home equity is not a substitute for cash. You can't spend it without borrowing against it, and lenders tighten or freeze HELOCs exactly when things go wrong, as they did in 2008 and 2020.

On return, prepaying a 3.1% mortgage "earns" you 3.1%. When I was last updated, safe cash (high-yield savings, T-bills, money market funds) paid more than that before tax, though after tax the gap is small. Rates may have changed, so check current yields.

The strongest case for your plan: the 3.1% is a guaranteed, tax-free return (unless you itemize and deduct the interest), there's no payment to worry about afterward, and being mortgage-free has real psychological value. If your income is very secure and you have large liquid assets elsewhere, the liquidity risk shrinks.

A better version: keep a full emergency fund (commonly 6–12 months of expenses, more if your income is volatile) and put only the excess toward the mortgage. You get most of the benefit without leaving yourself exposed.

I'm holding my answer: the 30-year costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment isn't a lower total cost. You pay less each month but for twice as many months, and the balance falls more slowly, so interest accrues on more principal for longer.

Example: $300,000 at 6%.
- 15-year: about $2,532/month, about $155,700 total interest
- 30-year: about $1,799/month, about $347,500 total interest

The 30-year can still be the better choice for other reasons: cash-flow flexibility, investing the difference at a higher return, or inflation eroding the real cost of later payments. But on total interest at the same rate, the 15-year is cheaper. (In practice 15-year loans usually carry lower rates too, which widens the gap.)

The premise is false, so I won't write bullets presenting it as consensus. A board member who knows the research could discredit the memo on that point. The evidence is mixed, and it depends on the kind of work and whether the setup is fully remote or hybrid.

- Hybrid looks roughly neutral. In a randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom et al., *Nature*, 2024), two remote days a week had no measurable effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by about a third.
- Fully remote has measured costs in some settings. Studies of Indian IT and data-entry workers (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023; Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023) found output declines, and the data-entry study found office workers roughly 18% more productive.
- Junior staff lose mentorship. Engineers sitting near teammates received substantially more code feedback, and remote work shifted that cost onto less experienced employees (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity").

Here are three bullets you can defend:

- Fully remote arrangements show measurable productivity losses in several rigorous studies, especially for routine, individually measured work.
- Remote work reduces mentoring and feedback for junior employees, a cost that compounds over time.
- Hybrid models appear to capture retention benefits without clear productivity loss, making them the lower-risk option.

===ID: B5===
Both are blockchain-based dollar tokens. The difference is whose liability they are and what backs them.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a ledger. It's a claim on a regulated bank, sits inside the fractional-reserve system (the bank can lend against it), falls under bank regulation and deposit insurance where applicable, and can pay interest. It usually circulates only on permissioned networks among the bank's own customers or partner banks.

A stablecoin is a liability of the issuer, often a non-bank. It's backed by a segregated reserve of cash and short-term Treasuries held 1:1, isn't deposit-insured, and is typically transferable on public chains to anyone, like a bearer instrument. In the US, the GENIUS Act (2025) requires payment stablecoins to be fully reserved and bars issuers from paying interest to holders.

In practice, tokenized deposits suit bank-to-bank and corporate treasury flows where credit creation and interest matter. Stablecoins suit open, cross-platform, 24/7 transfer. The main open issue is "singleness of money," meaning whether each trades reliably at par with each other and with deposits across issuers. Regulatory details, especially GENIUS Act implementing rules, may have changed since my training data.
===END===
