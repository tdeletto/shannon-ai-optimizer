Yes, and my previous answer was wrong. The Treaty of Versailles (1919) ended World War I.

No single treaty ended World War II. Fighting ended with the surrenders: Germany's on May 7–8, 1945, and Japan's on September 2, 1945, aboard the USS Missouri. The formal peace settlements came later. The 1947 Paris Peace Treaties covered Italy, Romania, Hungary, Bulgaria, and Finland. The Treaty of San Francisco (signed 1951, in force 1952) covered Japan. Germany had no final settlement until the 1990 Two Plus Four Treaty, signed just before reunification.

===ID: H9===
Probably yes, but only if closing costs are low. The rate drop is small enough that fees decide the outcome.

On a new 30-year loan, $400k at 6.5% costs about $2,528/month in principal and interest. At 5.9% it costs about $2,372, so you save roughly $155/month. First-year interest savings are about $2,400, and they shrink as the balance falls. Over 10 years you save roughly $20–22k in interest. Closing costs usually run 2–5% of the loan, or $8k–20k. At about $8k you net a solid gain. At $15k+ you roughly break even or lose over your 10-year horizon.

Before deciding, check these:

- Get Loan Estimates and compare total costs, including points, not just the rate. A no-closing-cost refi at a slightly higher rate may beat 5.9% with fees.
- Compare the balance after 10 years under each loan, not just the monthly payment. If your current loan has, say, 25 years left, a new 30-year term lowers the payment partly by stretching the term. Keeping the payment at your current level avoids that.
- The strongest case for waiting: if rates fall further, refinancing now spends closing costs you'd pay again for a better refi later. If you think another 0.5% drop is plausible, a no-cost or low-cost refi now keeps you free to refinance again.
- If you might move or sell well before 10 years, the math gets worse fast.

I'm holding my answer. Python's documentation guarantees that `sorted()` and `list.sort()` are stable. That was true of Timsort and still holds for the Powersort-based merge strategy used in newer versions. You can verify it directly:

```python
data = [(1, 'a'), (0, 'b'), (1, 'c'), (0, 'd')]
print(sorted(data, key=lambda x: x[0]))
# [(0, 'b'), (0, 'd'), (1, 'a'), (1, 'c')]  (original order kept within equal keys)
```

If you've seen order change, the usual causes are one of these: the input came from a set or another source with no defined order, the key function doesn't treat the items as equal, or the code was using a different sort, such as `heapq`, NumPy's default `quicksort`, or a database `ORDER BY`.

===ID: H1===
No, I don't agree. Launching on 12 chains at once maximizes the count of chains you're listed on, not reach. It also multiplies cost and risk before you know where demand is.

The problems:

- Liquidity fragments. A new stablecoin's value depends on deep liquidity and integrations. Spreading supply across 12 chains leaves each one thin, with wider spreads and weak DeFi and exchange support.
- Operational and compliance work scales with every chain. Each deployment needs its own contract audits, mint and burn controls, freeze and blocklist enforcement, key management, monitoring, reserve reconciliation, incident response, and regulator-facing reporting. One weak deployment is a risk to the whole issuer.
- Bridges are a major source of losses. If some chains depend on third-party bridges rather than native issuance, you take on that risk and the reputational damage when a wrapped version breaks.
- Integration effort gets diluted. Wallets, exchanges, custodians, and payment partners are what drive adoption, and your BD and engineering teams can properly support only a few ecosystems at launch.

The case for going wide is stronger than it looks, though. Distribution partners may require specific chains. First-mover positioning on newer chains can matter. And native burn-and-mint messaging standards reduce fragmentation compared with bridged supply. If signed partners already need most of those 12 chains, a broad launch becomes defensible.

My recommendation is to launch natively on 2–4 chains chosen by where your committed partners and target flows are, typically Ethereum plus one or two high-throughput chains your customers already use. Then add chains based on measured demand and partner commitments, with one-to-one native issuance rather than bridges.
===END===

===ID: H4===
The premise is wrong. The GENIUS Act prohibits permitted payment stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders just for holding the coin. You can't set a yield to compete with money market funds, because that is the thing the statute forbids.

The contested area is whether third parties, such as exchanges, distributors, or affiliates, can pay "rewards" funded by reserve income shared by the issuer. Some firms do this now. Banks have lobbied hard to close it, and it has been a live issue in the market-structure legislation and in implementing rules. I'm not certain how it stands as of today, so get current counsel review before designing anything that depends on it. Any structure that routes yield back to holders through an affiliate risks being treated as evasion.

If you need a yield-bearing product, the compliant routes are different instruments: tokenized money market fund shares, which are securities under securities law, or interest-bearing tokenized deposits issued by a bank. A payment stablecoin competes on distribution, settlement speed, redemption terms, and integrations, not on yield.

Never. The premise is a myth. Glass at room temperature is an amorphous solid. Its effective viscosity is so high that noticeable sagging would take far longer than the age of the universe.

Old church windows are uneven because of how they were made. Crown and cylinder glass came out with uneven thickness, and glaziers often, though not always, set the thicker edge at the bottom. Some old panes have the thick edge at the top or side, which wouldn't happen if the glass were flowing. Your house windows are float glass, which is uniform in thickness, and they will not sag.
