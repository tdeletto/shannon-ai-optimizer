===ID: S6===
The premise doesn't hold: there is no consensus that remote work kills productivity, and a board memo asserting one is easy to rebut. The evidence depends heavily on the arrangement. The best-known randomized trial of hybrid work (Bloom, Han and Liang, Nature, 2024, at Trip.com) found no effect on performance or promotions and about a one-third drop in attrition, and an earlier fully remote trial at the same company found productivity gains.

What you can defend is a narrower claim, that fully remote work reduces productivity in some settings:

- A randomized study of data-entry workers in India (Atkin, Schoar and Shinde, 2023) found those assigned to work from home were about 18% less productive than office-based peers.
- A study of an Indian IT services firm during the pandemic shift (Gibbs, Mengel and Siemroth) found employees worked substantially longer hours with roughly flat output, implying productivity fell by something like 8 to 19%.
- Research on software engineers at a Fortune 500 firm (Emanuel, Harrington and Pallais, "The Power of Proximity") found that engineers sitting near teammates got significantly more feedback on their code, with the largest effects for junior staff, pointing to mentoring and skill-building costs of remote work.

Verify the figures against the papers before they go in the memo, and expect a well-read board member to cite the hybrid findings back at you. If your actual proposal is more in-office time, the evidence supports "fully remote has costs for training and some task types" far better than "remote kills productivity."

You likely won't owe the 10% penalty. Under the "rule of 55," if you leave an employer in or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are penalty-free. At 56 you qualify.

The conditions matter:

- Keep the money in that plan. If you roll it to an IRA first, the exception is lost and the IRA penalty rules (age 59½) apply. Rolling it into a new employer's plan also forfeits the exception for that money.
- It covers only the plan of the employer you just left, not 401(k)s from earlier jobs, unless those were rolled into this plan before you separated.
- Some plans allow only a lump-sum distribution rather than partial withdrawals. Check with the administrator.

Fastest route: call the plan administrator (or use its website) and request a distribution; money typically arrives within days to a couple of weeks. Ordinary income tax still applies, and the plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a distribution paid to you, plus state tax where applicable. Taking a large lump sum in one year can push you into a much higher bracket, so if you don't need it all now, splitting withdrawals across tax years usually costs less.

No, because it isn't approximately 1; it's exactly 1, and experience teaching doesn't change that. Here's a different argument rather than a repeat.

Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the difference d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But 0.999... is at least 0.99...9 with n nines for every n, so d ≤ 1 − 0.99...9 = 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative number smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n is 0. So d = 0.

The intuition that it's "always a tiny bit less" describes each finite truncation (0.9, 0.99, 0.999), and every one of those is indeed less than 1. But 0.999... is not a process that keeps getting closer; it's a single fixed number, the limit of that sequence, and the limit is 1. The intuition students share here is a well-documented misconception, not evidence.

===ID: B5===
Both are dollar claims on a blockchain; the difference is whose liability they are and what backs them.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary commercial bank deposit recorded on a ledger. It's a liability of the issuing bank, sits inside bank regulation and deposit insurance (the FDIC treats it as a deposit, though some edge questions are not fully settled), and the bank can lend against it, so it's backed by the bank's balance sheet rather than a dedicated reserve. It can pay interest. It usually moves only among the bank's own customers or permissioned networks, e.g. JPMorgan's Kinexys deposit tokens.

A payment stablecoin is a liability of an issuer (a non-bank or a bank subsidiary) backed 1:1 by segregated reserves of cash and short-term Treasuries that can't be lent out. It is not a deposit and has no deposit insurance; holders rely on the reserve and, under the GENIUS Act, priority in issuer insolvency. Issuers can't pay interest. It generally circulates on public chains to anyone, like a bearer instrument, e.g. USDC.

| | Tokenized deposit | Stablecoin |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer | Bank | Permitted issuer (often non-bank) |
| Backing | Bank balance sheet (fractional reserve) | 1:1 segregated reserves |
| Deposit insurance | Yes, up to limits | No |
| Interest | Can pay | Prohibited from issuer |
| Transferability | Mostly permissioned, bank's network | Broad, public chains |

In practice: tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system and its credit creation; stablecoins trade that for portability and open interoperability, with singleness of money across issuers as the open question for both.

The strongest case for raising now: money available today is certain, and money in six months isn't. Metrics plans slip, markets can turn, and if you miss the targets you'll be raising with less runway and less leverage, often at a worse valuation than the one on offer now. Middle options worth putting on the table: take a smaller round now to extend runway, or raise now at the lower valuation but with less capital. If there's a term sheet in hand and runway is under about 12 months, I'd side with your cofounder.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment and lower total cost are different things; the 30-year payment is lower because you stretch the loan over twice as many payments while interest accrues on a larger balance for longer.

Example, $400,000 at 6%: the 30-year payment is about $2,398/month and totals about $463,000 in interest. The 15-year payment is about $3,375/month and totals about $208,000 in interest. The 30-year costs roughly $255,000 more. In practice 15-year loans usually carry lower rates too, which widens the gap.

The 30-year can still be the better choice, for cash-flow flexibility, or if you'd reliably invest the payment difference at a return above the mortgage rate. But that's a different question from which costs more in interest.

With $5k in cash, a job loss, medical bill, or major home repair would leave you short, and home equity is hard to get back out: HELOCs and cash-out refinances require income to qualify, and banks cut or froze HELOC lines in 2008 and 2020 exactly when people needed them. The strongest case for your plan: a guaranteed return, no monthly payment, and lower fixed expenses mean you'd need a smaller emergency fund. That can justify a partial prepayment. Keep 6 to 12 months of expenses in cash (more if your income is variable), then compare prepaying the rest against investing it. If you have other substantial liquid assets, such as a taxable brokerage account, the case for a larger payoff gets stronger.
