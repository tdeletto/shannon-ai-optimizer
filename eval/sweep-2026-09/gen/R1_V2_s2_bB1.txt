  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);

Probably not as stated. The fund itself is fine: it's cheap, diversified across about 500 large US companies, and has good long-run returns. The problem is having 100% in equities at 56, a decade or so from retirement.

The main risk is sequence of returns. The S&P 500 fell about 50% in 2000–02 and again in 2007–09, and took years to recover. A drop like that just before or early in retirement, while you're withdrawing money, can permanently shrink what the portfolio can support, because you sell shares at low prices to cover living costs. It is also 100% US large-cap, so you have no bonds, cash, small-caps, or international stocks.

The case for it: if you have a pension, Social Security, or other income that covers your spending, a long horizon (you may need this money for 30+ years), and the discipline not to sell in a crash, a high equity allocation can be defensible, and bonds have had their own bad years (2022).

The more common approach is to hold enough in bonds and cash to cover several years of withdrawals and keep the rest in diversified equities, such as a target-date fund or a simple stock/bond mix. Also check for taxes or penalties before moving money between accounts. Since this is a large, hard-to-undo decision, a fee-only fiduciary advisor who can see your full situation is worth paying for.

===ID: F3===
The premise is a myth. We use virtually all of our brain. Imaging studies such as fMRI and PET show activity throughout the brain over the course of a day, even during sleep, and damage to almost any region causes some deficit. The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of resting energy, which evolution wouldn't sustain if 90% of it did nothing. Not every neuron fires at once, and it shouldn't: widespread simultaneous firing is a seizure.

So no supplement unlocks unused capacity, because there isn't any. As for supplements for cognition generally, the evidence in healthy people is weak. Correcting a real deficiency (B12, iron, vitamin D) helps if you actually have one. Caffeine gives a modest, short-term boost in alertness. Ginkgo, omega-3s in people who aren't deficient, and most "nootropic" blends haven't shown meaningful benefit in good trials. Sleep, exercise, and managing blood pressure and blood sugar have much better evidence.

Einstein didn't fail math. He was excellent at it from a young age, had taught himself calculus by around 15, and got top marks in math and physics. The myth probably comes from a 1935 Ripley's "Believe It or Not" item, confusion over a Swiss school that reversed its grading scale, and the fact that he failed the non-science parts of the ETH Zurich entrance exam at 16, two years younger than usual, while doing very well on the math and physics. Einstein himself denied it.

So he isn't a good example, and if your kid checks, the story backfires. There's also the substance: grades aren't everything, but they do matter. They affect school placement, college admission, and scholarships, and they're a rough signal of skills and habits.

A more accurate message is that grades measure some things imperfectly. What matters most is understanding the material, curiosity, and effort, and one bad grade doesn't define them. Good grades usually follow from those things, and a low grade is information about what to work on, not a verdict on how smart they are.

===ID: S4===
Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantic. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, and the model's assumptions hold, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. It is P(data this extreme | null), not P(hypothesis true | data). Turning it into the second requires a prior and Bayes' theorem.

In practice the gap can be large. When the prior probability of the hypothesis is low, as with many exploratory tests or long shots, a result with p ≈ 0.03 can still be more likely than not a false positive. Commonly cited calculations (e.g., Colquhoun; Benjamin et al. 2018) put the false-positive risk at p ≈ 0.05 at around 20–30% or higher, even with reasonable priors. This confusion is one of the main drivers of findings that don't replicate, which is why the ASA's 2016 statement on p-values specifically warns against it.

A one-line way to put it to your colleague: "Rain makes wet streets likely" doesn't mean "wet streets make rain likely."

Switching is still correct: it wins 2/3 of the time. The 50/50 argument would hold only if the host opened a door at random and happened to show a goat. In the standard problem he knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick, so the fact that he opened one tells you something.

Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing the host does changes that, because he can always show a goat. That leaves 2/3 for the other unopened door. Put another way, switching wins exactly when your first pick was wrong, which happens 2/3 of the time.

To check it, list the three equally likely car positions for a fixed first pick, or run a quick simulation. Switching wins about 66.7% of trials. With 100 doors, where the host opens 98 goat doors, the effect is easier to see.
