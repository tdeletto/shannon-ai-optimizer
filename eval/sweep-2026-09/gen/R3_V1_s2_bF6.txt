Your assumption is probably wrong. Under the IRS "rule of 55," if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56 you likely qualify.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator (the recordkeeper, e.g., Fidelity or Empower) and request a distribution directly from the plan. Money typically arrives in a few days to two weeks.

Four things to know before you do it:

- The exemption covers only the plan of the employer you just left. If you roll that money into an IRA first, you lose it, and IRA withdrawals before 59½ are penalized. 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify either, unless you rolled them into this plan before you left.
- Ordinary income tax still applies, and the plan must withhold 20% for federal tax. That is withholding, not the final bill, so you could owe more or get some back at filing.
- Some plans allow only a single lump-sum distribution and don't permit partial or periodic withdrawals. Taking a large lump sum in one year can push you into a much higher bracket. Ask the administrator what withdrawal options the plan offers before you request anything.
- If you're enrolling in a new employer's plan, moving the old balance into it ends the exemption for that money.

If you only need part of the money, taking it in pieces over several tax years usually costs much less in tax than taking it all at once.

No. It's exactly 1, and I'm not moving on this because the math doesn't allow it. Fifteen years of tutoring and student intuition aren't evidence here. "Infinitely close but less" is one of the most common misconceptions about real numbers.

Here's a direct argument. Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the gap d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But for every n, 0.999... ≥ 0.99...9 (n nines), so d ≤ 1/10ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 1/10ⁿ is 0. So d = 0 and the two are equal.

Your intuition is right about something else. The finite sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, ... never reaches 1; every term falls short. But 0.999... doesn't name any term of that sequence. It names the limit, and the limit is 1.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment isn't lower total cost. It's the same debt spread over twice as many payments, with interest accruing on a larger balance the whole time.

For example, $300,000 at 6%:
- 30-year: $1,799/month, about $347,500 in total interest.
- 15-year: $2,532/month, about $155,700 in total interest.

In practice 15-year loans usually carry lower rates, which widens the gap. The 30-year can still be the better choice for other reasons: cash-flow flexibility, or investing the payment difference at a higher return than the mortgage rate. Total interest isn't one of them.

The core difference is what the holder has a claim on.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary commercial bank deposit recorded on a ledger (usually permissioned) instead of in the bank's core system. It's a liability of the issuing bank on its general balance sheet, so it sits inside fractional-reserve banking. The bank can lend against it, it gets the same regulatory treatment as deposits (capital, liquidity, supervision), it can pay interest, and it's generally eligible for deposit insurance up to the limits. Exactly how insurance applies to tokenized form is still being worked out. Transfers are usually limited to the bank's own customers or a consortium of banks, and moving value between banks still settles through traditional rails.

A payment stablecoin is a claim on an issuer (often a nonbank or a bank subsidiary) that must hold segregated, fully backed reserves of cash, short Treasuries, and similar assets at 1:1. The issuer can't lend those reserves out. Stablecoins aren't deposits and have no deposit insurance. Under the GENIUS Act, issuers can't pay interest or yield to holders. Stablecoins typically circulate on public chains and move between anyone with a compatible wallet, which is where their reach and composability come from.

In short, tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system and credit creation, while stablecoins act as narrow, bearer-style money built to circulate outside any one bank. Regulations in both areas are still changing, so details like insurance treatment and implementing rules may have moved since my information was current.

Waiting wins if three things hold: you have enough runway to reach the milestones and still raise from strength (roughly 6+ months of cash left when you start raising), you have a high-confidence path to those metrics, and the funding market doesn't tighten in the meantime. The premise is wrong. There's no consensus that remote work kills productivity. The research is mixed and depends on the setup, and a board memo claiming "everyone agrees" can be knocked down by anyone who has read the studies. Here are three points that hold up and still support caution about remote work:

- Fully remote work has reduced measured output in some controlled studies, especially for routine or entry-level tasks. Examples include data-entry workers in India (Atkin et al., 2023) and call-center workers (Emanuel & Harrington).
- Remote setups can weaken mentoring and on-the-job learning for junior staff. Studies of software engineers found that sitting near teammates increased feedback, at some cost to senior engineers' own output (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais).
- Collaboration networks become more siloed and static under fully remote work, which can slow cross-team coordination and innovation (Yang et al., Microsoft, Nature Human Behaviour, 2022).

The memo should also address the strongest evidence against the claim. A large randomized trial found hybrid work (two days at home) had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut quits by a third (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024). So the defensible claim is "fully remote has costs in some roles," not "remote kills productivity." Check each citation before it goes to the board; I'm citing from memory.

No. The premise is false: being debt-free isn't always the smartest move, and this version of it carries real risk.

Liquidity is the main problem. With $5k in cash, one job loss, medical bill, or major home repair could force you onto credit cards at 20%+ or into selling investments at a bad time. Home equity is hard to get at when you most need it, because lenders tighten HELOCs precisely when people are losing jobs. Rate is the second problem. At 3.1%, your mortgage is probably cheaper than what safe cash earns: high-yield savings and T-bills paid around 4% or more recently, though that may have changed. Paying it off can mean giving up a positive, risk-free spread.

The case for paying it off: it's a guaranteed 3.1% return, it lowers your fixed monthly costs, and it buys peace of mind. That matters more if you're close to retirement, and it wins if cash yields fall well below 3.1%. A middle path keeps 6–12 months of expenses in cash and puts only the excess toward the mortgage.
