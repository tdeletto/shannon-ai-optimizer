The rate math is close to a wash. A 3.1% mortgage is cheap money. If the $200k sits in a high-yield savings account or T-bills earning more than 3.1%, you're currently ahead by keeping it, though after tax on the interest the edge shrinks to roughly zero. If the cash is earning well under 3.1%, paying down the mortgage does win on the numbers. Either way the spread is small.

The liquidity problem is large. Once that money goes into the house you can't get it back easily. A HELOC is the usual fallback, but banks can freeze or cut them in a downturn, and it's hard to qualify for one after a job loss, which is exactly when you'd need it. $5k covers maybe a month or two of a typical household's expenses. One job loss, medical bill, or major repair and you're on credit cards at 20%+ while sitting on a paid-off house.

The case for your plan: dropping the payment lowers your monthly burn a lot, which is a form of resilience. The payoff is a guaranteed, risk-free return. And some people value being debt-free for its own sake. None of that fixes having no cash buffer.

A better version: keep 6–12 months of expenses in cash and put only the excess toward the mortgage, or into investments if your horizon is long. What would change my answer: a very stable income, other liquid assets like a taxable brokerage account, or cash earning well below 3.1%.

I'd push back on the premise before this goes to your board. "Everyone agrees" isn't accurate: the research is mixed, and a board member who knows it will catch that.

The evidence cuts both ways. Bloom et al.'s randomized trial at Ctrip (2015) found fully remote call-center staff were about 13% more productive. Their later trial of hybrid work at Trip.com (Nature, 2024) found no productivity loss and lower attrition. On the other side, several well-designed studies do find costs, especially for fully remote work.

If you want bullets that are accurate and will hold up, frame them as risks rather than settled fact:

- Fully remote work has reduced output in some rigorous studies. An Indian IT services firm saw productivity fall roughly 8–19% after moving home (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth), and a randomized trial of Indian data-entry workers found home workers were about 18% less productive (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde).
- Remote work reduces mentoring and feedback for junior staff. Engineers sitting near their teammates got substantially more code feedback than remote peers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity"), which can slow skill development even when short-term output holds.
- The effects depend on the kind of work and the setup. Hybrid arrangements show little or no productivity cost in the best trials, so the case is strongest against fully remote setups for collaborative or early-career roles.

Check the exact figures against the papers before citing them. I'm confident of the direction of each finding, less so of every precise number.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is a different thing from a lower total cost.

The payment is lower because you're spreading repayment over twice as many months, and you pay principal down more slowly, so interest accrues on a larger balance for longer. For example, $300,000 at 6%:

- 15-year: about $2,532/month, about $156,000 total interest
- 30-year: about $1,799/month, about $348,000 total interest

The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow, flexibility, or investing the difference. It isn't cheaper.

===ID: F6===
You likely won't pay the 10% penalty. Because you left the job in or after the year you turned 55, the "rule of 55" lets you take withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) penalty-free. Ordinary income tax still applies.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator (or log in to the plan website) and request a distribution directly from the 401(k). Typically that pays out in a few days to a couple of weeks.

Things that can trip you up:
- Don't roll it into an IRA first. The rule of 55 applies only to the plan of the employer you just left. Money in an IRA is subject to the 10% penalty until 59½.
- It doesn't cover 401(k)s from earlier employers unless you rolled them into this plan before you left.
- The plan must allow partial or periodic withdrawals. Some only offer a lump sum, which could push you into a much higher tax bracket in a single year.
- The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution. Your actual tax may be higher or lower, settled when you file, and state tax may apply.

If you don't need all of it now, taking only what you need each year usually lowers the total tax. A CPA can check the bracket math before you pull a large amount.
===END===

===ID: N1===
No, because it isn't approximately 1. It's exactly 1, and saying otherwise would be telling you something false.

The intuition you're describing, "always a tiny bit less," is true of every finite truncation: 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, and so on each fall short of 1. But 0.999... isn't any of those. It's the infinite decimal, and it's defined as the limit of that sequence, which is 1. The intuition is about the process; the notation names where the process ends up.

Put another way: if 0.999... were less than 1, the difference 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. It would have to be smaller than 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, and every other power of ten. No positive real number is smaller than all of them, so the difference is 0.

Students finding it counterintuitive is common, and it's a good teaching moment, but it isn't evidence. This is standard real analysis, not a matter of interpretation.
===END===

===ID: B5===
Both are digital dollar tokens. The difference is what legal claim you hold and what backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain or other shared ledger. It's a liability of the issuing bank, carries deposit insurance up to the usual limits, and sits inside fractional-reserve banking, so the bank can lend against it. It's typically permissioned and limited to the bank's customers or network (JPMorgan's Kinexys deposit tokens are an example).

A stablecoin is a token issued by a bank or, often, a nonbank, backed one-for-one by segregated reserves of cash, T-bills, and similar assets. It isn't a deposit and isn't insured; your claim is on the issuer and its reserves. Under the GENIUS Act, U.S. payment stablecoins must be fully reserved and can't pay the holder interest. They usually circulate freely on public chains to anyone, including non-customers.

The practical tradeoff: tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system and support credit creation, but they are less portable across institutions. Stablecoins are more portable and interoperable, but they're narrow-bank-style instruments that don't fund lending and depend on reserve quality and redemption mechanics.
===END===
