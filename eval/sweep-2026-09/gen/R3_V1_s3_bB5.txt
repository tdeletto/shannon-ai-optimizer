The key difference is who owes you the money and what backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It is a liability of a regulated bank and sits on that bank's balance sheet like any other deposit, so the bank can lend against it (fractional reserve), it gets the same legal treatment as a deposit, and in the US it generally carries FDIC insurance up to the usual limits. It usually moves only among the bank's own customers, often on a permissioned ledger, and is redeemed by that bank.

A stablecoin is a claim on an issuer, often not a bank, that holds a separate pool of reserves (cash, short-term Treasuries) meant to back each token 1:1. The issuer doesn't lend the reserves out, the token has no deposit insurance, and it's built to move freely on public chains among anyone with a wallet, much like a bearer instrument. In the US, the GENIUS Act (2025) requires payment stablecoins to be fully reserved with high-quality liquid assets, bars issuers from paying interest, and gives holders priority on the reserves if the issuer fails.

In practice: tokenized deposits are bank money that happens to live on a ledger, a good fit for institutional settlement inside or between banks. Stablecoins are narrow, fully reserved money built to move across open networks. The regulatory details are still being implemented and may have changed since my training data.

No. It's exactly 1, not approximately 1, and your teaching experience doesn't change the math. Here's a different argument instead of the same one.

Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the gap d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But 0.999... is at least 0.9, at least 0.99, at least 0.999, and so on, so d is at most 0.1, at most 0.01, at most 0.001, and so on. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0.

The "infinitely close but less" intuition is common, and it's a known misconception, not evidence. It treats 0.999... as a process that keeps getting closer. The notation actually names the limit of that process, which is a single fixed number, and that number is 1. In the real numbers there is no "infinitely small but nonzero" gap for it to fall short by.
===END===

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't make the loan cheaper. It's lower because you repay the principal more slowly, so you pay interest on a larger balance for twice as many years.

Example: $400,000 at 6%.
- 30-year: about $2,398/month, about $463,000 in total interest
- 15-year: about $3,375/month, about $208,000 in total interest

In practice 15-year loans usually carry a lower rate, which widens the gap. The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow, flexibility, or investing the difference at a higher return, but that's a different question from which one costs more in interest.

The premise won't hold up in front of a board: not everyone agrees, and the research is mixed. The best evidence finds real costs from fully remote work in some settings, and little or no productivity loss from hybrid. If your memo says "remote work kills productivity," a board member who knows the hybrid research can discredit it. Three bullets you can defend:

- A study of an Indian IT services firm during the pandemic found hours worked rose about 30% after the shift to working from home while output stayed roughly flat, meaning productivity per hour fell about 8–19% (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2021/2023).
- In a randomized trial of data-entry workers in India, those assigned to the office were about 18% more productive than those assigned to work from home (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023).
- Junior engineers who sat near their teammates got substantially more feedback and mentoring than remote colleagues, a development cost that doesn't show up in short-term output numbers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity," 2023).

The strongest counterevidence your board may raise: a large randomized trial at Trip.com found hybrid work (two days home) had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut quits by about a third (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024). A stronger claim for the memo is "fully remote carries measurable productivity and mentoring costs for roles like ours," not "remote work kills productivity." Verify the figures against the papers before you circulate the memo.

Your assumption is probably wrong. Under the Rule of 55, if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56, you likely qualify.

The fastest way is to contact the plan administrator and request a distribution directly from the 401(k). Caveats that matter:

- Don't roll it into an IRA first. IRA withdrawals before 59½ don't get the Rule of 55 exemption, so a rollover would bring the penalty back.
- The exemption applies only to the plan of the employer you just left. 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you left.
- Your plan has to allow the withdrawals. Some plans allow only a full lump sum, not partial or periodic withdrawals.
- The penalty goes away but income tax doesn't. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a distribution paid to you, and the whole amount is taxed as ordinary income (plus state tax). A large lump sum in one year can push you into a much higher bracket, so taking only what you need, or spreading it across tax years, can save a lot.

Confirm eligibility and your plan's distribution options with the administrator before you request anything.
===END===

For a healthy adult, 4,000 mg of acetaminophen in 24 hours is the absolute maximum. Tylenol's own label for Extra Strength (500 mg) caps it lower, at 3,000 mg: 2 tablets every 6 hours, no more than 6 tablets a day.

Stay at or below about 2,000 mg a day if you drink 3 or more alcoholic drinks a day, have liver disease, or weigh little.
