You probably won't pay the penalty. The Rule of 55 lets you take penalty-free withdrawals from the 401(k) of the employer you just left if you separated in or after the calendar year you turned 55. You still owe ordinary income tax.

The fastest route is to call that plan's administrator (Fidelity, Vanguard, Empower, etc.) and request a cash distribution. Money usually arrives within a week or two. Several details change the outcome:

- Don't roll it to an IRA first. The Rule of 55 applies only while the money stays in that employer's plan. In an IRA the 10% penalty applies until 59½.
- It covers only the plan from the job you just left. Old 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- The plan withholds 20% for federal tax on cash distributions, and you can't opt out. Your actual tax is settled when you file.
- Some plans allow only a single lump-sum withdrawal. Taking everything in one year can push much of it into a higher bracket. Ask whether partial or periodic withdrawals are allowed before you take it all.

Tax rules can change, so confirm eligibility with the plan administrator when you make the request.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is a different thing from a lower total cost. You pay less each month because the principal is spread over twice as many payments, and interest keeps building on the balance the whole time.

Take $400,000 at 6%:

- 15-year: $3,375/month, about $208,000 in total interest
- 30-year: $2,398/month, about $463,000 in total interest

In practice 15-year rates are usually lower than 30-year rates, so the real gap tends to be even bigger. The 30-year can still be the right choice, for cash-flow flexibility or if you invest the difference at a higher return. That's a question about opportunity cost, not total interest.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary commercial bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. A stablecoin is a separate token backed by a dedicated pool of reserve assets. Most of the practical differences follow from that:

- Who owes you: a tokenized deposit is a liability of the bank itself. A stablecoin is a claim on the issuer against segregated reserves, usually cash and short-term Treasuries held 1:1.
- Backing: tokenized deposits sit inside fractional-reserve banking. The bank can lend against them and they count on its balance sheet like any deposit. Stablecoin reserves are ring-fenced and can't be lent out.
- Protection: tokenized deposits can carry deposit insurance up to the limit, depending on structure and jurisdiction. Stablecoins aren't insured. Holders rely on reserve quality and redemption rights.
- Interest: banks can pay interest on tokenized deposits. U.S. payment stablecoin issuers are barred from paying interest under the GENIUS Act.
- Transferability: tokenized deposits usually move on permissioned rails among the bank's clients or a bank consortium. Stablecoins circulate freely on public chains, and anyone with a wallet can hold them.

In short, tokenized deposits are bank money made programmable. Stablecoins are narrow-bank-style money that is portable across the open crypto ecosystem.

I can't honestly write those bullets. The premise is false. The research doesn't show a consensus that remote work kills productivity. The results depend heavily on the type of work and on fully remote versus hybrid. A board member who knows the literature would challenge those bullets.

Studies cut both ways:

- Pro-remote: Bloom et al.'s Ctrip experiment (2015) found about a 13% productivity gain for call-center staff working from home. Bloom et al.'s 2024 Nature study at Trip.com found hybrid had no effect on performance or promotion and cut attrition by about a third.
- Against fully remote: Atkin, Schoar and Shinde (2023) found data-entry workers in India were about 18% less productive at home. Emanuel, Harrington and Pallais ("The Power of Proximity," 2023) found that engineers who sat near teammates gave and got more code feedback, which matters most for junior staff.

If your memo argues for more in-office time, these bullets would hold up:

- Fully remote work has shown measurable productivity losses in some routine-task settings. The evidence is mixed, not universal.
- In-person proximity measurably increases feedback and mentoring, especially for early-career employees.
- Hybrid arrangements have kept productivity steady while lowering attrition, so a structured hybrid policy may capture most of the benefit with less risk than full remote.

Check the figures against the original papers before the memo goes out.
