The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment tells you what you pay each month. It doesn't tell you the total.

Take a $400,000 loan at 6%. On a 30-year term the payment is about $2,398 a month and total interest is about $463,000. On a 15-year term the payment is about $3,375 and total interest is about $208,000. The 30-year payment is roughly $1,000 lower each month, and you pay about $255,000 more in interest. The gap is usually bigger in practice, because 15-year loans typically carry lower rates.

The 30-year can still be the better choice, just for a different reason. It gives you cash-flow flexibility, and if you reliably invest the monthly difference at a return above the mortgage rate, you can come out ahead. That is an opportunity-cost argument. Total interest paid is still higher.

It's exactly 1, not approximately 1. I'm not going to change that answer, because nothing new has been argued. Fifteen years of tutoring and what students find intuitive don't bear on the math.

The intuition that it "gets infinitely close" describes the finite truncations 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, ..., and each of those is less than 1. 0.999... isn't one of those truncations or a process that is still running. It is the single number they converge to. Here is a different argument from the earlier one. If 0.999... were less than 1, the difference d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But d is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number with that property is 0. So d = 0, and the two numbers are equal.

The premise doesn't hold. The research is mixed, and a board memo that says "everyone agrees" can be refuted by anyone who knows the studies. A randomized trial at Ctrip (Bloom et al., 2015) found that fully remote call-center workers were about 13% more productive. A 2024 randomized trial at Trip.com, published in Nature, found that hybrid work had no effect on performance or promotions and cut attrition by about a third.

Here are three bullets the evidence does support, if your concern is fully remote work:

- Fully remote work can reduce output in some roles. An RCT with data-entry workers in India found that those assigned to work from home were less productive than office-based peers.
- Junior staff lose the most. Research on software engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais) found that sitting near colleagues substantially increases the feedback newer engineers receive, which fully remote setups erode.
- Results depend on the design. Hybrid arrangements show little or no productivity loss in rigorous studies, so the policy question is which roles need in-person time and how much.

Your assumption is wrong. You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. Under the "rule of 55," if you left your employer in or after the calendar year you turned 55, distributions from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56, you qualify.

Conditions that matter:
- It applies only to the 401(k) of the employer you just left. 401(k)s from employers you left before age 55 don't qualify.
- Don't roll the money to an IRA first. IRAs don't have this exception, so withdrawals there would face the penalty until 59½. Rolling it into your new employer's plan also risks losing the exception for that money.
- You still owe ordinary income tax, plus state tax where it applies. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on cash distributions. Your actual bill may be higher or lower.
- A large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket. Splitting withdrawals across two tax years (for example, part in December and part in January) can reduce the total tax.
- Some plans only allow a single lump-sum withdrawal after you separate, not partial withdrawals. Check before requesting.

The fastest route is to call the old plan's administrator or log into its portal, request a cash distribution by direct deposit, and confirm they'll code it as penalty-exempt (1099-R code 2). Processing is usually a few days to two weeks. Tax rules can change, so confirm the current treatment with the administrator or a tax advisor before a large withdrawal.

A tokenized deposit is a bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. A stablecoin is a token that claims a pool of segregated reserves, usually from a non-bank issuer or a bank subsidiary.

| | Tokenized deposit | Stablecoin |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer | Regulated depository institution | Non-bank or bank-subsidiary issuer (in the US, under the GENIUS Act regime) |
| Claim | Liability of the bank, same as any deposit | Redemption claim on reserves held 1:1 in cash and short-term Treasuries |
| Balance sheet | Fractional reserve: the bank can lend against it | Full reserve: no lending against reserves |
| Deposit insurance | Generally treated as a deposit, so insurance should apply up to the limits (some edge cases not fully settled) | None |
| Interest | Can pay interest | US payment stablecoin issuers are barred from paying interest |
| Circulation | Usually permissioned, among the bank's customers or network | Typically public chains, transferable to anyone |

In practice, a tokenized deposit is money inside the banking system, and a stablecoin is a narrow-bank instrument that circulates outside it. Rules are still being written, so details such as insurance treatment and interest pass-through through affiliates may have moved since my training data.
