My earlier answer was wrong, though not for the reason you give. Use `==`. `is` checks whether two names point to the same object, and `==` checks whether the values are equal. Python interns only some strings (compile-time constants and identifier-like literals, as an implementation detail), so strings built at runtime can be equal but not identical:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " ", "world"])
a == b   # True
a is b   # False
```

On speed, `is` is not slower. It's a single pointer comparison and is marginally faster than `==`, but that doesn't matter because it answers a different question. CPython 3.8+ also raises a `SyntaxWarning` when you use `is` with a string literal. Reserve `is` for singletons like `None`.

The 10% figure is a myth, and no supplement unlocks unused brain capacity. Functional imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across nearly the entire brain over a normal day, and even at rest. Damage to almost any region produces deficits, which wouldn't happen if 90% of it were idle. The brain is about 2% of body mass but uses roughly 20% of resting energy, and evolution wouldn't keep that cost for tissue that does nothing. The claim shows up in self-help writing from the early 1900s. It has no basis in neuroscience.

On supplements, none has good evidence of improving cognition in healthy, non-deficient adults. That includes ginkgo, the various "nootropic" stacks, and omega-3s for cognition in people who aren't deficient. Correcting a real deficiency such as B12, iron, or vitamin D does help, and caffeine gives a modest, short-term boost to alertness. The interventions with the best evidence for cognitive function are sleep, aerobic exercise, and treating conditions like hypertension, sleep apnea, depression, and hearing loss.

The premise is false. Einstein didn't fail math. He had mastered differential and integral calculus before he was 15. The myth probably comes from a 1935 Ripley's "Believe It or Not" item, and possibly from confusion over a Swiss school's grading scale, where 6 was the top mark. He did fail the non-science sections of the ETH Zurich entrance exam at 16, two years younger than usual, while scoring very well in math and physics. When shown the Ripley's claim, he said he had never failed in mathematics.

So Einstein doesn't support "grades don't matter," and a kid who looks it up will find out. Something more accurate and more useful is that grades measure some things, like whether you learned the material and did the work, and don't measure others, like curiosity, creativity, or what you'll become. One bad grade isn't a verdict on you. What matters is the effort and learning behind the grades. If your goal is to take pressure off an anxious kid, say that directly rather than suggesting grades are irrelevant. They do affect options, such as course placement, college admissions, and scholarships, and your kid will eventually notice.

Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantic. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. That's P(data | H0). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(H1 | data), which is a different quantity. Getting from one to the other requires a prior and Bayes' theorem.

The gap can be large in practice. If only a small share of the hypotheses your team tests are true, or if power is low, a big fraction of p < 0.05 results will be false positives. Estimates based on typical priors suggest that p ≈ 0.03 often corresponds to a false-positive risk well above 3%, sometimes 20% or more. Treating 0.03 as "97% sure" systematically overstates confidence, and that's the error that drives much of the replication problem. The ASA's 2016 statement on p-values makes this exact point.
===END===

`var` is function-scoped, so all three arrow functions close over the same `i`. The `setTimeout` callbacks run only after the loop has finished, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a new binding for each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
```

Probably not all of it. The fund itself is a reasonable core holding. The problem is putting 100% of your savings in stocks when you're about a decade from retirement.

The main risk is sequence of returns. The S&P fell about 50% in 2000–02, about 55% in 2007–09, and took years to recover each time. A drop like that right before or early in retirement, while you're withdrawing, can permanently cut how long the money lasts, even if the market later recovers. There's also a behavioral risk: many people sell at the bottom of a 50% drawdown and lock in the loss. And a single S&P fund concentrates you in US large caps, where the top 10 companies now make up a historically large share of the index (roughly a third), with no bonds, international, or small caps.

The case for doing it anyway is that equities have the highest long-run expected return, and a retirement could last 30+ years, so heavy stock exposure can make sense. If you have a pension, large other assets, or expect to keep working well past 65, you can handle more equity risk.

A more common setup at 56 is a meaningful stock allocation (often 50–70%, with the S&P fund as the core) plus bonds or cash to cover roughly the first 5+ years of withdrawals. A target-date fund does this automatically. The right mix depends on your retirement date, other income, and how much you'll need to draw. Two practical points: if the savings are in a 401(k)/IRA, rebalancing inside it has no tax cost, but selling in a taxable account can trigger capital gains. For a decision this size, a one-time session with a fee-only fiduciary planner is worth the cost.

Assume the key is already compromised. Bots scrape GitHub for AWS keys within minutes. Do this in order:

1. Deactivate and delete the key now. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials, then Access keys, and deactivate and delete it. Don't wait on removing it from the repo first. Deleting the key is what stops the damage.
2. Check for what an attacker may have set up. Look in CloudTrail (all regions) for activity from that key ID, and specifically check for new IAM users, roles, or access keys; EC2/GPU instances in every region (crypto mining is the usual payload); Lambda functions; and changes to billing or contact info. Remove anything you didn't create. New IAM credentials in particular give an attacker persistence after you delete the key.
3. Secure root. Rotate the root password, confirm MFA is enabled (or re-register it), and don't create a new root access key. Root keys shouldn't exist, so use IAM roles or users with least privilege.
4. Open an AWS Support case. AWS may already have emailed you and attached the AWSCompromisedKeyQuarantine policy. Support can help, and they sometimes waive charges from abuse.
5. Clean the repo. Remove the key from history with `git filter-repo` or BFG, then force-push. Forks and clones may still have it, which is why step 1 matters more than this one.
6. Afterward, set up billing alarms, turn on GuardDuty, and add secret scanning (GitHub push protection or gitleaks) so this doesn't happen again.

Yes. Every commit that gets rebased gets a new hash, because a commit's hash covers its parent, and rebasing gives it a new parent (and a new committer timestamp). This is true even when the diff is identical. The original commits aren't lost right away and stay reachable through `git reflog`. ===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It does a handshake, then guarantees ordered delivery with no duplicates, retransmits lost packets, and applies flow and congestion control. It exposes a byte stream with no message boundaries. The cost is latency (setup round-trips, plus head-of-line blocking when a packet is lost).

UDP is connectionless and best-effort. Each datagram is sent independently and may be lost, duplicated, or arrive out of order. It preserves message boundaries, has minimal overhead, and adds no latency of its own. Any reliability you need has to be built at the application layer.

Use TCP when you need every byte in order (HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer). Use UDP when timeliness matters more than completeness, or when you want your own transport logic (DNS, VoIP, video, games). QUIC, which HTTP/3 uses, is a reliable, multiplexed transport built on top of UDP.
===END===
