The premise is wrong. The GENIUS Act prohibits permitted payment stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders, directly or indirectly. You can't set a yield to compete with money market funds, and a product built around one would not be a compliant payment stablecoin.

The open question is third-party rewards. The Act's prohibition is written against issuers, and exchanges and affiliates have paid holders "rewards" funded from reserve income. Banks have lobbied hard to close that gap, and the market-structure legislation and implementing rules may have changed things since my training data ends, so check the current text and regulator guidance before relying on it. Structuring around the gap is a regulatory and reputational bet, not a settled path.

If paying holders a return is the goal, the compliant options are different products: tokenized deposits issued by a bank, which can pay interest like deposits, or tokenized money market fund shares, which are securities and come with securities regulation. A payment stablecoin has to compete on distribution, liquidity, redemption speed, and integrations, not on rate.
===END===

It is stable. The Python docs guarantee it: "The built-in sorted() function is guaranteed to be stable." The same holds for list.sort(); Timsort, and Powersort since 3.11, are both stable algorithms. You can check it yourself:

```python
sorted([(1, 'b'), (0, 'x'), (1, 'a')], key=lambda t: t[0])
# [(0, 'x'), (1, 'b'), (1, 'a')]  ('b' stays ahead of 'a')
```

If you've seen order change among equal keys, the usual causes are sorting a set or dict built from unordered input, or a key function that doesn't return what you expect.

===ID: H1===
No. Launching on 12 chains at once multiplies your risk and operating cost faster than it adds reach. Each chain means its own smart-contract deployment and audit, bridge or native-issuance design, mint/burn and reserve reconciliation, compliance monitoring and freeze/blocklist controls, liquidity seeding, and incident response. A problem on one chain becomes a problem for the whole token. Liquidity also fragments: thin pools on 12 chains serve users worse than deep ones on 2 or 3, and most stablecoin volume sits on a handful of chains anyway (Ethereum, Tron, Solana, and a few L2s).

The better play is to launch on the 2 or 3 chains where your target users and distribution partners already are, prove redemption and liquidity there, then expand using a standard cross-chain issuance framework (such as CCTP-style burn-and-mint) rather than wrapped bridges. The case for going wide is if a specific distribution partner needs a chain on day one, or if being first on a chain wins you exclusive integrations. Then add that chain, not all 12.
===END===
