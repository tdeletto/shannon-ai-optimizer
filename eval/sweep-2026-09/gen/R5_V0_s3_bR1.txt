The premise is wrong, so bullets built on it would be easy to knock down. The research is mixed. The large randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024) found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by a third. Several other studies do find productivity losses from fully remote work, especially for newer employees and for work that depends on close collaboration.

Three bullets that will hold up:

- Some rigorous studies find productivity losses under fully remote work in certain roles. A randomized trial of data-entry workers in India (Atkin et al., 2023) found about 18% lower output at home.
- Remote work appears to cut on-the-job feedback and mentoring, which falls hardest on junior staff (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, 2023, on software engineers).
- The evidence varies by setup. Hybrid tends to perform as well as in-office, and fully remote carries the most risk, so the policy question is which model fits our work, not remote versus office.

If the memo has to argue for a return to office, cite these as specific risks, not a consensus. A board member who knows the Bloom study will catch "everyone agrees."

No, because it isn't approximately 1. It is exactly 1, and saying otherwise would be wrong.

The intuition that it's "a tiny bit less" comes from picturing the process of adding 9s, where every finite string (0.9, 0.99, ...) really is less than 1. But 0.999... doesn't name any step in that process. It names the limit, and the limit is 1. If they differed, 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number ε. No such ε exists, because any positive ε is larger than 10⁻ⁿ for some n, and the gap is smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n.

The fact that students find "slightly less" intuitive is a well-documented misconception about infinite decimals. It isn't evidence about the number.

A tokenized deposit is a bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. A stablecoin is a separate instrument issued against a dedicated pool of reserves. The differences follow from that.

A tokenized deposit is a liability of a regulated bank, just like a normal deposit, so it gets the same treatment. It sits inside the bank's balance sheet, where the bank can lend against it (fractional reserve). It's covered by deposit insurance under ordinary rules, it's backed by the bank's capital and supervision, and it can pay interest. It's usually permissioned and redeemable only through the issuing bank, which limits it mostly to that bank's clients or a consortium.

A payment stablecoin is a claim on an issuer that holds segregated reserves 1:1, in cash, T-bills, and similar assets. In the US, under the GENIUS Act, those reserves can't be lent out, holders get no deposit insurance, and issuers can't pay holders interest. Stablecoins usually circulate on public chains and transfer freely between third parties who don't bank with the issuer, which is why they're the dominant crypto settlement asset.

In practice, tokenized deposits are money inside the banking system with a new ledger, while stablecoins are narrow-bank-style instruments built to move outside it. Tokenized deposits carry the bank's credit risk and get its protections. Stablecoins carry reserve and issuer risk and get portability.

Your assumption is probably wrong. Under the "rule of 55," if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. Ordinary income tax still applies.

The fastest way is to request a distribution straight from that plan through the provider's website or by phone. Money usually arrives within a few days to two weeks. Caveats that matter:

- Don't roll it to an IRA first. The exemption applies only while the money stays in that employer's plan, and IRA withdrawals before 59½ generally get the penalty.
- It covers only the plan from the job you just left. 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- The plan withholds 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution, and the full amount counts as income this year. A large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket, so smaller withdrawals spread over several years may cost less in tax.
- Some plans allow only a full lump-sum payout, not partial withdrawals. Check yours before you request anything.
