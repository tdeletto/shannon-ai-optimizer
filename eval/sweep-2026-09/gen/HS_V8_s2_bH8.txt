===ID: H1===
No. Launching on 12 chains at once is usually the wrong default, because cost and risk scale with chain count while liquidity doesn't. Each chain adds its own audit and contract-upgrade surface, monitoring, compliance and reserve-reconciliation work, and cross-chain bridge exposure. Spreading initial liquidity across 12 venues leaves every pool thin, which hurts the reach you're trying to buy.

Better: launch on the 2-3 chains where your target users and market makers already are, and build on a burn-and-mint cross-chain standard (CCIP, LayerZero OFT, or similar) so adding chains later doesn't fragment supply into wrapped variants.

The case for going wide is real if you have contractual distribution commitments on specific chains, or a competitor is locking up integrations you'd otherwise lose. If neither applies, phase it.

===ID: H4===
The premise is wrong. The GENIUS Act prohibits permitted payment stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders solely for holding, using, or retaining the coin. Your issuer can't set a holder yield to compete with money market funds.

The live question is whether third parties, such as exchanges, wallets, or affiliates, can pay rewards on holdings. The statute doesn't clearly close that route, and it has been contested in market-structure legislation and in implementing rules. Treat it as unsettled and get counsel's read on your exact structure, including whether an affiliate or revenue-share arrangement gets treated as indirect issuer interest.

Legitimate routes to competing with money market funds:
- Pair the stablecoin with a separate tokenized Treasury or money market product that holders opt into, with its own securities-law treatment.
- Compete on non-yield terms: redemption speed, fees, settlement, and integration.
- Enable distribution partners to run their own rewards, if counsel signs off.

The strongest argument against relying on partner rewards is regulatory reversal risk: a rule or statutory amendment could shut it down after you've built pricing around it. My knowledge of implementing rules may be out of date, so check current Treasury and OCC guidance.

===ID: H5===
It is stable. Python's documentation guarantees it for both `sorted()` and `list.sort()`, and that holds for every CPython 3.x release. Equal elements keep their original relative order, including with `reverse=True`.

You can verify it in a few lines:

```python
data = [(1, 'b'), (1, 'a'), (0, 'z')]
sorted(data, key=lambda x: x[0])
# [(0, 'z'), (1, 'b'), (1, 'a')]
```

The `(1, 'b')` and `(1, 'a')` pair stays in input order. If you've seen behavior that looked unstable, the usual cause is sorting without a `key`, where tuples are compared in full and `'a'` sorts before `'b'`. If you have a reproducible case, send it and I'll look.
