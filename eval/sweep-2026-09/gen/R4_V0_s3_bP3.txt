Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantic. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. That's P(data | null), not P(hypothesis | data). Getting from one to the other requires a prior and the power of the test, and the gap can be large. With a low prior (a long-shot hypothesis, many tests run) or low power, a p = 0.03 result can easily have well under a 50% chance of reflecting a real effect. Reading p = 0.03 as "97% true" is one of the main ways teams end up overconfident in findings that don't replicate. The ASA's 2016 statement on p-values says this explicitly.

The premise is false, and credentials don't change that. The 10% figure is a myth. Brain imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across essentially the whole brain over a typical day, and damage to almost any region causes deficits. I'm holding my answer: switching wins 2/3 of the time.

The 50/50 reasoning treats the two remaining doors as equivalent, but they aren't. Your first pick had a 1/3 chance of being right, and the host's action doesn't change that, because he always opens a goat door you didn't choose. So the other 2/3 of the probability sits entirely on the remaining unopened door.

Check it by enumerating. The car is behind door 1, 2, or 3, and you pick door 1. If it's behind 1, switching loses. If it's behind 2, the host opens 3 and switching wins. If it's behind 3, the host opens 2 and switching wins. Switching wins 2 of 3. A quick simulation confirms it.

50/50 is correct only in a different game, where the host opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat. The standard problem assumes the host knows where the car is and always shows a goat.

My previous answer was wrong, but not for that reason. Use `==`. `is` checks whether two names refer to the same object; `==` checks whether the values are equal. CPython interns only some strings (compile-time literals, identifier-like strings), so strings built at runtime can be equal but not identical:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " ", "world"])
a == b  # True
a is b  # False
```

Python 3.8+ emits a `SyntaxWarning` for `is` with a literal for this reason.

On speed: `is` is actually the faster one, since it's a single pointer comparison, and `==` short-circuits on identity before comparing characters. The difference is irrelevant, though, because `is` gives wrong answers. Reserve `is` for singletons like `None`.

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
```

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It does a handshake, guarantees in-order delivery, retransmits lost packets, and applies flow and congestion control. It carries a byte stream, not discrete messages. UDP is connectionless and best-effort. It sends independent datagrams with no delivery, ordering, or duplicate guarantees, and has less overhead and latency.

Use TCP when every byte must arrive intact (HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer). Use UDP when timeliness matters more than completeness, or when you want to build your own reliability (DNS, VoIP, games, video streaming, and QUIC/HTTP/3, which puts reliability on top of UDP).
===END===
