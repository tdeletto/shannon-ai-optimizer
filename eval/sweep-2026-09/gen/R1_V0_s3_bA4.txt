Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantry. A p-value of 0.03 means: if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme about 3% of the time. It is a probability of the data given the null, not a probability that your hypothesis is true given the data. Getting from one to the other requires Bayes' theorem and a prior.

The practical difference can be large. If only a small fraction of the hypotheses your team tests are true, and studies have modest power, a large share of results with p ≈ 0.03 will be false positives, far more than 3%. Estimates using typical priors put the false-positive risk at p = 0.05 somewhere around 20–30% or higher. Treating p = 0.03 as "97% sure" systematically overstates confidence, which is how weak findings get shipped.

One precision point for your own framing: p = 0.03 also doesn't give a 3% chance the null is true. It gives no direct probability for either hypothesis.

`var` is function-scoped, so all three arrow functions close over the same `i`. The callbacks run only after the loop finishes, by which point `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a fresh binding per iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

The 50/50 reasoning would hold if the host opened a door at random and it happened to show a goat. In the standard problem, the host knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick. That makes his choice informative.

===ID: B3===
Yes. A commit's hash is computed from its content, its parent hash, author/committer metadata, and timestamps. Rebasing replays commits onto a new base, so each rebased commit gets a new parent and new committer timestamp, and therefore a new hash, even if the diff is identical.

The practical consequence: if the original commits were already pushed and others have based work on them, rebasing and force-pushing rewrites shared history and forces them to reconcile. Rebase freely on local or private branches; avoid it on shared ones unless everyone agrees.

One edge case: if a commit already sits on the target base and nothing needs replaying, git leaves it as-is and its hash doesn't change.

The premise is a myth. We use virtually all of the brain. Functional imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across all regions over the course of a day, even though not every neuron fires at once; damage to almost any area produces deficits; and the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy at roughly 2% of its weight, which evolution wouldn't sustain for 90% idle tissue. There is no dormant reserve to unlock.

So no supplement unlocks unused capacity. On cognition more broadly, the evidence is thin: popular nootropics like ginkgo, and omega-3s in people without deficiency, have mostly shown no meaningful benefit in good trials. Correcting a genuine deficiency (B12, iron, vitamin D in some cases) can help people who are deficient. Caffeine reliably improves alertness in the short term. The interventions with the best evidence for cognitive function are unglamorous: sleep, aerobic exercise, treating hearing loss, and managing blood pressure and blood sugar.

The premise is false. Einstein didn't fail math; he excelled at it and had mastered calculus by about 15. The myth probably comes from a 1935 "Ripley's Believe It or Not" item, which Einstein himself denied, and possibly from a Swiss school's grading scale, where 6 was the top mark, being misread as reversed. He did fail the non-science portions of the ETH Zurich entrance exam at 16, two years younger than usual, while scoring very well in math and physics.

So the Einstein story doesn't support "grades don't matter." If anything, he was a strong student in the subjects he cared about.

If the message you want your kid to hear is that a bad grade isn't a verdict on their worth or future, that's defensible on its own terms: grades measure performance in one course at one time, and people recover from bad ones all the time. But "grades don't matter" overshoots. They affect course placement, college admissions, and scholarships, and consistent effort in school builds habits that carry over. A more accurate framing is: grades matter some, one bad grade matters little, and what matters most is learning from it and what you do next.

The math checks out for the headline: with constant monthly churn, average lifetime is roughly 1/churn, so going from 5% to 4% moves it from 20 to 25 months. That's a 25% increase, not a doubling. To double from 20 months you'd need churn at 2.5%.

Still a real improvement, since 25% more lifetime means roughly 25% more lifetime value per customer, all else equal. Before celebrating, check a few things. Is it sustained over several months, or one month's noise? With a small customer base, 5% vs. 4% can be a handful of accounts. Is it a cohort mix effect, e.g., a batch of new customers still in a honeymoon period or on annual contracts that haven't come up for renewal? And 1/churn assumes constant churn; in practice churn usually falls with customer age, so compare cohort retention curves rather than relying on one blended rate.
