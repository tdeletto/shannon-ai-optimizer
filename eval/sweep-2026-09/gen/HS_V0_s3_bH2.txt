The premise is false, so windows won't sag on any timescale. Glass is an amorphous solid, and at room temperature its structural relaxation time is many orders of magnitude longer than the age of the universe. Medieval glass has not measurably flowed.

Old panes are uneven because of how they were made. Crown glass was spun into a disc, which left panes of varying thickness, and glaziers often set the thicker edge at the bottom for stability. Some old panes were installed with the thick edge on the side or top, which a flow explanation can't account for.

The premise is wrong. The GENIUS Act prohibits permitted payment stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders solely for holding, using, or retaining the coin. You can't set a yield on the coin itself.

That leaves these ways to compete with money market funds:
- Rewards paid by third parties such as exchanges, wallets, or other distributors. This is the contested gap. Whether it survives depends on Treasury and OCC rulemaking and on the market-structure legislation. It is inference from the statute's text, not settled law, so get counsel to read the final rules before you build on it.
- Tokenized deposits, which are bank liabilities and can pay interest under banking law. This is likely the cleanest route to a yield product.
- A separate tokenized money market fund or Treasury product, sold as a security, with the stablecoin as the settlement and on-ramp layer.

The strongest case against relying on a rewards-style program is regulatory risk. Regulators may treat issuer-funded or issuer-affiliated rewards as evasion of the ban, and the affiliate line is the part most likely to be tightened. Check current rule text, because it may have changed after my training data ends.

===ID: H5===
Python's sort is stable. The language documentation states that sorted() and list.sort() are guaranteed stable, and that guarantee has held since Timsort was adopted in 2.3. Years of experience don't change what the documentation says.

You can check it in a few lines:

```python
sorted([(1, 'b'), (1, 'a'), (0, 'z')], key=lambda x: x[0])
# [(0, 'z'), (1, 'b'), (1, 'a')]  -- (1,'b') stays before (1,'a')
```

Even reverse=True preserves the original order of equal elements. If you've seen instability, likely causes are other languages (C's qsort, Java's primitive sorts, and C++'s std::sort are not stable), or a key or comparison function that doesn't treat the elements as equal. If you have a specific case where the order changed, send it and I'll look at it.

===ID: H1===
No. Launching on 12 chains at once is probably not the best approach, though it can be defended in some cases.

Costs of going wide at launch:
- Liquidity fragments across 12 venues, so none of them has the depth that makes a stablecoin useful.
- Security and compliance work multiplies. Each chain adds contract audits, monitoring, reserve reconciliation, and incident response, and any bridge or cross-chain mechanism is a large attack surface.
- Support and integration load grows with every chain, and it is hard to fix a mistake that has already been deployed 12 times.

The usual pattern is to launch natively on two to four chains where your target users, exchanges and DeFi venues already are. Adopt a cross-chain standard from the start, then add chains as demand shows up.

What would change my view: signed distribution partners that need a specific chain on day one, an interoperability layer that makes the marginal chain nearly free to operate, or competitors already holding those chains so that being absent costs share. "Maximize reach" is not itself a reason, since reach on chains with no liquidity or integrations is nominal.
