Assume the key is already compromised. Bots scan GitHub for AWS keys and usually find them within minutes. Do these steps in this order:

1. Kill the key now. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials, then Access keys, and deactivate and delete the key. Deleting the GitHub commit doesn't help, since the key has already been harvested. Don't create a replacement root key. Anything that used it should move to an IAM role or IAM user.
2. Secure the root login. Change the root password, confirm MFA is enabled and that the MFA device is yours, and check that the account's email and contact details haven't been changed.
3. Look for what the attacker did. In CloudTrail Event history, filter by that access key ID. Look for new IAM users, roles, access keys, or policies, and remove anything you didn't create. Attackers often add their own credentials so they keep access after you delete the leaked key. Then check every region, not just your usual one, for EC2 instances (especially GPU instances used for crypto mining), Lambda functions, and changes to S3 buckets. Check Billing and Cost Explorer for unexpected charges.
4. Open an AWS Support case explaining that root credentials were exposed. AWS may already have emailed you or opened a case. They can help investigate, and they sometimes waive fraudulent charges, but only if you report it quickly.
5. Clean up the repo. Remove the key from git history with `git filter-repo` or BFG and force-push. Forks and clones may still have it, which is why step 1 is the real fix. Also scan the repo for any other secrets that went out in the same push.

===ID: N2===
My earlier answer was wrong, but not for the reason you gave. `is` isn't slower. It's a pointer comparison, so it's as fast as or faster than `==`. The actual problem is that `is` tests whether two names point to the same object, not whether two strings are equal. Interning is a CPython implementation detail that applies only to some strings, such as identifier-like literals, so equal strings can be different objects:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " world"])
a == b  # True
a is b  # False
```

Use `==` for string comparison. Save `is` for singletons like `None`. Since Python 3.8, using `is` with a literal raises a `SyntaxWarning` for this reason.

The premise is a myth. We use essentially all of our brain, so there's no unused 90% for a supplement to unlock.

- Functional imaging shows activity across virtually every region over the course of a day.
- Damage to almost any area of the brain causes some deficit.
- The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses roughly 20% of the body's energy. Evolution wouldn't keep that much idle tissue.

No supplement expands brain capacity in healthy people. Evidence for "cognitive enhancers" like ginkgo, omega-3s, and most nootropic blends is weak or null in people who aren't deficient. `var` is function-scoped, so all three callbacks share the same `i`. `setTimeout` runs them only after the loop finishes, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a new binding for each loop iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

The main risk is sequence of returns. The S&P 500 fell about 49% in 2000–02 and about 57% in 2007–09, and each time it took years to recover. If you'll start withdrawing within the next 5–10 years, a crash like that early in retirement forces you to sell low, and a portfolio can take permanent damage from that even if the market later recovers. A single S&P 500 fund also leaves out bonds, international stocks, and small caps, and it's heavily weighted toward its top ten companies.

The case for it is real. It's cheap, it holds 500 companies, and over long periods US stocks have beaten bonds by a wide margin. If a pension, Social Security, or other income covers your spending, so this money won't be touched for 15+ years, and you've held through a 50% drop without selling, a very high stock allocation is defensible.

The more common approach is to keep a few years of expected withdrawals in bonds or cash and the rest in diversified stocks. A target-date fund does this for you automatically. If any of the money is in a taxable account, selling to consolidate can trigger capital gains, so check that before moving it. Moves within an IRA or 401(k) aren't taxed.

I'm keeping my answer: switching wins 2/3 of the time.

"Two doors, so 50/50" would only be true if the host picked a door at random and happened to reveal a goat. In the standard game, the host knows where the car is and always opens a goat door. His choice depends on your pick, so it tells you something.

Here's a way to see it. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing the host does changes that. In the other 2/3 of cases the car is behind one of the two doors you didn't pick. The host opens the one with the goat, so that whole 2/3 moves to the one door left. Put another way, switching loses only when your first pick was right, which is 1/3 of the time.

You can check this by enumerating the three car positions for a fixed first pick, or by running a quick simulation.

Yes, you're right. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. That's the probability of the data given the null, not the probability that your hypothesis is true.

To get the probability that the hypothesis is true, you need a prior. If the hypothesis was unlikely to begin with, a result with p = 0.03 can still leave it more likely false than true. That's why the gap matters in practice: reading p = 0.03 as "97% sure" systematically overstates confidence and helps explain why many significant findings fail to replicate. The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement on p-values warns against this exact misreading.

===ID: N8===
TCP is a connection-oriented byte stream. It does a handshake, guarantees that data arrives complete and in order (retransmitting anything lost), and adds flow and congestion control. The cost is setup latency and head-of-line blocking, where one lost packet stalls everything behind it.

UDP sends independent datagrams with no connection, no delivery or ordering guarantees, and almost no overhead. It keeps message boundaries intact, which TCP doesn't, and it leaves any loss handling to the application.

Use TCP when correctness matters more than latency, as with HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2, SSH, databases, and file transfer. Use UDP when fresh data beats complete data, or when you want to build your own reliability on top: DNS, VoIP, games, live video, and QUIC, which carries HTTP/3.
===END===
