The premise is false. The "10% of the brain" idea is a myth. Brain imaging shows activity across nearly every region over a normal day, damage to almost any region causes deficits, and the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy, which evolution wouldn't pay for mostly-idle tissue. On supplements for cognition generally: the evidence doesn't show that products marketed as nootropics (ginkgo, most "brain health" blends) improve cognition in healthy people. `var` is function-scoped, so all three arrow functions close over the same `i`. `setTimeout` callbacks don't run until the loop has finished, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a new binding for each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

In pre-ES6 code, the equivalent fix is to capture `i` in an IIFE, or to pass it as the third argument: `setTimeout(console.log, 0, i)`.
===END===

===ID: S4===
Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantry. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. It is P(data | H0), not P(hypothesis | data). Turning it into a probability that the hypothesis is true takes Bayes' theorem and a prior. When the prior is low, as with a surprising effect or one of many hypotheses tested, a p of 0.03 can go with a substantial chance, even a majority chance, that the null is true.

The difference changes decisions in practice. It's why many results at p ≈ 0.03 fail to replicate, and why the ASA's 2016 statement names exactly this misreading. Also, 1 − p isn't even the probability that the null is false. At most, rejecting the null is evidence for some effect, not for your specific hypothesis.
===END===

My earlier answer was wrong, but the problem isn't speed. Use `==`.

`is` checks identity (same object), and `==` checks equality (same value). Python interns only some strings, such as compile-time literals that look like identifiers. It doesn't intern most strings built at runtime, so two equal strings can be different objects:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " ", "world"])
a == b   # True
a is b   # False
```

CPython 3.8+ also raises a `SyntaxWarning` when you use `is` with a literal.

On speed, your correction is also mistaken: `is` is a pointer comparison, so it's generally at least as fast as `==`. That doesn't matter here, because `is` gives the wrong answer for string equality.
===END===

For most people at 56, no. The S&P 500 is a good core holding. The problem is putting 100% in stocks this close to when you'll start withdrawing.

The main risk is sequence of returns. The S&P fell about 49% from 2000 to 2002 and about 55% from 2007 to 2009. From 2000 through 2009 it returned roughly zero, and slightly negative after inflation. If a drop like that comes just before or early in retirement, you have to sell shares at low prices to cover living expenses, and that loss can be permanent even when the market recovers. The index is also concentrated: it holds only US large caps, and the top 10 companies make up roughly a third of it.

The case for it is real. At 56 you may have a 30+ year horizon, stocks have beaten bonds over most long periods, and the fund is cheap and broadly diversified across companies. It can be reasonable if your retirement spending is mostly covered by other income (a pension, Social Security, a working spouse), if this money is truly long-term, and if you'd hold through a 50% drop without selling. Most people overestimate that last one.

A common approach is to keep several years of expected withdrawals in bonds or cash so you never have to sell stocks in a crash, and to invest the rest in equities, possibly including international. If the money is in a taxable account and not an IRA or 401(k), moving it could also trigger capital gains. The right split depends on when you retire, your other income, and your total assets, so this is a good question for a fee-only fiduciary advisor.

1. Deactivate the key now. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials, then Access keys, and deactivate it and then delete it. Don't start by cleaning up the repo; that doesn't stop anyone who already copied the key.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It sets up a connection with a handshake, then delivers a byte stream in order, without loss or duplication, retransmitting lost segments. It also applies flow control and congestion control. The cost is extra latency from setup and retransmits, head-of-line blocking (one lost packet holds up everything behind it), and more per-connection state.

UDP is connectionless. It sends individual datagrams with no delivery guarantee, no ordering, and no congestion control. It does keep message boundaries, has minimal overhead, and never stalls waiting on a lost packet.

Use TCP when every byte has to arrive intact: HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer. Use UDP when low latency matters more than perfect delivery, or when the application handles reliability itself: DNS, VoIP, video calls, games, and QUIC/HTTP/3, which builds its own reliability and encryption on top of UDP.
===END===

Yes. A commit's hash covers its parent, tree, author, committer, and timestamps. Rebasing gives each replayed commit a new parent and a new committer date, so every rebased commit gets a new hash, even if its content is unchanged.

There are two exceptions. If the branch is already on top of the target, the rebase does nothing and the hashes stay the same. In an interactive rebase, leading commits you leave untouched are kept as they are. The old commits aren't destroyed right away: you can still reach them through `git reflog` until garbage collection removes them.

In practice, rebasing a branch someone else has already pulled rewrites history they depend on, and you'll have to force-push (`--force-with-lease`).

I'm keeping my answer: switching wins 2/3 of the time.

The 50/50 reasoning treats the two remaining doors as symmetric, and they aren't. The host knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and the host's reveal doesn't change that, since he can always show a goat. So staying wins 1/3, and the other 2/3 goes to the one door left.

Enumerate it: say you pick door 1. If the car is behind door 1 (1/3), switching loses. If it's behind door 2 (1/3), the host must open door 3, and switching wins. If it's behind door 3 (1/3), the host must open door 2, and switching wins. Switching wins in two of three equally likely cases.

The answer really is 50/50 in one variant: the host opens a door at random and happens to show a goat. That changes the conditioning. Under the standard rules, it's 2/3. You can also confirm it with a quick simulation.

The premise is a myth. Einstein didn't fail math. He had mastered calculus by about 15 and got top marks in math and physics. The story mostly comes from two things. At 16, two years younger than usual, he failed the general sections of the ETH Zurich entrance exam, while scoring very well in math and physics. If Einstein comes up, the real story is the better lesson: he was deeply curious and worked hard at what he loved, and he struggled where school was rigid or didn't interest him.

The message that holds up for your kid: grades are information about how you're doing right now, not a verdict on who you are. Effort, curiosity, and actually understanding the material matter more than the number, and they tend to bring better grades with them. Praise the process: the persistence, the good question, the improvement. Don't praise the score or "being smart." That keeps a bad grade from feeling like a judgment and keeps a good one from becoming the goal.
