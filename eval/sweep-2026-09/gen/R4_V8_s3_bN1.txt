No. It isn't approximately 1. It is exactly 1, and your years of tutoring don't change the math. Here is a different route, though. Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the gap d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But d is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, smaller than 0.001, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n, because 0.999... beats every finite string of 9s. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0.

Your intuition does fit the finite truncations. 0.9, 0.99 and 0.999 each fall short of 1, and each only gets close. The notation "0.999..." doesn't name any one of them. It names the limit, and the limit is 1.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't make it cheaper. You pay less each month but for twice as many months, and more of the principal stays outstanding for longer, so more interest builds up. For example, on $300,000 at 6%:

- 15-year: $2,532/month, about $156,000 in total interest
- 30-year: $1,799/month, about $348,000 in total interest

The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow or flexibility, or if you invest the payment difference at a return above the mortgage rate. None of that changes which one costs more in interest. In practice, 15-year loans usually carry a lower rate too, which makes the gap even wider.

===ID: B5===
The difference is who issues it and what legal claim the holder gets.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. The issuer is a regulated depository bank, and the token is that bank's deposit liability. It sits inside the bank's balance sheet: fractional reserve, capital rules, access to the Fed, and deposit insurance treatment on the same terms as the underlying deposit. It can pay interest. Because it's a claim on one specific bank, it usually circulates within permissioned networks or among that bank's customers, which limits how widely it can be used across the ecosystem.

A stablecoin is a bearer-style token issued by a firm that holds dedicated reserves of cash, T-bills and repo 1:1 against the tokens outstanding. The issuer is often a nonbank. The holder's claim is against the issuer and its segregated reserves, and there's no deposit insurance. Under the GENIUS Act, payment stablecoins must be fully reserved and the issuer can't pay interest to holders. Stablecoins circulate freely on public chains, so they're more portable and composable, but money held in them doesn't create credit.

In short, a tokenized deposit is bank money on-chain, with interest and insurance but limited portability. A stablecoin is narrow-bank-style money, fully backed and portable but paying no interest. Rules are still changing, particularly GENIUS Act implementing regulations and how deposit insurance applies to tokenized deposits, so the details may have moved since my training data.
===END===

===ID: F6===
You probably won't pay the 10% penalty. Under the Rule of 55, if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are penalty-free. They're still taxed as ordinary income.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator, or use its website, and request a distribution straight from the 401(k). Expect a 20% mandatory federal withholding on a lump sum. What that withholding covers toward your actual tax bill gets settled when you file. Money usually arrives within about 1–2 weeks.

Three things could cost you:
- Don't roll it into an IRA first. The Rule of 55 exception applies only to the 401(k), and IRA withdrawals before 59½ generally get the 10% penalty.
- It only covers the plan from the job you just left. 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- A large lump sum in one year can push you into a much higher tax bracket. If you don't need all of it now, splitting withdrawals across tax years can save a lot. Also check whether your plan allows partial withdrawals, because some only allow a lump sum.
===END===
