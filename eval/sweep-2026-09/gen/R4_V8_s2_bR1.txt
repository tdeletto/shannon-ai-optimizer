The difference is whose liability it is and what backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It is a liability of the issuing bank and sits on the bank's balance sheet, so it is backed the way any deposit is: by the bank's capital, its lending book under fractional reserve, and deposit insurance up to the limit. It can pay interest. Holders are generally the bank's own KYC'd customers, and transfers usually happen on permissioned rails or between participating banks.

A payment stablecoin is a liability of an issuer, often a nonbank, that holds reserves segregated 1:1 against the tokens outstanding, typically in cash, T-bills, and repo. Under the GENIUS Act, reserves must be high-quality liquid assets, the issuer can't pay interest to holders, and there is no deposit insurance. The token moves as a bearer-like instrument on public chains, so it circulates among parties who have no relationship with the issuer.

In practice: a tokenized deposit is bank money that happens to be programmable, and it keeps credit creation and insurance but has limited reach. A stablecoin is a narrow-bank-style instrument with full reserve backing and broad transferability, but no insurance, no yield, and redemption risk that sits on the issuer and its reserve custodians.

No. It's exactly 1, and I won't call it approximately 1, because that would be false.

Here's a different angle. Assume 0.999... is less than 1. Then the gap d = 1 − 0.999... is some positive number. Any positive d, however small, is larger than 10⁻ⁿ for some n. But 0.999... is at least 0.99...9 with n nines, so the gap can be at most 10⁻ⁿ, which contradicts the assumption. The only nonnegative number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0, so d = 0.

The intuition your students have is real, but it describes the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, …, every term of which is less than 1. The notation 0.999... doesn't name any of those terms. It names their limit, and the limit is 1.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not the same as a lower total cost. Take $300k at 6%: the 30-year payment is about $1,799 a month and the total interest is about $348k, while the 15-year payment is about $2,532 a month and the total interest is about $156k. You pay less each month on the 30-year, but you pay for twice as many months, and more of each early payment goes to interest.

The one real case for the 30-year is cash-flow flexibility, or investing the monthly difference at a return above the mortgage rate. That's an opportunity-cost argument, not lower interest. In practice, 15-year rates are usually lower too, which widens the gap.

I'd push back on the premise first. Not everyone agrees, and a board member who knows the literature will catch it. The research is split. Bloom's randomized trial at Ctrip found fully remote call-center staff were about 13% more productive, and his 2024 Nature study of hybrid work at Trip.com found no productivity loss and lower attrition. The weakest results are for fully remote work, not remote work in general.

Here are three bullets you can defend:

- Remote work reduces the feedback and mentoring junior staff get. A study of software engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity") found that engineers who sat near teammates got substantially more code-review feedback, which matters most for newer hires.
- Company-wide remote work makes collaboration networks more siloed. Microsoft's study of 61,000 employees (Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2021) found cross-group collaboration fell and communication became more asynchronous.

Check the figures against the papers before the memo goes out. I'm citing them without looking them up. To make the case hold up in the room, frame it as "fully remote carries these costs" rather than "remote kills productivity."

You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. The Rule of 55 lets you take penalty-free withdrawals from the 401(k) of the employer you left, as long as you separated from service in or after the calendar year you turned 55. At 56, you qualify.

Three things decide whether you actually get this:

- Leave the money in that employer's plan. If you roll it to an IRA, the Rule of 55 no longer applies, and IRA withdrawals before 59½ carry the penalty.
- It only covers the plan from the job you just left. 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify, unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- Rolling this 401(k) into your new employer's plan also forfeits the exception.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan administrator, which usually means a form or an online request and a check or ACH within a week or two. Check whether the plan allows partial withdrawals. Some plans only allow a full lump sum, which could push a lot of income into one tax year. Ordinary income tax still applies, and the plan must withhold 20% federal on a direct distribution, so you may owe more or get some back at filing depending on your bracket.

No. Being debt-free isn't always the smartest move, and this plan leaves you exposed.

At 3.1%, the mortgage is cheap debt. Cash in a high-yield account or T-bills has recently earned about as much or more, though rates may have changed. Paying it off earns you a guaranteed 3.1%, but you move almost all your liquid money into home equity, which you can't spend. If you lose your job, have a medical bill, or need a major repair, $5k won't last long. Borrowing against the house works best when you don't need it: banks tighten and freeze HELOCs in downturns, and they won't lend to someone without income. Also, the payoff doesn't lower your property taxes or insurance, so your fixed costs don't go to zero.

If you want the debt gone, keep 6–12 months of expenses liquid and put only the excess toward the mortgage.

The strongest case for your plan is that it's a guaranteed, risk-free return and it gives real peace of mind. That case holds up if you have other substantial liquid assets outside this $200k, like a taxable brokerage account, and stable income. Without those, I wouldn't do it.
