Yes, you're right, and the difference is more than pedantic. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. It is P(data | H0), not P(H0 | data), and it says nothing directly about the probability your hypothesis is true.

Getting from one to the other requires Bayes' theorem and a prior. When the hypothesis was unlikely to begin with, or the study is underpowered, a p of 0.03 can still leave a high chance the effect isn't real; calibration estimates (e.g., Sellke, Bayarri & Berger) put the false-positive risk at p ≈ 0.03 in the ~20–30% range even with a 50/50 prior. The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement on p-values lists this exact misreading as a core error. It matters in practice because it's why "significant" findings fail to replicate at the rates they do.
===END===

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable: a handshake sets up the connection, and it guarantees delivery, in order, without duplicates, using acknowledgments and retransmission, plus flow and congestion control. It's a byte stream with no message boundaries. UDP is connectionless and best-effort: each datagram is sent independently and may be lost, duplicated, or arrive out of order, with no retransmission or congestion control, but message boundaries are preserved and overhead and latency are lower.

Use TCP when correctness matters more than latency (HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer). Use UDP when late data is useless or you want to build your own reliability (DNS, VoIP, video, games, and QUIC, which HTTP/3 runs over).
===END===

My previous answer was wrong, but not for the reason you give. The real problem is correctness: `is` tests identity (same object), `==` tests equality (same value). Use `==` for strings. Interning is an implementation detail and applies only to some strings (literals, identifier-like strings); strings built at runtime, e.g. `"".join(["a", "b"])` or input read from a file, usually won't be the same object as an equal literal, so `is` returns False. Python 3.8+ even warns about `is` with a literal (SyntaxWarning).

On speed, your claim doesn't hold: `is` is a single pointer comparison and is not slower than `==`. It's irrelevant, though, because `is` gives wrong answers.

The premise is a myth, whatever anyone's credentials. Imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across essentially the whole brain over a day, even though not every region is active at once; there's no dormant 90%. Damage to almost any area produces deficits, and the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy, which wouldn't make evolutionary sense for a mostly idle organ.

So no supplement "unlocks" unused capacity. On cognition in general, the evidence for supplements in healthy people with no deficiency is weak. The exceptions are correcting real deficiencies (B12, iron, vitamin D) and caffeine, which modestly improves alertness. Sleep, exercise, and managing cardiovascular risk have much better evidence for brain function.

My answer stands: switch, and you win 2/3 of the time. Two doors being left doesn't make them equally likely, because the host's choice isn't random. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick.

Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing the host does changes that. In the other 2/3 of cases the car is behind one of the two doors you didn't pick, and the host eliminates the goat among them, so the remaining door gets that whole 2/3.
