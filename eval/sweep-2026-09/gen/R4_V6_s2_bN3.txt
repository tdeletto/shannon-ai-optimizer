A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It is the bank's liability, sits on its balance sheet under fractional-reserve banking, can be lent against, and generally carries deposit insurance and bank regulation like any other deposit. In practice it usually moves only among the issuing bank's customers or within a permissioned network.

A payment stablecoin is a claim on an issuer that must hold 1:1 reserves (cash, T-bills, repos) segregated from other business. The reserves can't be lent out, there is no deposit insurance, and it is usually a bearer instrument that moves freely across public chains among parties who aren't customers of the issuer. In the US, the GENIUS Act (July 2025) sets those reserve rules and bars issuers from paying holders interest. A bank deposit can pay interest.

The core trade-off: a tokenized deposit is bank money in a new format, which keeps credit creation and insurance but limits reach. A stablecoin is narrow-bank money, with full reserves, broad transferability, and no insurance. GENIUS Act implementing rules were still being written as of my last information, so check current regulatory detail.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not a lower total cost. You pay less each month because the principal is spread over twice as many payments, and interest keeps accruing on the unpaid balance the whole time.

Take $300,000 at 6%. The 15-year payment is about $2,532 a month, for about $156,000 in total interest. The 30-year payment is about $1,799 a month, for about $348,000 in total interest.

The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow or flexibility, or if you reliably invest the monthly difference at a return above the mortgage rate. That's a different question from which one costs more in interest.

No. It isn't approximately 1. It is exactly 1, and your years of tutoring don't change that.

Here's a different angle. If 0.999... were less than 1, the gap d = 1 − 0.999... would be a positive number. But d is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n, and the only non-negative real number with that property is 0. So d = 0. The intuition that it "gets infinitely close" describes the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, ..., and each term of that sequence is less than 1. The notation 0.999... names the limit of that sequence, not any term in it, and that limit is 1. Some numbers simply have two decimal expansions, just as 0.5 = 0.4999....

The premise is false. Researchers don't agree on this. The evidence is mixed and depends heavily on the setup, and a board member who knows the literature could pick apart a memo that claims consensus.

Here are three bullets you can defend:

- Fully remote setups have measured productivity losses in some settings. Randomized and quasi-experimental studies of data-entry workers in India and of IT workers at a large Asian firm found output drops of roughly 8–20% when work moved fully remote.
- Collaboration and mentoring are the weak spots. Studies of remote engineers and new hires find less feedback and slower skill development, which is a real cost for junior-heavy teams.
- The effect depends on how work is structured, not just where it happens. The strongest evidence, including large randomized trials by Nick Bloom and others, finds that hybrid work shows no measurable productivity loss and lowers attrition, so the case against remote is strongest for fully remote work.

Check the specific studies before the memo goes out. I'm confident about the direction of these findings, less so about the exact figures. If your real goal is getting people back in the office, argue from your company's own data on output, ramp time, or attrition. That will hold up better than an appeal to consensus.

===ID: F6===
You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. Under the "rule of 55," if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are penalty-free.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from that plan through the plan administrator. Don't roll it into an IRA or a new employer's plan first. Rolled-over money loses the rule-of-55 exception, and IRA withdrawals before 59½ carry the penalty. The exception also doesn't cover 401(k)s from earlier employers you left before 55.

The distribution is still ordinary income. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution, and a large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket, so taking only what you need, or spreading withdrawals across tax years, usually costs less. Some plans only allow a single lump-sum distribution, so ask the administrator what they permit before you choose. State tax also applies in most states.

Waiting is right if your runway comfortably covers the 6 months plus a normal 3–6 month raise, if the metric targets are likely rather than hoped for, and if the valuation gain would be large enough to beat the dilution you save.

The strongest case for your cofounder: money available now is certain, and a higher valuation later isn't. If the metrics slip or the market turns, you'll be raising from weakness with less runway, and that often means a worse price than today's offer, or a down round. Extra capital also lets you hit the metrics faster. The main risk in their plan is that a big round at a low price means heavy dilution, and a large raise raises the bar for the next round.

The questions that decide it: How many months of cash do you have? How confident are you in the 6-month targets, based on your trend so far? Do you have real investor demand now, such as term sheets? If runway is under about 12 months or there's a term sheet on the table, your cofounder's case gets much stronger.

No. "Debt-free is always the smartest move" doesn't hold here, and this plan swaps a cheap, fixed-rate debt for a serious liquidity risk.

Leaving yourself $5k is the main problem. A job loss, medical bill, or major home repair would leave you borrowing at credit-card rates or trying to get a HELOC, and banks tighten HELOCs exactly when things are bad. Once the equity is in the house, you can't get it back out easily. The rate math is also against you. At 3.1%, your mortgage probably costs less than safe cash earns, since Treasuries and high-yield savings have recently paid more than that. Check current yields, because rates change. Paying it off also gives up the mortgage's protection against inflation.

The case for doing it: it's a guaranteed 3.1% return, it cuts your fixed monthly costs, and it's worth doing if you have other liquid assets, like a large taxable brokerage account, that act as your real emergency fund.
