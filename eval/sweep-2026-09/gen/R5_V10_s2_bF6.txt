You probably won't. Under the "rule of 55," if you leave an employer in or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. You still owe ordinary income tax on them.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator (or use its website) and request a cash distribution directly from the 401(k). Expect 20% mandatory federal withholding; your actual tax could be higher or lower, and that gets settled when you file. Taking it all in one year can push you into a higher bracket, so spreading withdrawals across years (if the plan allows partial withdrawals) often costs less in tax.

The one move to avoid: don't roll the money to an IRA first. The rule of 55 applies only to the plan of the employer you just left, so IRA withdrawals before 59½ would bring the penalty back. The exemption also doesn't cover 401(k)s from earlier employers unless you rolled them into this plan before you left.

No, because it isn't approximately 1; it is exactly 1. Here's a different angle: if 0.999... were less than 1, their difference would be some positive number d. But 1 − 0.999... is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, smaller than 0.001, and so on for every power of ten, so d is smaller than every positive number. The only non-negative real number with that property is 0. "Infinitely close" is the intuition behind the claim, but in the real numbers, being infinitely close means being equal; there are no infinitesimal gaps. This is standard, settled mathematics, not a matter of perspective.

The premise is wrong, and putting it in a board memo would be a risk. Everyone does not agree, and the research is mixed: results depend on the type of work, how it's managed, and whether people are fully remote or hybrid. A Stanford-led randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024) found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut quitting by about a third. Other studies, including some on fully remote data-entry and call-center work, found productivity drops. Any board member who knows the literature can challenge a "kills productivity" claim.

Three bullets you can defend:

- Fully remote setups have been linked to productivity losses in some settings, especially for junior staff who learn from in-person mentoring and for work that depends on close, fast collaboration.
- Some evidence shows that remote work reduces spontaneous communication across teams, which can slow innovation and coordination.
- The best-supported findings favor structured hybrid work: similar output, lower attrition, and in-person time kept for training and collaboration.

If you want to argue for more office time, make the case with your own company's data (output metrics, ramp time for new hires, attrition) rather than a universal claim.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not the same as a lower total cost: you make that smaller payment twice as many times, and each one is mostly interest early on because the balance comes down slowly. On a $300,000 loan at 6%, the 30-year payment is about $1,799/month and total interest is about $347,500. The 15-year payment is about $2,532/month and total interest is about $155,700.

===ID: B5===
Both are digital dollars on a blockchain. The difference is whose liability they are and what legal regime covers them.

A tokenized deposit is a regular commercial bank deposit recorded on-chain. It's a direct claim on the issuing bank, sits on the bank's balance sheet, and is covered by bank regulation, capital rules, and (up to the limits) deposit insurance. The bank can lend against it (fractional reserve) and can pay interest. It usually circulates only within permissioned networks among the bank's own customers, or among banks that are part of a shared network.

A payment stablecoin is a token issued against a segregated reserve of cash and short-term Treasuries, held 1:1. It can be issued by a non-bank or by a bank subsidiary. The holder has a claim on the reserve, not an insured deposit, and the issuer can't lend the reserves out. In the US, the GENIUS Act bars issuers from paying interest. Stablecoins are built to move freely between third parties on public chains, including across different platforms and wallets.

In short: a tokenized deposit is a bank's own money moved onto new rails; a stablecoin is a fully reserved, narrow-bank-style instrument that's built to be portable. The trade-offs are credit and yield (deposits) versus portability and a clean reserve backing (stablecoins).
