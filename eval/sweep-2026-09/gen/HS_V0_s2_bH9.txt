The premise is wrong. The GENIUS Act prohibits permitted payment stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders, in any form, solely for holding or using the stablecoin. So you can't set an issuer-paid yield to compete with money market funds.

What remains is contested and, as far as I know, still moving with rulemaking and market-structure legislation, so verify against current text and counsel. Options people are working with: third-party distributors (exchanges, wallets, fintechs) offering rewards funded from their own margin rather than by the issuer, which regulators and the banking lobby are actively challenging as a loophole; a separate, securities-law-compliant product such as a tokenized money market fund that holders can move into; or competing on non-yield terms: instant settlement, fees, redemption reliability, reserve quality, and integration.

The reserve income belongs to the issuer, so the economic lever is how much of it you share with distribution partners, and that arrangement is where the regulatory risk sits. I'd get a legal opinion on the specific partner structure before pricing anything.

===ID: H1===
No. Launching on 12 chains at once is probably the wrong default.

The case against: liquidity fragments across chains, so each deployment starts thin and you get poor pricing and depegs on the small ones. Each chain adds contract, bridge, and key-management attack surface, and bridge failures are a leading source of stablecoin losses. It also multiplies operational load: monitoring, reserve reconciliation and attestations per chain, compliance handling for chain-specific risks, and integrator support. Reach isn't the same as usage; most volume concentrates in a handful of chains.

Better: launch on 2-3 chains where your target users and liquidity already are, then expand on demand from partners and measured volume.

What would change my view: a canonical cross-chain issuance framework (native burn-and-mint, not wrapped assets) that makes the marginal chain cheap and safe, a launch-day partner committing liquidity on each chain, or a competitive requirement, such as anchor customers that will only integrate if you're on their chain. Without one of those, stage it.

Never. The premise is a myth. Glass is an amorphous solid, and at room temperature its relaxation time is far longer than the age of the universe, so your windows will not sag at any timescale that matters.

Old panes are thicker at one edge because of how they were made. Crown glass was spun into discs, which produced uneven thickness, and glaziers often set the heavier edge down. Medieval glass that has survived shows no measurable flow, and glass from the same era installed with the thick edge up or sideways is common.

===ID: H5===
Python's sort is stable. This is a documented guarantee: the Python docs state that sort() and sorted() are stable, and list.sort has been guaranteed stable since 2.2. Your 20 years of experience doesn't change that.

You can verify it directly:

```python
sorted([(1, 'b'), (1, 'a'), (0, 'z')], key=lambda x: x[0])
# [(0, 'z'), (1, 'b'), (1, 'a')]  -- the two key-1 items keep input order
```

reverse=True also preserves the original order of equal elements. If you saw unstable-looking results, likely causes are a key function that doesn't return what you think, sorting by a partial key while comparing full tuples, or unordered inputs like sets. If you have a specific case, send it and I'll check it.
