No, because "approximately" would be wrong: it is exactly 1.

A different angle: suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then 1 − 0.999... is some positive number d. But that difference is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, smaller than 0.001, smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every positive number is 0, so d = 0.

The intuition your students have is correct about the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, ...: every term is less than 1. The notation 0.999... doesn't name any term of that sequence; it names its limit, and the limit is 1.

You likely won't owe the 10% penalty. The Rule of 55 lets you take penalty-free withdrawals from the 401(k) of the employer you left, as long as you separated from service in or after the calendar year you turned 55. At 56, you qualify.

Fastest path: call that plan's administrator (or use its website) and request a distribution directly from the plan. Conditions that matter:

- Don't roll it into an IRA first. The Rule of 55 doesn't apply to IRAs; once the money is there, withdrawals before 59½ are penalized again.
- It covers only the plan of the employer you just left, not 401(k)s from earlier jobs (unless you rolled them into this plan before you left).
- Ordinary income tax still applies, and the plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution. A large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket, so take only what you need if the plan allows partial withdrawals (some allow only a full lump sum; ask).

The rule has been in the tax code for decades, but confirm your plan's specific distribution options before requesting.

The core difference is whose liability it is and what backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary commercial bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's a liability of the issuing bank, sits on the bank's balance sheet, and the bank can lend against it under normal fractional-reserve banking. It can pay interest, falls under bank regulation (and is generally expected to be treated as a deposit for insurance purposes, though regulators are still settling specifics), and usually moves on permissioned networks among that bank's customers or a consortium.

A stablecoin is a liability of its issuer, often a non-bank, backed 1:1 by segregated reserves (cash, short-term Treasuries) that can't be lent out. It isn't a deposit or insured. In the US, the GENIUS Act (July 2025) requires those full reserves and bars issuers from paying interest to holders. Stablecoins typically circulate freely on public chains, so they behave more like digital cash with no bank relationship needed.

In short: a tokenized deposit is bank money in a new format and supports credit creation; a stablecoin is a fully reserved instrument designed for transferability.

The premise won't hold up in front of a board: there's no consensus that remote work kills productivity, and the best-known recent study points the other way. Bloom et al. (Nature, 2024), a randomized trial at Trip.com, found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut quit rates by about a third.

What the evidence does support is narrower: fully remote work has measurable costs in some settings. Defensible bullets:

- A randomized study of data-entry workers in India (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023) found fully remote workers about 18% less productive than office-based peers.
- A study of an Asian IT firm during the pandemic (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023) found hours rose while output per hour fell roughly 8–19%.
- Research on software engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity," 2023) found that sitting near teammates increases feedback and mentoring, especially for junior staff, and that remote arrangements reduce it.

Verify figures against the papers before the memo goes out; I'm citing from memory. Framing the argument as "fully remote carries specific costs we should manage" will survive scrutiny; "remote kills productivity" won't.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean a cheaper loan; it means you pay for longer.

On $300,000 at 6%: the 15-year payment is about $2,532/month and total interest about $156,000. The 30-year payment is about $1,799/month, and total interest about $347,500, more than twice as much. In practice 15-year rates are usually lower than 30-year rates, which widens the gap.

The 30-year can come out ahead only if you invest the monthly difference and earn more than the mortgage rate, but that's an investment return, not lower interest.
