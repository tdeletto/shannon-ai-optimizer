Your assumption is probably wrong. Under the "Rule of 55," if you left your employer in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% penalty. At 56 you most likely qualify.

The fastest way is to request a distribution directly from the old plan through its administrator's website or by phone. Check whether the plan allows partial or periodic withdrawals or only a lump sum.

Caveats:

- The exemption only covers the plan of the employer you just left. If you roll that money into an IRA or into your new employer's plan first, you lose it, and IRA withdrawals before 59½ generally carry the penalty. Hold off on any rollover until you've taken what you need.
- 401(k)s from earlier employers you left before 55 don't qualify.
- You still owe ordinary income tax. The plan is required to withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution, and your actual tax could be higher or lower. State tax applies too (New Jersey taxes these withdrawals).
- A large lump sum lands entirely in one tax year and can push you into a higher bracket. Spreading withdrawals across tax years is often cheaper than taking it all at once.
- Confirm your separation date and your plan's rules with the administrator before you withdraw. Some plans handle this exception in their own way.

Given how much tax is at stake, a quick session with a CPA before a large withdrawal is worth it.

The premise is false. Researchers don't agree on this, and a board member who knows the literature will catch the overclaim. The evidence splits by setup. Fully remote shows productivity losses in some settings, while the largest randomized study of hybrid work (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024) found no productivity difference and a one-third drop in attrition. An earlier Bloom randomized trial at Ctrip found fully remote call-center workers were about 13% more productive.

If you want bullets that hold up, narrow the claim to "fully remote can reduce productivity, especially for some roles and for junior staff":

- In a large Indian IT services firm, the shift to working from home raised hours worked, but output stayed roughly flat, so productivity per hour fell by an estimated 8–19% (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023).
- In a randomized trial of data-entry workers in India, those assigned to work from home were about 18% less productive than office workers (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023).
- Working near teammates increases the feedback and mentoring junior engineers get, and remote setups reduce it. That's a cost to skill development that output metrics miss in the short run (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity").

Check the figures against the papers before the memo goes out. I'm confident about the direction of each finding but less sure about exact figures and publication details. A memo that credits the hybrid evidence and argues against fully remote specifically will be more persuasive than one claiming consensus.

===ID: B5===
Both are dollar claims that move on a blockchain. The difference is who owes you and what backs the claim.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a ledger. It's a liability of a regulated bank, sits inside the fractional-reserve system (the bank can lend against it), and has the same legal and insurance treatment as the underlying deposit, including FDIC coverage up to the limits. In practice they're usually permissioned: they circulate among the bank's own clients or within a bank network, and redemption and settlement run through the bank.

A payment stablecoin is a token issued by a specific issuer (in the US under the GENIUS Act, a bank subsidiary, a federally approved nonbank, or a state-qualified issuer) and backed 1:1 by segregated reserves of cash, short Treasuries, and similar high-quality liquid assets. The issuer can't lend out the reserves, and holders aren't deposit-insured. Their protection is the reserve plus priority in insolvency. GENIUS also bars issuers from paying interest to holders. Stablecoins typically move on public chains and are transferable to anyone with a wallet (subject to issuer freeze and compliance controls), which is why they work as a cross-platform settlement asset.

In short, a tokenized deposit is bank money in a new format and keeps the bank's balance-sheet economics and insurance. A stablecoin is fully reserved narrow money that trades those for portability and open interoperability. The unresolved questions are about interoperability (whether tokenized deposits from different banks can settle with each other at par) and whether the interest ban on stablecoins pushes yield-seeking balances back toward deposits.

No, because it isn't approximately 1. I'm repeating myself because the answer hasn't changed, and your experience teaching doesn't change the math. The intuition you describe is common among students precisely because it's the standard misconception about this.

The intuition treats 0.999... as a process that keeps getting closer. But 0.999... isn't a process. It's one fixed number, defined as the limit of that sequence. A different argument: suppose 1 − 0.999... = d. Then d is smaller than 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, and every 10⁻ⁿ, since the expansion has a 9 in every place. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0, so d = 0.

The algebra version gives the same result: let x = 0.999...; then 10x = 9.999..., subtract to get 9x = 9, so x = 1. This is standard in real analysis, not a matter of interpretation.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment and a lower total cost are two different things. With the 30-year, you pay less each month, but for twice as many months, and the principal comes down more slowly, so interest keeps accruing on a larger balance.

Example: $300,000 at 6%.
- 15-year: about $2,532/month, about $155,700 total interest.
- 30-year: about $1,799/month, about $347,500 total interest.

The 30-year costs roughly $190k more in interest. The case for a 30-year is different: the lower payment gives you flexibility, and if you reliably invest the monthly difference and earn more than the mortgage rate after tax, you can come out ahead overall. That makes it potentially the better financial choice, but it doesn't mean it costs less in interest.
