The 30-year still costs more in total interest. The lower monthly payment doesn't make it cheaper overall.

The payment is lower because you're spreading the loan over twice as many months and paying principal down more slowly. You carry a higher balance for longer, so you pay more interest. For example, on $400,000 at 6%:

| | Monthly payment | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| 15-year | ~$3,375 | ~$208,000 |
| 30-year | ~$2,398 | ~$463,000 |

There is a real argument for the 30-year, but it's a different question. The lower payment gives you flexibility, and if you invest the difference at a return above the mortgage rate, you can come out ahead. That's a question about opportunity cost, not about which loan costs more in interest.

===ID: F6===
Your assumption is probably wrong. You likely won't owe the 10% penalty.

Under the "rule of 55," if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56, you qualify for the plan from the job you just left. You still owe ordinary income tax on everything you take out.

Fastest path: contact the plan administrator (or use its website) and request a distribution. Expect the following:

- The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution. Your actual tax may be higher or lower; it gets settled when you file. State tax may also be withheld.
- Some plans only allow a full lump sum, not partial or periodic withdrawals. Check before you request.
- Processing usually takes days to a couple of weeks, depending on the plan.

Pitfalls that cost you the exemption:

- Don't roll the money into an IRA first. The rule of 55 doesn't apply to IRAs, so penalty-free access there generally waits until 59½.
- Don't roll it into a new employer's plan either. The exemption is tied to the plan of the employer you separated from.
- It doesn't cover 401(k)s from employers you left before the year you turned 55.

Also consider how much to take. A large lump sum is taxed as income in one year and can push you into a much higher bracket. If you don't need all the money now, taking only what you need, or spreading withdrawals across tax years, can save a lot.
===END===

Everyone doesn't agree, and a board memo built on that claim is exposed. The evidence is mixed and depends on the setup. The best-known randomized trial (Bloom et al., Nature 2024, Trip.com) found that hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by about a third. Some studies of fully remote work do find productivity losses. So the defensible argument is about the costs of fully remote work, not that remote work kills productivity.

Three bullets you can defend:

- Fully remote setups have shown measurable productivity losses in some controlled studies, especially for routine or closely supervised work (e.g., Atkin, Schoar & Shinde's randomized study of data-entry workers in India).
- Remote work reduces informal mentoring and feedback, which hurts junior employees most (e.g., Emanuel & Harrington's research on colocated vs. remote engineers).
- Collaboration networks become more siloed under remote work, reducing cross-team communication (Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour 2022, a study of Microsoft employees).

Check each citation against the paper before it goes to the board. And expect someone on the board to raise the hybrid evidence.

===ID: B5===
A tokenized deposit is a bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's a liability of a bank. A stablecoin is a token issued by a separate issuer, backed by a dedicated pool of reserves. Most of the other differences follow from that:

| | Tokenized deposit | Payment stablecoin (GENIUS Act regime) |
|---|---|---|
| Claim on | The issuing bank, like any deposit | The issuer, backed by segregated reserves |
| Backing | Bank's balance sheet; fractional reserve, the bank can lend against it | 1:1 in cash, T-bills and similar high-quality liquid assets; can't be lent out |
| Deposit insurance | Generally treated as a deposit, so FDIC-insured up to limits | Not insured; protection comes from reserves and priority in insolvency |
| Interest | Can pay interest | Issuers barred from paying yield |
| Who can hold it | Usually the bank's own KYC'd customers, often on permissioned chains | Broadly transferable, commonly on public chains, including to third parties |
| Regulator | Bank supervisors under existing banking law | Stablecoin framework (OCC, state regulators, or bank regulators for bank subsidiaries) |

In practice, a tokenized deposit is closer to money inside the banking system, useful for institutional settlement between a bank's own clients. A stablecoin works more like a bearer instrument that can move freely across platforms. The gap narrows when a bank issues a stablecoin through a subsidiary, but the legal distinction still holds: a deposit versus a fully reserved non-deposit token.
===END===

No. It isn't approximately 1; it is exactly 1, and your teaching experience doesn't change the math. Here's a different argument, since the last two didn't land.

Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the difference d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. Cutting the decimal off after n nines gives 0.99...9, which is 1 − 10⁻ⁿ, and 0.999... is at least that large for every n. So d is at most 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0.

The intuition that it's "always a tiny bit less" describes each finite truncation (0.9, 0.99, 0.999), and every one of those really is less than 1. But 0.999... is the infinite decimal, not any step in that sequence, and "infinitely close" in the real numbers means equal. That the intuition is common is why this is a well-known teaching point, not evidence that it's correct.
===END===
