Yes, you're right, and the difference isn't pedantic. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, data at least this extreme would turn up 3% of the time. That is P(data | H0). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(H1 | data), and you can't get that from a p-value alone. It depends on how plausible the hypothesis was to begin with and on the study's power. The two numbers can be far apart. In a field where most tested hypotheses are false, or where studies are underpowered, a large share of results with p ≈ 0.03 are false positives. That gap is one of the main causes of the replication crisis.

Assume the key is already compromised. Bots scrape GitHub for AWS keys within minutes. Work in this order:

1. Deactivate the key now: sign in as root → Security credentials → Access keys → Deactivate, then Delete. Do this before cleaning up the repo, because removing the commit doesn't revoke the key.
2. Secure the root account: change the root password, confirm MFA is on (add it if not), and check that the root email account itself hasn't been compromised.
3. Look for attacker activity in every region, not just your usual one. In CloudTrail, check events made with that key ID since the push. Look for new IAM users, roles, access keys and policies; unexpected EC2/GPU instances, Lambda functions or ECS tasks, often in unused regions; changes to S3 buckets or their policies; and Billing / Cost Explorer spikes.
4. Delete anything the attacker created, and rotate any credentials they could have read or made.
5. Open an AWS Support case (Account and billing → security). They can help with investigation and sometimes with fraudulent charges.
6. Clean the repo: rewrite history with `git filter-repo` or BFG and force-push, or make the repo private. Forks and caches may keep copies anyway, which is why step 1 is the real fix.
7. Going forward, don't create root access keys at all. Use IAM roles or SSO with short-lived credentials, and turn on GitHub secret scanning with push protection.

My earlier answer was wrong, but not for the reason you give. `is` checks identity, not equality, so use `==` to compare strings. Interning is a CPython implementation detail and applies only to some strings, such as literals and identifier-like names. Strings built at runtime (concatenation, `input()`, slicing, reading from files) are usually distinct objects even when they're equal. So `a is b` can be `False` when `a == b` is `True`, and Python 3.8+ warns about `is` used with a literal. On speed, you're mistaken: `is` is a pointer comparison and isn't slower than `==`. That doesn't matter, though, because `is` gives the wrong answer for this job.

===ID: R2===
For most people at 56, putting 100% in a single S&P 500 fund carries more risk than it should. The main problem isn't the fund. It's how close you are to withdrawing.

The strongest case against it is sequence-of-returns risk. The S&P 500 fell about 49% in 2000–02 and about 55% in 2007–09, and the 2000 peak took roughly seven years to recover in nominal terms. A drop like that just before or early in retirement, while you're selling shares to live on, can permanently shrink what the portfolio can pay out, even if the market later recovers. The fund is also concentrated: US-only, large-cap only, with the top ten stocks recently making up over a third of the index. There's the behavioral risk too. People fully in stocks often sell near the bottom of a crash, which locks in the loss.

The case for it: the S&P 500 is cheap and broadly diversified across US large companies, and a 56-year-old still has a horizon of 30+ years for at least part of the money. It can be reasonable if you won't draw on this money for 10+ years, have a pension, Social Security or other income that covers your essential spending, could watch a 50% drop without selling, or this is only part of your total assets.

The more common approach at your stage is to keep several years of planned withdrawals in bonds or cash and hold the long-horizon rest in broad equities, ideally with some international exposure. The right split depends on your retirement date, other income and spending needs, which I don't know. With a decision this large, a one-time session with a fee-only fiduciary planner is worth the cost. If the money is in a 401(k) or IRA, rebalancing within the account isn't a taxable event.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It sets up a connection with a handshake and guarantees that data arrives in order, with nothing missing or duplicated, using acknowledgments, retransmission, and flow and congestion control. It delivers a byte stream with no message boundaries. The cost is latency and overhead, and one lost packet stalls everything behind it (head-of-line blocking).

UDP is connectionless and best-effort. Each datagram is sent on its own, with no delivery guarantee, no ordering, no retransmission and no congestion control, but message boundaries are preserved and overhead is minimal.

Use TCP when correctness matters more than latency, as in HTTP/1.1–2, databases, file transfer and SSH. Use UDP when fresh data beats complete data, or when the application handles reliability itself: DNS, VoIP, video, games, and QUIC/HTTP/3, which builds its own reliability on top of UDP.
===END===

You should still switch. It's 2/3. There are two doors left, but they aren't equally likely, because the host's choice isn't random. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick.

Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing the host does changes that.
