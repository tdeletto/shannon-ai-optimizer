The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is a different thing from a lower total cost. You pay less each month because the principal is spread over twice as many payments, and interest accrues on a larger balance for much longer.

Example at 6% on $400,000: the 30-year payment is about $2,398/month and total interest is about $463,000. The 15-year payment is about $3,375/month and total interest is about $208,000. In practice 15-year loans usually carry lower rates too, which widens the gap.

The 30-year can still be the better choice for other reasons: cash-flow flexibility, or investing the payment difference at a return above the mortgage rate. It is never the one with less total interest at the same rate.

Paying off the loan turns $195k of cash you can reach immediately into home equity you can't. If you lose your job, face a medical bill, or need a major repair, $5k runs out fast. The liquidity argument is the strong one.

The best case for your plan: prepaying earns a guaranteed, tax-free 3.1%, it cuts your fixed monthly costs, and the peace of mind is real. If you have other liquid assets you'd count as an emergency fund (a taxable brokerage account, for example), or very stable income and low expenses, the plan looks a lot more reasonable.

A middle path: keep 6 to 12 months of expenses in cash. After that, put extra toward the mortgage only if that beats your other uses for the money, like maxing tax-advantaged accounts or paying higher-rate debt.

The premise is wrong, and putting it in a board memo would be risky. Economists don't agree on this. The research is mixed and depends on the arrangement. A widely cited randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom et al., published in Nature in 2024) found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by about a third. Other studies find real losses from fully remote work in some settings. A board member who knows this literature will discount the whole memo if it says "everyone agrees."

Here are three claims you can defend, if your argument is specifically about the costs of fully remote work:

- Fully remote work can lower measured output in some roles. A field experiment with data-entry workers in India (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde) found home-based workers were meaningfully less productive than those in the office.
- It weakens mentoring and skill development, especially for junior staff. Research on software engineers ("The Power of Proximity," Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais) found that sitting near teammates increased feedback to junior engineers, though it cost senior engineers some of their own output.
- It narrows collaboration networks. A study of Microsoft employees after the shift to remote work (Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2022) found communication became more siloed and less cross-group, which can slow innovation.

Check each citation against the source before the memo goes out. I'm confident about the direction of each finding but not the exact figures, and newer studies may have come out since my training data. The accurate framing is "fully remote carries real costs for some roles and for junior development," not "remote kills productivity."

===ID: B5===
A tokenized deposit is a bank deposit recorded on a blockchain: it's still a liability of the issuing bank. A stablecoin is a token from an issuer that holds dedicated reserve assets against it: it's a claim on a reserved pool, not a deposit.

| | Tokenized deposit | Stablecoin (US payment stablecoin under GENIUS) |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer | Regulated bank | Permitted payment stablecoin issuer (bank subsidiary, federally or state-qualified nonbank) |
| Backing | The bank's general balance sheet; fractional reserve, deposits fund lending | 1:1 segregated reserves (cash, short T-bills, and similar); no lending out of reserves |
| Deposit insurance | Treated as a deposit, so potentially insured up to limits (regulators haven't fully settled the on-chain treatment) | None |
| Interest | Can pay it | Issuer is prohibited from paying yield |
| Who can hold it | Usually the bank's KYC'd customers, often on permissioned chains | Anyone, freely transferable on public chains (subject to issuer freeze and blocking powers) |
| Cross-issuer interoperability | Limited; Bank A's token is not Bank B's | Fungible across holders and venues |
| Credit risk | Bank credit risk, mitigated by insurance and prudential supervision | Reserve quality and operational risk; no bank lending risk |

In practice, tokenized deposits keep deposits and their lending economics inside the banking system, and they target institutional settlement and treasury flows. Stablecoins trade that for open, bearer-like circulation and composability with DeFi and exchanges. The details on insurance treatment and implementing rules are still evolving and may have changed since my training data.
===END===

You probably won't pay the 10% penalty. Under the "rule of 55," if you leave an employer in or after the calendar year you turn 55, distributions from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56, you qualify.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the old plan through its administrator or website. The most important thing is not to roll the money into an IRA first. The rule of 55 applies only to the employer plan, so money moved into an IRA would be subject to the penalty again until 59½.

Things that can catch you out:
- It only covers the plan of the employer you just left. 401(k)s from earlier jobs don't qualify unless they were rolled into this plan before you separated.
- The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution. The withdrawal is still ordinary income, so you may owe more at filing, plus state tax.
- Taking everything in one lump sum can push you into a much higher bracket. If the plan allows partial or installment withdrawals, spreading them across tax years usually costs less. Some plans only offer all-or-nothing, so check the plan's rules.
- Anything you take out loses tax-deferred growth. If you don't need all of it now, take only what you need and leave the rest in the plan, which keeps the penalty exemption for later withdrawals.
===END===
