The premise is wrong, and a board memo built on it is exposed. Not everyone agrees remote work kills productivity. The research is mixed and depends on the setup: Bloom et al.'s Ctrip experiment found fully remote call-center staff about 13% more productive, and their 2024 Nature RCT found hybrid work had no measurable effect on productivity or promotions while cutting attrition by about a third. Other studies find real costs. A director who knows this literature will discount the whole memo.

Here are three bullets that hold up under scrutiny:

- Some controlled studies find measurable productivity losses from fully remote work. Atkin et al. found data-entry workers in India about 18% less productive at home, and Emanuel and Harrington found output losses among remote call-center workers.
- Junior staff get less feedback and mentoring when teams are remote. Emanuel, Harrington and Pallais ("The Power of Proximity") found engineers sitting near their teammates received substantially more code-review feedback, and the effect was largest for newer and female engineers.
- Collaboration networks get more siloed. Microsoft's firm-wide study (Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2021) found that remote work reduced cross-group communication and shifted it toward asynchronous channels, which can slow knowledge transfer.

If you want to argue for more in-office time, "fully remote carries costs for some kinds of work and for early-career people" is defensible. "Remote kills productivity" is not.

===ID: B5===
Both are dollar-denominated tokens on a blockchain. What differs is what backs them and whose liability they are.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary commercial bank deposit recorded on a ledger. It is a liability of the issuing bank and sits inside the bank's fractional-reserve balance sheet, so the bank can lend against it. It gets the same treatment as any deposit: FDIC insurance up to the limits, bank capital and liquidity regulation, and it can pay interest. In practice it is usually permissioned, meaning it moves only among the bank's own customers or within a consortium.

A payment stablecoin is issued by a non-bank or a bank subsidiary and is backed 1:1 by segregated reserves (cash, short-dated Treasuries, repo, etc.) that are not lent out. Under the GENIUS Act it is explicitly not a deposit, is not FDIC-insured, and the issuer cannot pay holders interest. It is typically a bearer-style instrument that circulates freely on public chains among parties who have no relationship with the issuer.

The practical tradeoff: tokenized deposits keep money creation and yield inside the banking system but are less portable. Stablecoins are more portable and composable, but the holder relies on reserve quality and redemption rights rather than on deposit insurance.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not a lower total cost. You pay less each month because you're stretching the loan over twice as many months, and interest keeps accruing on the balance the whole time.

Take $300,000 at 6%. The 30-year payment is about $1,799 a month, and total interest comes to about $347,500. The 15-year payment is about $2,532 a month, and total interest comes to about $155,700. The 30-year costs roughly $190,000 more.

There is a legitimate argument for the 30-year, but it's a different claim: the lower payment gives you flexibility, and if you invest the difference at a return above the mortgage rate, you can come out ahead. That's a bet on investment returns. It doesn't mean the loan itself is cheaper. (In practice, 15-year loans also usually carry lower rates, which widens the gap.)

I won't, because it isn't approximately 1. It is exactly 1, and your years of tutoring don't change the math. Your students' intuition matches a well-documented misconception. It is not evidence.

Here is a different argument than the ones I gave before. Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the difference d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But d is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, smaller than 0.001, and in fact smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n, because 0.999... is at least as big as any finite string of 9s. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0, so d = 0.

The intuition "it gets infinitely close" describes the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, ..., and every term of that sequence is less than 1. But "0.999..." doesn't name any term. It names the limit, and the limit is 1. That distinction is usually what clears up the confusion for students.

Your assumption is probably wrong. If you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, the "rule of 55" lets you withdraw from that employer's 401(k) without the 10% penalty. Ordinary income tax still applies.

The catches:
- It applies only to the plan of the employer you just left. If you roll the money into an IRA first, you lose the exemption, and IRA withdrawals before 59½ are penalized. Take withdrawals directly from the 401(k).
- Balances in older employers' 401(k)s don't qualify unless you roll them into this plan before withdrawing (if the plan accepts roll-ins).
- The plan must allow distributions after separation. Some allow only a lump sum, not partial withdrawals, so check with the administrator.
- The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax. Your actual liability may be higher or lower, and a large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket, so splitting withdrawals across tax years is often cheaper.

The fastest route is to request a distribution through the plan administrator's website or by phone. It typically takes a few days to two weeks. If you don't qualify for the rule of 55, 72(t) substantially equal periodic payments also avoid the penalty but lock you into a fixed schedule for at least 5 years.

The case for raising now: a round you can close today is worth more than a hypothetical higher-valuation round later. Waiting only works if three things all happen: you hit the metrics, the market holds or improves, and you still have enough runway to raise from strength. The case for waiting: if the metric improvement is highly likely and material (say, doubling revenue, or proving retention you can't show today), the valuation difference can easily outweigh the dilution. And raising "big" at a low price means extra dilution you can't undo.

The deciding questions: How many months of runway will you have at the 6-month mark? How confident are you in the metrics, based on pipeline or trend and not hope? What are investors signaling right now, with actual term sheets or only interest? A middle path is to raise a smaller amount now (an extension, or a SAFE with a cap) to extend runway, then do the priced round once the metrics are in. If you'd be below about 12 months of runway at the 6-month mark, your cofounder is probably right.
