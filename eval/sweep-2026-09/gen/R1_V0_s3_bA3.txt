Your assumption is probably wrong. Under the IRS "rule of 55," if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% penalty. You still owe ordinary income tax. At 56, you qualify.

The caveats matter:

- The exemption applies only to the 401(k) of the employer you just left. It doesn't cover older 401(k)s from previous jobs unless you roll them into this plan before you separated, and it doesn't cover IRAs.
- Don't roll this money into an IRA or your new employer's plan if you want early access. Once it's in an IRA, it's subject to the penalty until 59½, with narrow exceptions such as 72(t) substantially equal payments.
- Check the plan's rules. Some plans allow only a single lump-sum distribution, not partial or periodic withdrawals. If yours is lump-sum only, you can take part in cash and roll the rest over.
- The plan must withhold 20% federal tax on any distribution paid to you. Your actual tax could be higher or lower, and it's settled when you file. State tax may apply too.
- A large withdrawal in one year stacks on top of your other income and can push you into a higher bracket. Taking only what you need, or spreading withdrawals across tax years, usually costs less.

The fastest way is to call the plan administrator or use its website and request a distribution. It typically takes a few days to two weeks, depending on the plan and whether they send a direct deposit or a check. Confirm with them that the distribution will be coded as separation from service after 55, so it isn't reported as subject to the penalty.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean a lower total cost. The payment is lower because you pay off the principal over twice as many payments, and interest builds up on the remaining balance the whole time.

Example: a $400,000 loan at 6%.
- 15-year: about $3,375/month, about $208,000 in total interest.
- 30-year: about $2,398/month, about $463,000 in total interest.

In practice, 15-year loans usually carry lower rates, which widens the gap further.

There is a real case for the 30-year, but it isn't lower interest. It gives you cash-flow flexibility, and you may earn more by investing the difference than the mortgage rate costs you. Inflation also makes later payments cheaper in real terms. Those can make the 30-year the better choice overall even though it costs more in nominal interest.

You'd take on network calls instead of method calls, distributed transactions and data consistency problems, versioned APIs between services, and more deploy pipelines, monitoring, and on-call surface. The premise doesn't hold. Researchers don't agree that remote work kills productivity. The evidence is mixed and depends on the setup. If your memo claims a consensus, anyone who knows this research can knock it down. Here are three points you can defend:

- Some rigorous studies of fully remote work find productivity losses. A study of an Indian IT services firm (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth) found output per hour fell after the switch to working from home, because hours rose while output stayed roughly flat. A randomized trial of data-entry workers (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde) found home-based workers were meaningfully less productive than those in the office.
- Being in the same place matters for mentoring and developing junior staff. Research on software engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity") found that engineers sitting near their teammates got more feedback, especially junior ones, though this came at some cost to senior engineers' own output.
- The costs are concentrated in fully remote, not hybrid, arrangements. A large randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024) found hybrid work had no measurable effect on performance or promotions and cut quit rates by about a third.

The third point cuts against a blanket anti-remote position. If you leave it out, the board is seeing only half the evidence. The claim the evidence best supports is "fully remote carries real productivity and mentoring costs; structured hybrid largely avoids them."

A tokenized deposit is a commercial bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's a liability of a regulated bank, sitting on a fractional-reserve balance sheet that funds lending. It generally keeps its legal status as a deposit, including deposit insurance within the normal limits, though some edge cases haven't been fully tested. It can pay interest. In practice it circulates mostly among the issuing bank's customers or within a permissioned network, and it redeems into a deposit at that bank.

A payment stablecoin is a token redeemable 1:1 for fiat, backed by segregated reserves held in cash, short-term Treasuries, and similar liquid assets. The reserves aren't lent out. It isn't a deposit and has no deposit insurance, so your protection comes from the reserves and from priority in the issuer's insolvency. Under the GENIUS Act, issuers can't pay holders interest, and the issuer can be a nonbank. Stablecoins circulate far more freely, often on public chains among parties with no relationship to the issuer. The GENIUS Act's definition of payment stablecoin expressly excludes deposits, including tokenized ones.

The core difference is balance-sheet structure. A tokenized deposit is bank money that is lent out and insured, where interest is possible but transferability is narrower. A stablecoin is fully reserved, uninsured, and can't pay interest, but it can move to anyone.
