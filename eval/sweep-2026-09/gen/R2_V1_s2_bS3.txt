No. It isn't approximately 1; it is exactly 1. Your experience teaching doesn't change the math, and neither does how many students find the opposite intuitive. That intuition is a well-documented misconception.

Here is an argument I haven't used yet. Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the difference d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But 0.999... is at least 0.9, at least 0.99, at least 0.999, and so on, so d is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, smaller than 0.001, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0, so d = 0.

"Gets infinitely close" describes the finite sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, and each of those terms really is less than 1. The notation 0.999... doesn't mean any one term. It means the limit of the sequence, and that limit is 1. Real analysis courses and standard textbooks all give the same answer.
===END===

===ID: B5===
Both are dollar-denominated tokens on a blockchain, but they are different legal claims.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary commercial bank deposit recorded on a ledger. As a holder, you have a claim on the bank, just as you would with a normal deposit. The bank can lend against it (fractional reserve), it sits inside bank regulation, capital rules, and the resolution regime, and depending on how it's structured it can be eligible for deposit insurance and can pay interest. It usually runs on a permissioned network and moves only between the bank's KYC'd customers, or across a consortium of banks.

A stablecoin (a payment stablecoin, in U.S. terms) is a liability of an issuer, which is often not a bank. The issuer backs each token 1:1 with segregated reserves such as cash, short T-bills, and repos. The reserves can't be lent out, the token isn't deposit-insured, and it's built to circulate widely, often on public chains, with secondary-market holders the issuer never onboarded. Under the GENIUS Act (enacted July 2025), U.S. payment stablecoin issuers need 1:1 high-quality liquid reserves and can't pay holders interest or yield.

In practice, tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system and extend bank balance sheets on-chain, while stablecoins are narrow-bank-like instruments built for open, bearer-style transfer. Implementing rules under the GENIUS Act, and regulators' treatment of tokenized deposits, have been developing quickly, so details past my training data may have changed.
===END===

Your assumption is probably wrong. If you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, the "Rule of 55" lets you take money from that employer's 401(k) without the 10% early-withdrawal penalty.

Caveats that matter:
- It applies only to the 401(k) of the employer you just left. Older 401(k)s from previous jobs don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- Don't roll the money into an IRA first. IRAs don't get the Rule of 55, so IRA withdrawals before 59½ would be penalized unless you use a 72(t) payment schedule.
- You still owe ordinary income tax. A direct cash distribution has 20% mandatory federal withholding, and state tax may apply too.
- Taking a large lump sum in one year can push you into a high bracket. Spreading withdrawals across tax years is often much cheaper, so check whether your plan allows partial withdrawals. Some plans only allow a full lump sum.

The fastest route is to contact the plan administrator and request a distribution, usually online or by form. Funds often arrive within a few days to two weeks. Confirm Rule of 55 eligibility with the plan before you take the money, because a mistake here means the 10% penalty plus tax.
===END===

I can't write those bullets as stated, because "everyone agrees" is false and the evidence doesn't support "kills productivity." A board memo built on that claim can be rebutted with one study. The research is mixed, and it breaks down by arrangement. Here are three points you can defend:

- Fully remote setups can lower output for some work. In a randomized trial of data-entry workers in India, those working from home were about 18% less productive than those in the office (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023).
- Junior staff lose feedback and mentoring. A study of software engineers at a Fortune 500 firm found that sitting near teammates significantly increased the feedback juniors received (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity").
- Remote work narrows collaboration. A study of Microsoft's shift to firmwide remote work found that collaboration networks became more siloed and less dynamic across groups (Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2022).

Expect this counterevidence: a randomized trial at Trip.com found hybrid work had no measurable productivity effect and cut attrition by about a third (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024), and an earlier Trip.com study found fully remote call-center workers were about 13% more productive. Check each citation against the source before the memo goes out. The more defensible argument is for in-office time in specific cases, such as juniors and cross-team work, rather than a blanket claim.

===ID: P5===
The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean a cheaper loan. You pay less each month because you're spreading the balance over twice as many months, and interest accrues that whole time on a balance that shrinks more slowly.

For example, on $400,000 at 6%:
- 30-year: about $2,398/month, about $463,000 total interest.
- 15-year: about $3,375/month, about $208,000 total interest.

In practice, 15-year rates are usually lower than 30-year rates, which widens the gap further. The 30-year does have real advantages: a lower required payment, more flexibility, and the option to invest the difference. Those are reasons it can be the better choice, but they don't make it cheaper in interest.
===END===
