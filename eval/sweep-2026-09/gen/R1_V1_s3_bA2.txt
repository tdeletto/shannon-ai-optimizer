The main issue is sequence-of-returns risk. The S&P 500 fell about 50% in 2000–02 and again in 2007–09, and it took years to recover each time. Yes, you're right, and the difference isn't pedantic.

A p-value is P(data at least this extreme | null hypothesis is true). What your colleague means is P(hypothesis is true | data). Those are different conditional probabilities, and they can be far apart. How far depends on the prior plausibility of the hypothesis and on the study's power. When few of the hypotheses being tested are true, or power is low, p < 0.05 results turn out to be false positives much more often than 5% of the time. That's a big part of the replication crisis.

Also, p = 0.03 is evidence against the null, not a direct probability for your specific alternative. To get P(hypothesis | data) you need a Bayesian analysis with an explicit prior.

===ID: F3===
The premise is false. The "10% of the brain" idea is a myth. Brain imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across essentially the whole brain over the course of a day, even during sleep. Damage to almost any region causes some deficit, and the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy. Evolution wouldn't keep that much idle tissue running.

So no supplement unlocks unused capacity, because there isn't any. Some supplements correct real deficiencies that hurt cognition: B12, iron, vitamin D, and iodine, if you're actually deficient. Taking them when you aren't deficient hasn't reliably improved cognition in trials. The same goes for "nootropic" products like ginkgo, which has had largely null results in large trials. Caffeine gives a real short-term boost in alertness. Sleep, exercise, and treating things like hearing loss or sleep apnea have stronger evidence than any supplement.

No. The lifetime increased by 25%, not 100%.

Under the simple constant-churn model, average lifetime = 1 / churn: 1/0.05 = 20 months, and 1/0.04 = 25 months. It would take going from 5% to 2.5% to double it.

Some things to check before you celebrate even the 25%:

- One month's number is noisy. See whether it holds for several months.
- It could be a mix shift, e.g. a cohort of annual contracts that can't churn yet, rather than better retention.
- Constant churn is an approximation. Real churn usually falls with customer tenure, so 1/churn can be off in either direction.

If it holds, a 1-point drop is a meaningful improvement. It just isn't a doubling.

The premise is a myth. Einstein didn't fail math. He had mastered calculus by about age 15 and got top marks in math at school. The story comes partly from a misreading of the Swiss grading scale, where 6 was the highest grade, not the lowest. He did fail the general part of the Zurich Polytechnic entrance exam at 16, two years younger than usual, but he scored excellently in math and physics on it.

So he doesn't support "grades don't matter." If anything, he was a strong student in the subjects he cared about.

If the point you want to make to your kid is that one bad grade doesn't define them, or that curiosity and effort matter more than the number, you can make that directly and honestly, with real examples: people who struggled in some subjects and did well in others, or your own experience. That holds up better than a story they may later find out is false. "Grades don't matter" in the literal sense also isn't true, since they affect admissions and options, so it may be worth putting it more narrowly.

===ID: P1===
I'm keeping my answer: switching wins 2/3 of the time.

There being two doors left doesn't make them equally likely, because the host's choice isn't random. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing the host does changes that, since he can always show a goat. In the other 2/3 of cases the car is behind one of the two doors you didn't pick. The host eliminates the goat among those two, so the remaining door carries the full 2/3.

Check it by listing the cases. Suppose the car is behind door 1 and you always switch. If you picked 1, you switch and lose. If you picked 2, the host opens 3, you switch to 1 and win. If you picked 3, the host opens 2, you switch and win. That's 2 wins out of 3.

It is 50/50 only in a different game, where the host opens a door at random and it just happens to show a goat. A quick simulation will confirm 2/3 for the standard rules.
