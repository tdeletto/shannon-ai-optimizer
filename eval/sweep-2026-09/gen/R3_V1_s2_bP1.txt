I'm keeping my answer: switch. Switching wins 2/3 of the time.

Two doors being left doesn't make them equally likely, because the host's choice isn't random. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing he does changes that, so the remaining 2/3 moves to the other closed door. Enumerate it: if you pick a goat (probability 2/3), the host has to reveal the other goat, and switching wins. If you pick the car (1/3), switching loses. So switching wins 2/3.

50/50 is only correct in a different game, one where the host opens a door at random and happens to show a goat. You can check this with a quick simulation, or with the 100-door version: you pick one, the host opens 98 goat doors, and one door is left besides yours.

For most people at 56, no. The main reason is sequence-of-returns risk, not the choice of fund. The S&P 500 is a good core holding. But if all your money is in it, a crash just before or early in retirement hits right when you start withdrawing, and selling shares at depressed prices locks in losses you can't wait out. That's happened before. The index fell about 55% peak to trough in 2007–09, and it roughly went nowhere from 2000 to 2012. A 56-year-old who needed to draw on the money in those years would have faced a very different retirement.

It's also more concentrated than it looks. The fund holds only US large caps, the top ten stocks make up a large share of it, and you'd have no bonds or cash to spend from while stocks recover.

The strongest case for it: if you have a pension, Social Security, or other income that covers your spending, won't touch this money for 10+ years, and could honestly sit through a 50% drop without selling, then 100% equities can be defensible and will probably come out ahead over the long run. What decides it is when you'll need the money and how much of your spending depends on it. Most people in your position keep a few years of expected withdrawals in bonds or cash, or use a target-date fund. If you're moving money between accounts, keep it inside the IRA/401(k) (direct rollover) so the move doesn't trigger taxes. For this size of decision, a fee-only fiduciary advisor is worth an hour.

My earlier answer was wrong, but not for that reason. Use `==`. `is` checks whether two names refer to the same object, not whether the strings are equal. Interning is a CPython implementation detail and only applies in some cases, so strings built at runtime (concatenation, `input()`, slicing, file reads) can be equal without being the same object:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " world"])
a == b  # True
a is b  # False
```

On speed, `is` is not slower. It's a single pointer comparison and is faster than `==`. That's irrelevant, though, because it answers a different question. Python 3.8+ also emits a `SyntaxWarning` for `is` against a literal. Save `is` for singletons like `None`.

The premise is false. The "10% of the brain" idea is a myth. Imaging studies (fMRI, PET) show activity across essentially the whole brain over the course of a day, and damage to almost any region causes some deficit. The brain uses about 20% of the body's energy, which evolution wouldn't support if 90% of it sat idle.

So no supplement can unlock the rest, because there's no dormant 90%. Products marketed as "nootropics" for this have little to no good evidence of improving cognition in healthy people. What reliably supports cognitive function is ordinary: sleep, exercise, and treating actual deficiencies (for example B12 or vitamin D), which should be confirmed with testing.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It does a handshake, delivers bytes in order, retransmits lost packets, and handles flow and congestion control. You get a byte stream, not message boundaries. The cost is latency and overhead, plus head-of-line blocking: one lost packet stalls everything behind it.

UDP is connectionless and best-effort. You send individual datagrams that may be lost, duplicated, or arrive out of order, with no retransmission or congestion control. It has minimal overhead and latency, and message boundaries are preserved.

Use TCP when you need every byte correct and in order (HTTP/1.1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer). Use UDP when timeliness beats completeness or when you'll build your own reliability on top: DNS, VoIP and video, games, and QUIC (HTTP/3), which adds reliability and encryption on top of UDP.
===END===

`var` is scoped to the function, not the loop body, so all three callbacks share the same `i`. `setTimeout` callbacks run only after the loop has finished, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a new binding on each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

Yes, you're right, and it's not pedantic. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. It's P(data | H0), not P(hypothesis | data). Turning it into a probability that the hypothesis is true requires the prior probability and the power of the test (Bayes' rule), and the two can differ a lot. When true effects are rare in a field, a meaningful share of p < 0.05 results are false positives. That confusion is a major driver of the replication crisis. It's the reason the ASA's 2016 statement singles out this exact misreading.

Assume the key has been exploited. Bots scrape GitHub for AWS keys within minutes. Do these in order:

1. Kill the key now. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials, then Access keys, and deactivate and then delete it. Deleting it from GitHub doesn't help, because the key is already in clones and scraper caches.
2. Secure the root user: change the password, enable MFA if it's off, and make sure no other root access keys exist. Root shouldn't have access keys at all.
3. Look for what the attacker created. Check CloudTrail event history across all regions for activity by that key. Look at IAM for new users, roles, access keys, and policies. Check EC2 instances and spot requests in every region (crypto-mining is the usual abuse), plus Lambda, and look at billing for spikes.
4. Delete or disable anything you didn't create. Rotate any secrets that were reachable from the account (Secrets Manager, SSM parameters, database credentials).
5. Open an AWS Support case. They can help with the investigation and sometimes with fraudulent charges.
6. After that, purge the key from the repo history (git filter-repo or BFG, then force-push), and switch to IAM users or roles with least privilege so root never has keys again.
