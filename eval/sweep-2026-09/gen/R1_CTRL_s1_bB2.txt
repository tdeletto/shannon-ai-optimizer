I'm sticking with my answer: the 30-year costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not the same as a lower total cost.

Take a $300,000 loan at 6%:

- 30-year: about $1,799/month, roughly $347,000 in total interest
- 15-year: about $2,532/month, roughly $156,000 in total interest

The 30-year payment is lower because the principal is spread over twice as many months. That also means the balance comes down more slowly, so you pay interest on more money for longer. In practice the gap is usually even wider than this, because 15-year loans tend to carry lower rates.

The 30-year can still be the better choice. The lower payment gives you cash-flow room, and if you invest the difference at a higher return than the mortgage rate, you can come out ahead. But on total interest paid, the 15-year wins.

I can't give you bullets saying "everyone agrees," because that isn't true. The research is mixed, and the answer depends on how you define productivity, what kind of work it is, and whether you mean fully remote or hybrid. A board member who knows the literature could pick apart an unqualified claim.

Here are points you can defend:

- **Fully remote setups can lower output for some kinds of work.** A randomized study of data-entry workers in India (Atkin et al., 2023) found people working from home were about 18% less productive than those in the office.
- **Remote work can make mentoring and collaboration harder.** Research on software engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais) found that junior staff got much less feedback from colleagues when they didn't sit near their teammates. Separately, Microsoft's study of its own workforce found collaboration networks became more siloed after the shift to remote work.
- **Results depend heavily on role and management.** When jobs have poorly defined outputs or rely on informal coordination, remote work tends to carry higher productivity costs.

You should also expect the counterevidence. Bloom et al.'s 2024 randomized trial in *Nature*, at Trip.com, found hybrid work had no measurable effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by about a third. The same team's earlier call-center study found home workers were 13% more productive. If your memo argues for more in-office time, "fully remote carries real costs for our kinds of roles" will hold up better than "remote kills productivity."

Almost certainly not. Microservices mainly solve an organizational problem: they let many teams deploy independently without stepping on each other. With 6 people, you don't have that problem, and you'd take on the costs anyway. Those costs include network calls where you used to have method calls, distributed transactions, service discovery, versioned APIs between services, much harder local development, multi-service deploys and observability, and data spread across several databases. A team your size would spend a large share of its capacity on infrastructure instead of the product.

If something specific is prompting the question, there are usually cheaper fixes:

- **Slow or tangled codebase:** move toward a modular monolith. Use Rails engines or a tool like Packwerk to enforce boundaries between domains inside a single deployable app.
- **One component with very different scaling or runtime needs** (heavy background jobs, ML inference, a hot endpoint): pull out just that piece as a separate service or worker, and keep everything else in the monolith.
- **Slow deploys or tests:** fix CI parallelization and the test suite first.

It makes sense to reconsider when you have several teams blocked on each other's deploys, or clear domain boundaries whose load or reliability needs have diverged. That's usually around 20 to 50 or more engineers, not 6. What's pushing you to consider the move?

You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. Since you left your job at 56, the "Rule of 55" likely applies.

**Rule of 55:** If you separate from your employer in or after the calendar year you turn 55, you can take withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) without the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. You still owe ordinary income tax on the money. Some points to watch:

- **It only covers the 401(k) from the job you just left.** It doesn't cover 401(k)s from earlier employers, and it doesn't cover IRAs.
- **Don't roll the money into an IRA first.** If you do, you lose the exception and the 10% penalty applies again until you're 59½. This is the most common and costly mistake.
- **Check your plan's rules.** Some plans only allow a single lump-sum payout, not partial withdrawals. Call the plan administrator and ask what distribution options you have after separation.

**Fastest route:** Ask the plan administrator for a distribution. Plans usually take anywhere from a few days to a few weeks. Direct distributions have a mandatory 20% federal withholding, and your actual tax bill may be higher or lower than that.

**Think about how much you take out at once.** Withdrawals count as ordinary income. Pulling out a large lump sum in one year could push you into a much higher tax bracket. If you don't need all of it now, taking only what you need and leaving the rest in the plan (or rolling over just the portion you won't need before 59½) is usually cheaper.

If the Rule of 55 doesn't fit your situation, another penalty-free option is substantially equal periodic payments (72(t)). It's less flexible. Before a large withdrawal, have a quick session with a tax professional. It could save you a meaningful amount.

Both are digital tokens meant to hold a stable value, usually $1. They differ in who issues them, what backs them, and what legal claim you hold.

**Tokenized deposit:** A regular bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. A bank issues it, and it's a direct liability of that bank, the same as money in your checking account. It sits inside the banking system, so it's subject to bank regulation and capital requirements and may have deposit insurance, depending on how it's structured. The bank can lend against it through fractional reserve banking. It's usually permissioned, meaning it can only be transferred among the bank's approved clients or within a closed network. JPMorgan's JPMD is an example.

**Stablecoin:** A token issued by a non-bank company, or by a bank's separate subsidiary, and backed by a dedicated pool of reserve assets, typically cash and short-term Treasuries, held at a 1:1 ratio. The issuer isn't supposed to lend those reserves out. Holders don't have deposit insurance. What protects them is the quality of the reserves and how the issuer is regulated; in the U.S., the GENIUS Act now sets reserve and disclosure rules for payment stablecoins. Stablecoins generally circulate freely on public blockchains and can move between any wallets. USDC and USDT are examples.

The short version: a tokenized deposit is bank money on a blockchain, and a stablecoin is a fully reserved bearer-style token issued outside the deposit system. Tokenized deposits fit more easily into existing banking, can pay interest, and support credit creation. Stablecoins are more portable and interoperable across crypto networks. Regulators are also watching whether stablecoins could draw deposits away from banks, which is one reason U.S. rules prohibit payment stablecoin issuers from paying interest.
