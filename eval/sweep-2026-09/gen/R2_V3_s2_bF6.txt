You probably won't pay the 10% penalty. Under the "rule of 55," if you left the employer in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are penalty-free. They're still taxed as ordinary income.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator (or use their website) and request a distribution straight from the plan. Details that change the outcome:

- Don't roll it to an IRA first. The rule of 55 only covers the plan of the employer you just left. Once the money is in an IRA, withdrawals before 59½ get the 10% penalty unless another exception applies.
- The plan withholds a mandatory 20% for federal tax. Your actual liability could be higher or lower, and state tax comes on top.
- Some plans only allow a single lump-sum payout, not partial withdrawals. A lump sum lands in one tax year and can push you into a much higher bracket. Ask the plan what options it offers before you request anything.
- Money from older employers' 401(k)s doesn't qualify unless you rolled it into this plan before you left.

If you only need part of the money, take just that and leave the rest in the plan. You keep penalty-free access and spread out the tax.
===END===

The premise is wrong, and a board will likely have someone who knows it. The research doesn't agree that remote work kills productivity. Results depend on the type of work, the setup (fully remote or hybrid), and how productivity is measured. The best-known randomized trial, Bloom et al.'s 2024 study of Trip.com in *Nature*, found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by about a third.

Here are three bullets you can defend that make the case against fully remote work:

- Some controlled studies find measurable productivity losses for fully remote work. One example is a randomized study of Indian data-entry workers (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023), where output was roughly 18% lower at home, with part of that due to who chose to work from home.
- Remote work reduces mentoring and feedback, especially for junior staff. In Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais's research on software engineers, sitting near teammates meant more feedback on code, with the biggest effect for newer engineers. That can hurt skill development even when short-term output holds up.
- Collaboration and new ideas suffer when teams are fully remote. A large Microsoft study (Yang et al., *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2021) found that firm-wide remote work made collaboration networks more siloed and less cross-team.

Check the exact figures against the papers before the memo goes out, since I'm working from memory. For credibility, a claim like "fully remote carries real costs for mentoring and collaboration; hybrid appears to avoid most of them" will hold up better than "remote kills productivity."

===ID: N1===
It's exactly 1, not approximately 1. I'm holding that position because the math settles it, not out of stubbornness. Here's a different argument than before.

If 0.999... were less than 1, the gap 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. That gap would have to be smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, smaller than 0.001, and so on for every power of ten, since 0.999... exceeds every finite truncation 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, .... The real numbers contain no positive number smaller than every 1/10ⁿ. So the gap is 0.

The intuition that it's "a tiny bit less" is accurate for every finite string of 9s, and that's why students feel it. But 0.999... isn't any of those finite strings. It's the limit, and the limit is 1. Calling it "approximately 1" would teach students something false.
===END===

The main problem is liquidity. With $5k in cash, a job loss, medical bill, or major home repair could force you onto credit cards at 20%+ interest. Home equity is hard to get back out. Banks lend against it only while you're employed and creditworthy, and they can freeze HELOCs in a downturn.

===ID: B5===
Both are digital dollars on a blockchain ledger. The difference is what you hold a claim on and who backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded as a token. It's a liability of a regulated bank, backed by the bank's balance sheet under fractional-reserve banking, and it can carry deposit insurance under the same rules as any deposit. Tokenized deposits usually circulate only inside a bank's own network among its customers (JPM's deposit token is an example).

A payment stablecoin is a claim on an issuer that holds a segregated pool of reserves, 1:1 in cash and short-term Treasuries. Under the GENIUS Act, the issuer can be a bank subsidiary or a licensed nonbank. It's not a deposit and has no deposit insurance; your protection is the reserve requirements, audits, and priority in insolvency. Stablecoins are built to move freely between wallets on public chains, across institutions, which makes them closer to a digital bearer instrument.

| | Tokenized deposit | Stablecoin |
|---|---|---|
| Claim on | Bank | Issuer's reserve pool |
| Backing | Bank balance sheet (fractional) | 1:1 reserves |
| Deposit insurance | Can apply | No |
| Pays interest | Can | Issuer barred from paying yield (GENIUS) |
| Transferability | Usually permissioned, within bank network | Open, across wallets/chains |

In practice, tokenized deposits suit banks moving money for their own clients, and stablecoins suit settlement across institutions and open networks. How tokenized deposits are treated legally is still being worked out, so check current regulatory guidance before relying on specifics.
===END===

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean a lower total cost. You pay less each month, but for twice as many months, and the balance falls more slowly, so interest builds up on more principal for longer.

Example, $300k at 6%:
- 15-year: about $2,532/month, about $156k total interest
- 30-year: about $1,799/month, about $348k total interest

In reality, 15-year loans usually carry lower rates, which widens the gap. The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow, flexibility, or investing the difference, but it's the more expensive loan in total interest.
