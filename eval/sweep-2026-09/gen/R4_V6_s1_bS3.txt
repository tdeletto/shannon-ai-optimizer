Your assumption is probably wrong. Under the "Rule of 55," if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) have no 10% penalty. You still owe ordinary income tax.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator and request a cash distribution. Things that affect how much you actually get:

- The exemption covers only the plan from the job you just left. Old 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify, and rolling this one into an IRA first loses the exemption. Withdraw directly from the plan.
- The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a distribution paid to you. Your real tax bill may be higher or lower.
- A lump sum is taxed as income for that year and can push you into a much higher bracket. Partial withdrawals spread across years usually cost less, but some plans only allow a lump sum, so ask.

The premise won't survive a board that reads the research. The evidence is mixed, not unanimous. A 2024 randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom et al., Nature) found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition. Bullets claiming a consensus invite easy rebuttal. Here are three you can defend, but check each citation before it goes in the memo:

- Fully remote work has measurably reduced output in some controlled studies. For example, Atkin, Schoar and Shinde (2023) found home-based data-entry workers were about 18% less productive than office-based peers.
- Remote work can raise hours without raising output. A study of a large Asian IT firm (Gibbs, Mengel and Siemroth, 2023) found hours rose while productivity per hour fell.
- Distance hurts mentoring for junior staff. Research on software engineers (Emanuel, Harrington and Pallais) found physical proximity substantially increased the feedback juniors received.

A more accurate framing for the memo: fully remote arrangements carry productivity and development costs for certain roles and junior staff, while hybrid shows little downside.

No. At 6 people, microservices add costs you'll feel immediately: distributed failures, network calls where method calls used to be, per-service deploys and monitoring, and cross-service data consistency. They solve the problem of many teams blocking each other in one codebase, and you don't have that problem. Invest in a modular monolith instead, with clear internal boundaries (Rails engines or Packwerk), so you can extract pieces later if needed. The exception is a single component with very different scaling or runtime needs, such as heavy background processing or ML inference. Extract only that component. You should reconsider once you have several teams whose deploys and changes routinely conflict.

===ID: B5===
The core difference is whose liability it is and what backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary commercial bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's a liability of a regulated bank, sits inside fractional-reserve banking (the bank can lend against it), can pay interest, and generally carries the same deposit-insurance treatment as the underlying deposit. It usually circulates on permissioned or bank-controlled networks among the bank's clients.

A stablecoin is a token issued by a specialized issuer, often a non-bank, and backed 1:1 by segregated reserves such as cash and short-term Treasuries. Holders have a claim on those reserves, not an insured deposit, and under the GENIUS Act issuers can't pay holders interest. Stablecoins circulate freely on public chains and act like bearer instruments, which is why they're used across exchanges and DeFi.

In practice, a tokenized deposit is bank money moving on new rails, while a stablecoin is fully reserved, narrow-bank-style money that anyone can hold.
