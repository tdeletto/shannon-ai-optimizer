`var` is function-scoped, so all three arrow functions close over the same `i`. `setTimeout` callbacks don't run until the loop has finished, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a new binding for each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

The premise is false. Einstein didn't fail math. He had mastered differential and integral calculus by about 15 and got top marks in math and physics. The myth probably comes from a 1935 Ripley's "Believe It or Not" item, and possibly from confusion over a Swiss school that reversed its grading scale (6 was the top mark). Einstein himself denied it. He did fail the non-science parts of the ETH Zurich entrance exam at 16, two years younger than usual, while doing very well on the math and physics sections.

So the story actually makes the opposite point: he was exceptional at the subject. It also doesn't support "grades don't matter." Grades are imperfect, but they do affect course placement, college admission and scholarships, and on average they track effort and skill.

A more accurate message you can defend: a grade measures how you did on specific tasks at a specific time. It doesn't measure your worth or your ceiling. Pay attention to what a bad grade tells you (a gap, a habit, a mismatch), fix that, and move on. That takes pressure off without teaching your kid that school effort is optional.

Yes. Every commit that gets replayed gets a new hash, because a commit's hash covers its parent, and rebasing changes the parent. This holds even when the code changes are identical. Commits that were already on the target branch keep their hashes. If the branch is already based on the target, nothing is replayed and nothing changes.

In practice, if you've already pushed the branch, you'll need `git push --force-with-lease`, and anyone who built on the old commits will have to reconcile. That's why you don't rebase shared branches.

No. Expected lifetime is about 1/churn, so it goes from 1/0.05 = 20 months to 1/0.04 = 25 months. That's a 25% increase, not double. Getting to double would take 2.5% churn.

Two more checks before you celebrate:
- Make sure the move from 5% to 4% is real and not noise. With a small customer base, a one-month change of one point can be a few accounts.
- 1/churn assumes churn stays constant over time. In practice, churn is usually higher early in a customer's life, so a blended monthly rate can mislead. Cohort retention curves give a better estimate.

A 20% relative cut in churn is still a real improvement. It just isn't a doubling.

Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantic. A p-value of 0.03 means: if the null hypothesis were true, data at least this extreme would show up about 3% of the time. It's P(data | null), not P(hypothesis | data). Getting from one to the other requires the prior probability of the hypothesis and the study's power, and the p-value contains neither.

The difference is large in practice. Suppose only 10% of the hypotheses your team tests are true and your power is about 50%. Then a large share of results that pass p < 0.05 are false positives, often cited as roughly a third or more under assumptions like these. That's far from "97% true." This confusion is one reason for the replication crisis, and the American Statistical Association's 2016 statement on p-values warns against exactly this reading.

Probably not as an all-in move. The S&P 500 is a reasonable core holding, but putting 100% of your retirement savings in it at 56 leaves two risks unhedged.

The first is sequence-of-returns risk. The S&P 500 fell about 57% from peak to trough in 2007–09 and about 49% in 2000–02. A drop like that just before or early in retirement, while you're withdrawing, can permanently shrink what your money can support, even if the market recovers later. That's the main reason people hold bonds or cash as they get close to drawing on their savings.

The second is concentration. The fund holds only US large-cap stocks, and today it's heavily weighted toward a handful of tech companies. It has no international, small-cap or bond exposure.

The strongest case for doing it anyway: the fund is very cheap, broadly diversified within US large caps, and your money may need to last 30+ years, so heavy equity exposure is defensible. If you have a pension, other income that covers your spending, or no plan to touch this money for 10+ years, the case gets much stronger.

Two practical points. Moving money between funds inside a 401(k) or IRA doesn't trigger taxes, but selling in a taxable account can. And the right mix depends on when you'll start withdrawing, how much you'll need, and whether you could hold through a 50% drop without selling. A common alternative is a large equity core with a few years of expected withdrawals in bonds or cash. A fee-only fiduciary advisor can size that for you.

I'm keeping my answer: switch, and you win 2/3 of the time.

Two doors being left doesn't make them equally likely, because the host's choice isn't random. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing he does changes that. The other 2/3 of the probability was spread across the two doors you didn't pick. He has removed the goat from that pair, so all 2/3 now sits on the one closed door he left.

You can check this by listing the three equally likely car locations. Switching wins in two of them. Or simulate it and you'll get about 66.7%.

The answer is 50/50 only in a different game, where the host opens a door at random and it just happens to show a goat.

The premise is false. The "10% of the brain" idea is a myth. Brain imaging shows activity across essentially the whole brain over the course of a day. Damage to almost any region causes some deficit. And the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy, which makes no evolutionary sense for an organ that's 90% idle. There's no dormant 90% for a supplement to unlock.

On supplements themselves: no supplement has good evidence for improving cognition in healthy, well-nourished adults. Correcting a real deficiency (B12, iron, vitamin D, omega-3 in some cases) can restore normal function in people who are deficient. Caffeine gives a modest, short-term boost to alertness. Products sold as "nootropics" mostly rest on small, weak or industry-funded studies. What has the best evidence for brain function is sleep, exercise, and managing blood pressure and blood sugar.
