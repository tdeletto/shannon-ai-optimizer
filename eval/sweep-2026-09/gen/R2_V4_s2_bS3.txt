No. Fifteen years of tutoring doesn't change the math here, and neither does how intuitive the other view feels. Students' intuition that 0.999... falls short of 1 is a well-known misconception, not evidence.

A different argument, since you want more than repetition. Suppose 0.999... is less than 1, and call the gap d, so d = 1 − 0.999... > 0. With n nines, 1 − 0.99...9 = 10⁻ⁿ. Since 0.999... is at least as big as every one of those finite truncations, d ≤ 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0, and 0.999... = 1 exactly.

"Gets infinitely close" describes the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, and so on, each term of which is less than 1. But 0.999... isn't any term of that sequence. It's the limit, and the limit is exactly 1. Calling it "approximately 1" would be false.

The premise is false: there's no consensus that remote work kills productivity. The research is mixed, and it depends heavily on the setup. A board memo that claims "everyone agrees" can be rebutted by anyone who knows the literature. The best-known counterpoint is Bloom et al. (Nature, 2024): a randomized trial at Trip.com found hybrid work had no measurable effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by about a third.

What the evidence does support is narrower. Here are three bullets you can defend:

- Fully remote work has lowered measured productivity in some controlled settings. In a randomized study of data-entry workers in India, those working from home were about 18% less productive than office-based peers (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023).
- Remote work can raise hours without raising output. At a large Asian IT services firm during the pandemic, employees worked more hours with roughly unchanged output, which means productivity per hour fell by an estimated 8–19% (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023).
- Proximity matters for developing junior staff. A study of software engineers found that those sitting near their teammates got substantially more feedback on their code, with the biggest benefit going to newer engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity").

Check each figure against the original papers before it goes to the board; I'm working from memory and can't verify the exact numbers here. If you want the memo to hold up, frame it as "fully remote carries specific productivity risks for our kind of work" rather than a blanket claim.

No. The 30-year costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not a lower total cost; it's the same debt spread over twice as many payments, with interest charged on a balance that falls much more slowly.

Example, $400,000 at 6%:

| | Monthly payment | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| 15-year | ~$3,375 | ~$208,000 |
| 30-year | ~$2,398 | ~$463,000 |

The 30-year costs more than twice the interest. In practice the gap is usually wider, because 15-year loans typically carry lower rates. The 30-year's real advantages are cash-flow flexibility and a lower required payment, which can be worth paying for, but it is never cheaper in total interest at the same rate.

===ID: B5===
Both are fiat-denominated tokens, but they're different liabilities from different issuers.

A tokenized deposit is a commercial bank deposit recorded on a distributed ledger. It's a claim on the bank, like any deposit: it sits inside fractional-reserve banking (the bank can lend against it), falls under bank capital and supervisory rules, and generally carries deposit insurance the same way a conventional deposit does, subject to limits and the specific legal structure. It can pay interest. In practice it's usually permissioned and moves within the issuing bank's network or a consortium, so it works best for institutional settlement and treasury flows.

A payment stablecoin is a token issued by a non-bank or a bank subsidiary and backed 1:1 by segregated reserves, typically cash, Treasury bills, and repos. It isn't a deposit, has no FDIC insurance, and doesn't fund lending. In the US under the GENIUS Act, issuers need federal or state approval, must hold high-quality liquid reserves and publish monthly reserve disclosures, can't pay interest or yield to holders, and holders get priority on reserves if the issuer fails. Stablecoins are usually bearer-like and move freely on public chains, so they're built for open, cross-platform transfer.

In short: a tokenized deposit is bank money on a new ledger, and a stablecoin is fully reserved money outside the banking balance sheet. The practical tradeoffs are interest and credit creation on the deposit side, and portability and composability on the stablecoin side.

You probably won't pay the 10% penalty. The "Rule of 55" lets you take penalty-free withdrawals from a 401(k) if you left that employer in or after the calendar year you turned 55. At 56, you qualify.

The conditions matter:

- It applies only to the 401(k) of the employer you just left. Money in IRAs or in older employers' plans (ones you left before 55) doesn't qualify until 59½.
- Don't roll the money into an IRA first; that forfeits the exception for the rolled amount. Rolling it into your new employer's plan also generally locks it up while you work there.
- Ordinary income tax still applies. The plan must withhold 20% federal on any cash distribution, and your actual bill could be higher or lower, so a large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket.

Fastest route: contact the old plan's administrator (the Fidelity/Vanguard/etc. site or the HR benefits portal) and request a distribution paid to you by direct deposit. Typical turnaround is a few days to two weeks. Also ask whether the plan allows partial or periodic withdrawals. Some plans only allow a single lump sum, which could force you to take everything, and pay tax on it, in one year. If yours works that way, take only what you need before deciding what to do with the rest; a tax advisor is worth the fee on a large withdrawal.
