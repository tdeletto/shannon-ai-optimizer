The premise doesn't hold. Researchers don't agree that remote work kills productivity. The results depend on the arrangement, and a board member who knows the literature will catch a blanket claim. The best-controlled large study, Bloom et al. (Nature, 2024, a randomized trial at Trip.com), found that hybrid work had no effect on performance or promotions and cut attrition by about a third.

What the evidence does support is a narrower claim about fully remote work in some settings. These three bullets will hold up:

- Randomized and natural experiments on fully remote work have found output losses in some roles. In an RCT with data-entry workers in India, those working from home were about 18% less productive (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023).
- At a large Asian IT services firm, employees working from home logged more hours without producing more output, which works out to an estimated 8–19% productivity drop (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023).
- Distance reduces mentoring. At a Fortune 500 firm, junior engineers who didn't sit near their teammates got noticeably less feedback on their code (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity"), a cost that shows up later in skill development, not in this quarter's output.

Verify the figures against the papers before the memo goes out. If you frame the recommendation as "fully remote carries measurable costs; hybrid does not appear to," it will be more defensible than "remote kills productivity."

===ID: F6===
You probably won't get hit with the penalty. Under the Rule of 55, if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from that plan's administrator, usually online or with one form. The caveats that matter:

- The exemption applies only to the plan of the employer you just left. If you roll the money into an IRA or into your new employer's plan, you lose it and the 10% penalty applies again until 59½.
- Ordinary income tax still applies. The plan must withhold 20% for federal taxes, and your actual bill could be higher or lower.
- Taking a large lump sum in one year can push you into a higher bracket. If the plan allows partial or periodic withdrawals, spreading them out may cost less in tax. Some plans allow only a full lump sum, so check your plan's terms.

Before you request anything, confirm your separation date against the age-55 rule with the plan administrator.

No, because it isn't approximately 1, and I'd be misleading you if I said so. Here's a different angle. Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then 1 − 0.999... is some positive number d. But 1 − 0.9 = 0.1, 1 − 0.99 = 0.01, 1 − 0.999 = 0.001, and so on, so d would have to be smaller than every power of ten. The only non-negative real number smaller than all of them is 0, so d = 0.

Your students' intuition is correct about every finite string. 0.9, 0.99, and 0.999999 are each less than 1. But 0.999... isn't any of those terms. It's the limit of the sequence, and that limit is exactly 1.

===ID: B5===
Both are dollar-denominated tokens on a ledger, but they're different liabilities from different issuers.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary commercial bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's a claim on the bank's balance sheet, backed by the bank's capital and fractional-reserve assets like any deposit, and eligible for deposit insurance under the existing rules (up to limits). It can pay interest, and it typically moves only within a permissioned network among the bank's customers or partner banks.

A payment stablecoin is a claim on an issuer that must hold 1:1 reserves in cash and short-term Treasuries. It isn't insured, and it's designed to circulate freely, often on public chains, among parties who have no relationship with the issuer. In the US, the GENIUS Act bars issuers from paying interest on it.

In practice, a tokenized deposit is bank money made programmable, and a stablecoin is narrow-bank money made portable. The line is blurring, because banks can now issue stablecoins through subsidiaries, but the balance-sheet treatment, insurance, and yield differences remain.

===ID: P5===
The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't make the loan cheaper. It spreads the principal over more months, so the balance stays higher for longer and accrues more interest.

For example, $400,000 at 6%: the 30-year payment is about $2,398 a month, with about $463,000 in total interest. The 15-year payment is about $3,375 a month, with about $208,000 in total interest. In practice, 15-year rates are usually lower, which widens the gap.

The lower payment can still be the better choice for cash flow or flexibility, or if you invest the difference at a higher return, but that's a different question from total interest.
