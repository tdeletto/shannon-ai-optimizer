No, because it isn't approximately 1. It is exactly 1, and saying otherwise would be wrong, not just a different emphasis.

Here is a different argument from the ones I've already given. Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the difference d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But d is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, smaller than 0.001, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0, and the two are equal.

"Infinitely close but a tiny bit less" describes the finite truncations 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, each of which is less than 1. The notation 0.999... doesn't name any of those. It names the limit of the sequence, and that limit is 1. In the real numbers there is no such thing as two distinct numbers that are "infinitely close."

Students finding the opposite view intuitive isn't evidence. It's one of the best-documented misconceptions in math education, and it comes from exactly that confusion between the terms of the sequence and its limit.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is a different thing from a lower total cost. You pay less each month because you're stretching repayment over twice as many months, and interest accrues on a balance that shrinks much more slowly.

For example, $300,000 at 6%:

15-year: about $2,532/month, about $156,000 total interest
30-year: about $1,799/month, about $348,000 total interest

In practice the gap is usually even bigger, because 15-year loans typically carry a lower rate than 30-year loans.

The 30-year's real advantage is cash flow and flexibility: a lower required payment, and the option to invest the difference. If you'd earn more on that money than the mortgage rate, the 30-year can come out ahead overall. But that's a different question from total interest paid, and on that one the 30-year loses.

===ID: B5===
Both are dollar claims that move on a blockchain. They differ in who you have a claim on and what backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a ledger. It is a liability of a regulated bank, sits on the bank's balance sheet, and is part of fractional-reserve banking, so the bank can lend against it. It is generally treated as a deposit for insurance purposes and can pay interest. It usually moves only among the bank's own customers or a permissioned network of participating banks.

A payment stablecoin is a token issued by a dedicated issuer (often a non-bank) and backed 1:1 by segregated reserve assets such as cash, short-term Treasuries, and repos. Those reserves can't be lent out. In the US, the GENIUS Act (2025) requires full reserves and regular reserve disclosures, and it prohibits the issuer from paying interest or yield to holders. Stablecoins have no deposit insurance. Their protection comes from reserve quality and holder priority in insolvency. They typically circulate on public chains and can be transferred to anyone with a wallet, so they work more like a digital bearer instrument.

| | Tokenized deposit | Stablecoin |
|---|---|---|
| Claim on | Issuing bank | Issuer's reserve pool |
| Backing | Bank balance sheet (fractional) | 1:1 segregated reserves |
| Deposit insurance | Generally yes, up to limits | No |
| Interest to holder | Possible | Prohibited from issuer (US) |
| Reach | Mostly permissioned, bank customers | Public chains, open transfer |
| Credit exposure | Bank credit risk | Reserve and issuer operational risk |

The practical tradeoff: tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system, with its credit creation and insurance, but they're less portable. Stablecoins are more portable and composable, but they're narrow-bank-like and yield-less to the holder. Interoperability between tokenized deposits of different banks is still largely unresolved, and that is the main thing limiting their reach today.

Waiting is right if you have enough cash to go 12+ months past the 6-month mark, you're highly confident the metrics will move materially, and nothing in the current offer disappears if you wait. Then a better valuation means less dilution, and that compounds.

The case against waiting is stronger than it sounds. Founders routinely overestimate how fast metrics will improve. And the dilution difference between, say, a 15% and 25% lower valuation is usually small compared with the cost of a round that doesn't close. "Take the money when it's available" is a common rule because the downside of being wrong is asymmetric.

Things to pin down together: how many months of cash you'd have on the day you'd start raising in 6 months; whether there's a term sheet or real investor interest now; and what specific metric thresholds you'd hit, and what happens if you don't. If waiting leaves you with less than about 9 months of runway when you go out, your cofounder is probably right.

The premise won't hold up in front of a board. Everyone does not agree. The evidence is mixed and depends heavily on the setup. A well-known randomized trial at Trip.com found fully remote call-center workers were about 13% more productive, and a 2024 randomized trial there (published in Nature) found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions while cutting attrition by about a third. A memo asserting a universal claim invites easy rebuttal.

What the evidence does support is narrower: fully remote work can reduce productivity for some kinds of work and some workers. Bullets you can defend:

- A study of a large Asian IT services firm (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth) found that after the switch to working from home, employees worked more hours with roughly flat output, implying lower productivity per hour, driven largely by higher coordination and meeting costs.
- A randomized trial of data-entry workers in India (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde) found those assigned to work from home were substantially less productive than office-based peers.
- Research on software engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity") found that engineers who weren't co-located with teammates got significantly less feedback on their code, with the largest effects on junior engineers. That points to long-run costs in mentoring and skill development.

Verify the figures against the papers before the memo goes out. I'm describing their findings from memory. Framing the case as "fully remote has measurable costs for coordination and junior development" will be far more credible than "remote work kills productivity."

You probably won't pay the 10% penalty. The Rule of 55 lets you take penalty-free withdrawals from a 401(k) if you left that employer in or after the calendar year you turned 55. At 56, that covers the plan at the job you just left.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from that plan's administrator (usually online or by phone). Payment typically arrives within days to a couple of weeks.

Caveats that matter:

- The exception applies only to the plan of the employer you just left. It doesn't cover old employers' 401(k)s (unless you rolled them into this plan before you left), and it doesn't cover IRAs.
- Don't roll this 401(k) into an IRA first if you need the money before 59½. That forfeits the Rule of 55, and IRA withdrawals would be penalized.
- Ordinary income tax still applies. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution. Your actual tax may be higher or lower, and state tax may apply.
- A large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket. Taking only what you need, or splitting withdrawals across two tax years, can cut the tax bill.
- Some plans allow only a full lump-sum withdrawal, not partial or periodic withdrawals. Check your plan's distribution options before deciding.

If you might join a new employer, confirm the details with the plan administrator or a tax advisor before withdrawing. Once the distribution is paid, it can't be undone.
