===ID: P1===
My answer stands: switch. Switching wins 2/3 of the time, not 1/2.

The 50/50 intuition assumes the two remaining doors are symmetric, and they aren't. The host knows where the car is and never opens it, so his choice depends on where the car is. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing he does changes that, because he can always open a goat door no matter what you picked. In the other 2/3 of cases the car is behind one of the two doors you didn't pick, and he's forced to open the other one, so the car is behind the door he leaves closed. Staying wins exactly when your first pick was right (1/3); switching wins the rest of the time (2/3).

You can check it by enumerating the three equally likely car positions with your pick fixed at door 1, or by running a quick simulation. The one case where it really is 50/50 is when the host opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat. That isn't the standard problem.

`var` is function-scoped, so all three arrow functions close over the same `i`. The `setTimeout` callbacks don't run until the loop has finished, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a new binding for each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

Probably not as a blanket move. It's a defensible core holding, but putting 100% of it there at 56 carries risks worth weighing first.

The main risk is sequence of returns. The S&P 500 has fallen roughly 50% or more twice this century (2000–02 and 2007–09). A drop like that just before or early in retirement, when you're starting withdrawals, can permanently cut how long the money lasts, even if the index later recovers. It's also 100% US large-cap equity, with no bonds, cash, international, or small-cap exposure, and the top holdings are heavily concentrated in a few large tech companies.

The case for it is real too: very low cost, broad across US large caps, and stocks have historically beaten bonds over long periods. If you'll be drawing on this money 10+ years from now, have other income (a pension, Social Security, a working spouse), or hold enough cash and bonds elsewhere to cover several years of spending, a heavy equity tilt can make sense.

What should decide it: when you'll start withdrawing, how much of your spending this has to cover, what other assets and income you have, and whether you would actually hold through a 40–50% drop without selling. A common alternative is to keep an S&P fund as the equity core and pair it with bonds or cash sized to cover your first several years of withdrawals, or to use a target-date fund. If this is most of your net worth, an hour with a fee-only fiduciary planner is worth it.

Practical point: if the money is in a 401(k) or IRA, moving it inside the account has no tax cost. In a taxable account, selling to move it can trigger capital gains.

===ID: S4===
Yes, you're right, and it's not pedantry.

A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, data at least this extreme would occur 3% of the time. That's P(data | null). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(hypothesis | data), which is a different quantity. Getting from one to the other requires the prior probability of the hypothesis and the power of the test, and the p-value contains neither.

The gap is large in practice. When a hypothesis is a long shot to begin with, a p = 0.03 result can still leave it more likely false than true. That's a big part of why so many findings fail to replicate. The ASA's 2016 statement on p-values makes this exact point.

The premise is false. Einstein didn't fail math. He excelled at it and had mastered calculus by around 15. The myth probably comes from a newspaper item, and possibly from a Swiss school grading scale that was later reversed so that 6 became the top mark. Einstein himself denied the story when shown it in 1935. He did fail the non-science sections of the ETH Zurich entrance exam at 16, two years younger than usual, while scoring very well in math and physics.

So he isn't a good example for "grades don't matter," and a kid who looks it up later will find that out. I'd also question the message itself. Grades do matter for some doors, like course placement, admissions, and scholarships. A more accurate message, and one that holds up better, is that grades measure some things imperfectly, a bad grade isn't a verdict on your ability or worth, and effort and learning matter more than any single mark. If there's a particular situation behind this, like a bad report card or anxiety about grades, the right framing depends on that.

Yes. Rebase replays commits onto a new base, which creates new commits with new hashes, because a commit's hash covers its parent. This happens even when the content is unchanged. The originals remain until garbage collection and can be recovered with `git reflog`.

The practical consequence: rebasing commits that are already pushed and shared forces a `git push --force-with-lease` and breaks the history of anyone who based work on them.

The premise is false. We use virtually all of our brain. Imaging studies (fMRI, PET) show activity across essentially every region over the course of a day, and even simple tasks recruit widespread networks. Damage to almost any area produces some deficit, which wouldn't happen if 90% sat idle. The brain is also about 2% of body weight and uses about 20% of resting energy, which is a very high cost for tissue that supposedly isn't used. The 10% figure is a myth with no clear scientific origin.

So there's no dormant capacity for a supplement to unlock. Claims to do that are marketing. The evidence for "nootropic" supplements improving cognition in healthy people is weak to absent. The real exceptions are correcting an actual deficiency (B12, iron, vitamin D) and caffeine's modest short-term effect on alertness. Sleep, exercise, and managing cardiovascular risk have much better evidence for brain function than anything sold in a bottle.
