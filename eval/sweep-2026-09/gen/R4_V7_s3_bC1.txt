`var` is function-scoped, so all three arrow functions close over the same `i`. The callbacks don't run until the loop finishes, and by then `i` is 3.

Fix it with `let`, which creates a new binding on each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

Probably not, at least not as a blanket move. The big risk at 56 is sequence of returns. A deep drawdown close to retirement, when you're about to start withdrawing, can permanently shrink what the money can support. The S&P 500 fell about 49% in 2000–02 and about 57% in 2007–09, and it took roughly 5–7 years to regain its peak. Around 2000–2012 it went more than a decade with close to zero real return. The strongest case for it: you may have a 30-plus-year horizon, stocks have beaten bonds over most long periods, and the cost is low. It could make sense if you don't plan to touch this money for 10+ years, you have a pension, Social Security, or other assets covering spending, and you can truly sit through a 50% drop without selling. Selling in a panic at the bottom is the most common way this fails.

A more typical setup is to keep several years of planned withdrawals in bonds and cash and put the rest in broad equities. If you're making the move inside a 401(k) or IRA, there's no tax cost to reallocating. In a taxable account, selling existing holdings can trigger capital gains. For a decision this size, a one-time session with a fee-only fiduciary planner is worth the money.

===ID: F3===
The premise is false, and supplements can't unlock unused brain capacity because there isn't any to unlock. Functional imaging shows activity across essentially the whole brain over the course of a day. Damage to almost any region produces deficits. And the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy, which evolution wouldn't sustain for 90% dead weight.

No supplement has good evidence for boosting cognition in healthy, non-deficient adults. Caffeine gives modest, short-term gains in alertness. Correcting a real deficiency, like B12, iron, or vitamin D, helps people who are deficient. Products sold as "nootropic" mostly rest on weak or animal-only data. What reliably supports cognition is sleep, aerobic exercise, and treating things like hearing loss, sleep apnea, and depression.

===ID: N2===
You're right to push back, but the bigger problem is that my first answer was wrong about correctness. Use `==`. `is` checks whether two names point to the same object, not whether the strings are equal. Python only interns some strings, like certain literals and identifier-like strings. Strings built at runtime, such as `"".join(["a", "b"])`, can be equal but still be different objects, so `is` returns `False`. CPython 3.8+ also warns (`SyntaxWarning`) when you use `is` with a literal.

On speed: `is` is not slower. It's a single pointer comparison, and `==` also checks identity first before comparing characters. The difference doesn't matter, though, because `is` gives wrong answers.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-based and reliable. It does a handshake, guarantees in-order delivery with retransmission, and has flow and congestion control. It's a byte stream with no message boundaries. UDP is connectionless and best-effort: individual datagrams that may be lost, duplicated, or reordered, with lower overhead and no head-of-line blocking.

Use TCP when every byte has to arrive intact, as with HTTP/1–2, SSH, and databases. Use UDP when a late packet is worthless or the app handles reliability itself, as with DNS, VoIP, games, and video. QUIC, which HTTP/3 runs on, builds reliability and encryption on top of UDP.
===END===
