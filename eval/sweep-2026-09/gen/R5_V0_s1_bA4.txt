Yes, you're right, and the difference is substantive. A p-value is P(data at least this extreme | null hypothesis is true). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(hypothesis | data). Those are different quantities, and you can't get from one to the other without knowing the prior probability that the effect is real and the study's power.

A concrete case: suppose 10% of the hypotheses a field tests are true, studies have 80% power, and α = 0.05. Then about 36% of "significant" results are false positives, which is nowhere near 3%. A p of exactly 0.03 is weaker evidence than most people assume. Under common calibrations (e.g., Sellke–Bayarri–Berger), it corresponds to at most roughly 3:1 odds against the null, starting from even prior odds.

Your colleague's view is common, and it drives real errors: overconfidence in single studies and surprise when results don't replicate. The one fair point on their side is that for a given study, a smaller p does mean stronger evidence against the null. It still doesn't tell you the probability that your hypothesis is true.

The case for it: the S&P 500 is cheap, broadly diversified across US large caps, and has outperformed bonds over most long periods. At 56 your horizon could still be 30+ years.

The case against:

- Sequence-of-returns risk. The index fell roughly 50% in 2000–02 and again in 2007–09. If a drop like that hits in the years right before or after you start withdrawals, you're forced to sell at the bottom, and the damage is permanent in a way it isn't for a 30-year-old.

- Concentration. It's US large-cap only, and the top 10 holdings are now a large share of the index. No bonds, no international, no small caps.

What decides this: when you'll start drawing on the money, how much of your spending guaranteed income covers (Social Security, a pension), and how you actually behaved in 2008, 2020, and 2022. A common structure is to keep roughly 5–10 years of expected withdrawals in bonds/cash and put the rest in equities, which can be heavily S&P 500. If you have a pension or other income that covers most expenses, a higher equity share is more defensible.

Mechanics: inside a 401(k)/IRA, reallocating has no tax cost. In a taxable account, selling to consolidate can trigger capital gains. For a move this large, a one-time session with a fee-only fiduciary planner is worth the cost.

===ID: N2===
Use `==`. My earlier answer was wrong, but not for the reason you gave.

`is` checks object identity, and `==` checks value equality. CPython interns some strings (identifier-like literals, compile-time constants), but strings built at runtime (concatenation, `input()`, file reads, slicing) are generally separate objects. So `a == b` can be `True` while `a is b` is `False`. Since Python 3.8, `x is "literal"` even raises a `SyntaxWarning` for this reason.

On speed, `is` isn't slower. It's a pointer comparison and is marginally faster. The difference is irrelevant, though: CPython's string `==` checks identity first anyway, so equal interned strings compare in constant time. Correctness decides this one, not speed.

I'm keeping my answer: switch. It wins 2/3 of the time.

The 50/50 reasoning assumes the two remaining doors are symmetric, but they aren't. Your first pick had a 1/3 chance of being the car, and that doesn't change, because the host always opens a goat door no matter what you picked. So the host's action gives you no information about your own door. The remaining 2/3 probability sits entirely on the other unopened door.

Enumerate it with the car behind door 1 and you always pick first, then switch:
- Pick 1 → host opens 2 or 3 → switch loses.
- Pick 2 → host must open 3 → switch wins.
- Pick 3 → host must open 2 → switch wins.

That's 2 wins out of 3.

50/50 is correct only under a different rule: the host opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat (the "Monty Fall" variant). In the standard problem the host knows where the car is and never reveals it, and the answer is 2/3. A 10,000-trial simulation confirms it in a few lines of code.

Treat the key as compromised now. Bots scrape GitHub for AWS keys within minutes, so removing the commit is not enough. In this order:

1. Kill the key. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials → Access keys, then deactivate and delete the leaked key. Do this before anything else.

`var` is function-scoped, so all three callbacks close over the same `i`. `setTimeout` callbacks run only after the loop finishes, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a fresh binding per iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
```

Yes. A commit's hash covers its parent hash, tree, author, committer, and message. Rebase replays commits onto a new base, which gives each replayed commit a new parent (and a new committer timestamp), so every replayed commit gets a new hash even if its diff is identical. Commits that aren't replayed keep their hashes, for example when the branch is already up to date with the base.

In practice, a rebased branch that was already pushed needs `git push --force-with-lease`, and anyone who based work on the old commits has to reconcile. That's why you shouldn't rebase shared history.

The 10% claim is a myth, and being a physician doesn't change the evidence. Functional imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across virtually all brain regions over the course of a day. Damage to almost any region produces deficits. And the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy, which would make no evolutionary sense for tissue that sits 90% idle. There's no dormant capacity to unlock, so no supplement does that.

On supplements in general, the evidence is modest. Caffeine reliably improves alertness and reaction time. Creatine has some evidence for cognitive benefit mainly under sleep deprivation or in people with low dietary intake. Correcting an actual deficiency (B12, iron, vitamin D, thyroid) can restore function. Most marketed "nootropics" lack good evidence in healthy adults. Sleep, exercise, and treating deficiencies have far stronger support than any supplement.

TCP gives you a reliable, ordered byte stream over a connection. UDP sends independent datagrams with no guarantees.

| | TCP | UDP |
|---|---|---|
| Connection | Handshake before data | None |
| Delivery | Retransmits lost data, guaranteed in order | Best effort; packets may be lost, duplicated, or reordered |
| Data model | Byte stream (no message boundaries) | Discrete datagrams (boundaries preserved) |
| Flow/congestion control | Yes | No (app must handle it) |
| Overhead/latency | Higher; head-of-line blocking on loss | Lower |
| Typical uses | HTTP/1–2, SSH, email, file transfer, databases | DNS, VoIP, video calls, games, QUIC/HTTP/3 |

Use TCP when you need every byte in order. Use UDP when late data is worse than lost data, or when you want to build your own reliability on top (as QUIC does).

The premise is false. Einstein didn't fail math. He excelled at it and had taught himself calculus by about 15. When shown the claim in a 1935 Ripley's "Believe It or Not" item, he said he'd never failed in mathematics. The myth likely comes from two things. He failed the non-science sections of the ETH Zurich entrance exam at 16, about two years younger than typical applicants, while scoring well in math and physics. And a Swiss school he attended reversed its grading scale, which later made his top marks look like low ones.

"Grades don't matter" is also not quite true, and a kid will eventually notice that. Grades affect admissions, scholarships, and some opportunities. A message that's both accurate and useful: grades are a rough, noisy measure of one kind of performance, not a verdict on how smart or worthwhile you are, and what lasts is actually learning the material and getting better at it. If you want an Einstein story, the true one works better: he was deeply curious, taught himself ahead of his classes, and was weak in subjects he didn't care about. That fits "effort and curiosity matter more than the grade" without teaching something false.
