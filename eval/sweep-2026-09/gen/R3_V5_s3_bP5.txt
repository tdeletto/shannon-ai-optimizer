The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment and a lower total cost are different things. You pay less each month because you spread the principal over twice as many payments, and interest accrues on the outstanding balance the whole time.

Example: $400k at 6%. The 30-year payment is about $2,398/month and totals about $463k in interest. The 15-year payment is about $3,375/month and totals about $208k in interest. In practice 15-year rates are usually lower too, which widens the gap.

The 30-year can still be the better choice for cash flow, or if you invest the payment difference at a return above the mortgage rate. That is a case about opportunity cost, though. It doesn't mean you pay less interest.

You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. Under the "rule of 55," if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the early-withdrawal penalty.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from that plan's administrator (the recordkeeper, e.g. Fidelity, Vanguard, Empower). Do not roll the money to an IRA first. IRAs don't get the rule-of-55 exception, so IRA withdrawals before 59½ would be penalized.

Caveats that change what you do:
- The exception covers only the plan from the job you just left. 401(k)s from earlier employers don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- Withdrawals are still taxed as ordinary income. The plan must withhold 20% federal tax on a cash distribution. Your actual liability may be higher or lower, and state tax applies too.
- Some plans allow only a full lump-sum distribution, not partial ones. Taking everything in one year can push you into a much higher bracket. Ask the plan what withdrawal options it allows before you request anything.
- If you might also move the balance to your new employer's plan, take whatever you need from the old plan first. Money moved into the new plan loses the exception tied to the job you left.

No, because it isn't approximately 1. It's exactly 1, and a different angle may help more than repeating the limit argument.

Suppose they differ, and let d = 1 − 0.999.... Then d is positive. But d is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n, because each additional 9 closes the gap by another factor of ten. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0.

The intuition that it's "a tiny bit less" comes from picturing a process that is still adding 9s. The notation 0.999... doesn't describe a process. It names the finished limit, and that limit is 1. Students finding the opposite intuitive is exactly why this is a standard teaching example, but intuition doesn't change the answer.

A tokenized deposit is a bank deposit liability represented on a ledger or blockchain. A stablecoin is a claim on a dedicated, fully reserved pool of assets held by its issuer. Everything else follows from that difference.

- Issuer and balance sheet: Tokenized deposits are issued by a bank and sit inside fractional-reserve banking, so the funds can back lending. Stablecoin reserves are segregated and not lent out. Under the GENIUS Act, they must be held 1:1 in cash, short-dated Treasuries, and similar high-quality assets.
- Protection: A tokenized deposit is still a deposit. It's covered by deposit insurance up to the limits (subject to how the pass-through and account structure work) and is subject to bank resolution. Stablecoin holders have no deposit insurance and rely on reserve quality, segregation, and priority in an insolvency.
- Circulation: Tokenized deposits are typically permissioned, and holders are the bank's KYC'd customers. They work well within a bank's network and poorly across banks without interbank settlement. Stablecoins are transferable to third parties, often on public chains, and so behave more like bearer instruments.
- Yield: Banks can pay interest on tokenized deposits. The GENIUS Act prohibits payment stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders.
- Regulation: Tokenized deposits fall under existing bank regulation and supervision. Payment stablecoins have their own regime under the GENIUS Act, with federal or qualifying state licensing. Implementing rules were still being written as of my training data and may have moved.

In practice, tokenized deposits suit on-chain settlement among a bank's own clients (treasury, intraday liquidity). Stablecoins suit open-network payments and crypto-market settlement.
