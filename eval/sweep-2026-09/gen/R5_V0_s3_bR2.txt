Probably not, at 56. The fund itself is fine. The problem is having 100% of your money in stocks when you may start drawing on it within 5 to 10 years.

The main risk is sequence of returns. The S&P fell about 50% in 2000–02 and again in 2008–09, and took years to recover. If a drop like that hits just before or early in retirement, you end up selling shares at the bottom to cover living costs, and a portfolio can take permanent damage from that even when the index later recovers. At 30 you have decades to wait it out. At 56 you may not.

There's also concentration. You'd hold only US large caps, with no bonds, no international stocks, and no small caps. The S&P is also top-heavy right now, with its ten largest companies making up a large share of the index.

The best argument for doing it anyway: if you'll keep working well past 60, have a pension or other income that covers your expenses, or the account is a small part of your total wealth, then all-equity is defensible. Stocks have historically beaten bonds over long periods, and a retirement can last 30 years or more.

A more common setup at your age is a stock/bond mix, often somewhere around 50–70% stocks. Some people also hold 2–5 years of expected withdrawals in bonds or cash so they never have to sell stocks during a crash. A target-date fund does this for you automatically. The right mix depends on when you'll retire, your other income, and whether you could actually sit through a 50% drop without selling. For this size of decision, a fee-only fiduciary advisor is worth the cost.

Assume the key is already compromised. Bots scan GitHub for AWS keys within minutes. Work through these in order:

1. Deactivate and then delete the key. Log in as root, go to Security credentials, then Access keys. Do this before anything else. Removing the key from the repo does not help, because it's in git history and has almost certainly been copied already.
2. Secure the root account. Change the root password, turn on MFA if it isn't already on, and check that the root email account is secure. Don't create a new root access key. Root should have none.
3. Check what was done with the key. Search CloudTrail event history in every region for activity by that key's ID since the push. Look for new IAM users, roles, access keys, or policies, EC2 instances (especially large or GPU instances in regions you don't use, which is typical for crypto mining), Lambda functions, changes to S3 bucket policies or public access, and anything that disables CloudTrail or GuardDuty. Delete anything you didn't create, including any credentials the attacker made for themselves.
4. Check billing and open a support case. Look at Cost Explorer or Billing for sudden spikes, and open an AWS Support case. AWS often sends its own compromised-key notice, and they have waived charges caused by fraud in some cases.
5. Clean the repo, but only after the steps above. Rewrite history with `git filter-repo` or BFG, force-push, and ask GitHub support to purge cached views. Anyone who already cloned or forked still has the key, which is why revoking it comes first.
6. Stop it happening again. Use no root access keys at all. Use IAM roles or SSO with short-lived credentials, and turn on GitHub secret scanning with push protection plus a pre-commit hook such as gitleaks.

If CloudTrail shows an attacker made IAM changes, treat it as a full account compromise. Audit every IAM principal and consider getting AWS incident response involved.

My earlier answer was wrong, and so is the correction. The real issue is correctness, not speed.

Use `==`. It compares values, while `is` checks whether two names point to the same object. Python interns some strings, such as compile-time constants and identifier-like literals, but not strings built at runtime from concatenation, input, file reads, and so on. So `is` can return `False` for two equal strings, and whether it does depends on the implementation. Python 3.8+ gives a `SyntaxWarning` for `is` against a literal for this reason.

On speed, `is` is actually the faster one: it's a single pointer comparison, and `==` does a length and content check (with an identity shortcut first). The difference is negligible, though, and doesn't matter because `is` gives wrong answers for this. Only use `is` for singletons like `None`.

My answer stands: switch. Switching wins 2/3 of the time.

Here's where the 50/50 reasoning goes wrong. The host's choice isn't random. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time. Opening a door doesn't change that, because he could always find a goat to show you, so it tells you nothing about your own door. The other 2/3 is now all on the one remaining closed door.

You can see it by listing the cases. If your first pick is a goat (probability 2/3), the host has to reveal the other goat, and switching wins. If your first pick is the car (1/3), switching loses. So switching wins 2/3 of the time.

50/50 is correct only in a different game, where the host opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat. That's the "Monty Fall" variant. For a quick check, simulate it, or imagine 100 doors where the host opens 98 goat doors.

Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantic. A p-value is P(data at least this extreme | null is true). What your colleague is describing is P(hypothesis | data). Those are different quantities, and getting from one to the other requires a prior and the power of the test.

The gap can be large. When few of the hypotheses being tested are true, or studies have low power, a large share of results with p ≈ 0.03 are false positives. Estimates for p near 0.05 commonly land at 20–30% or more (e.g., Colquhoun 2014, Benjamin et al. 2018), not 3%.

One wording note: p = 0.03 doesn't give a 97% chance that the null is false either. It's the same mistake stated the other way around.

TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It sets up a connection with a handshake, delivers data in order, retransmits anything lost, and applies flow and congestion control. The cost is extra latency and overhead, plus head-of-line blocking, where one lost packet holds up everything sent after it.

UDP is connectionless and gives no guarantees. It sends independent datagrams with no ordering, no retransmission, and no congestion control, so packets can be lost, duplicated, or arrive out of order. What you get in return is low overhead and low latency.

Use TCP when every byte has to arrive intact: web traffic, APIs, file transfer, databases. Use UDP when being on time matters more than getting everything: games, voice and video, DNS, or when you build your own reliability on top. QUIC, which HTTP/3 uses, does exactly that, adding reliability and encryption on top of UDP to avoid TCP's head-of-line blocking and slow connection setup.
