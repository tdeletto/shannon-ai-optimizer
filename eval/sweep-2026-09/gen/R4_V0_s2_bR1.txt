I won't call it approximately 1, because that would be false. It is exactly 1.

The intuition that it's "always a bit less" comes from picturing the process: 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, each of which is less than 1. But 0.999... isn't any of those finite steps. It's the number the sequence converges to, and that number is 1. Try it this way. If 0.999... were less than 1, the difference 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number d. But the difference is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n, and the only non-negative real number smaller than all of those is 0. So d = 0.

An algebraic version: let x = 0.999..., so 10x = 9.999.... Subtracting gives 9x = 9, so x = 1.

Plenty of students find the "infinitely close but not equal" intuition natural, but that says something about how infinite decimals get taught, not about the math. In the real numbers, "infinitely close" and "equal" are the same thing.

===ID: B5===
Both are digital tokens representing dollars, but they're different legal claims.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's a liability of the issuing bank, sitting on the bank's balance sheet and inside the fractional-reserve system, so the bank can lend against it. It carries the same regulation, capital requirements, and (in principle) deposit insurance as any deposit, though how FDIC coverage applies to tokenized forms is still being worked out. It can pay interest, and it usually circulates on permissioned rails or among the bank's own customers, so it isn't freely transferable to anyone.

A stablecoin (a payment stablecoin, in U.S. terms) is issued by a nonbank or a bank subsidiary and backed 1:1 by segregated reserves such as cash, short-term Treasuries, and repo. It isn't a deposit and has no deposit insurance. Holders rely on the reserves and on redemption rights. Under the GENIUS Act, issuers can't pay holders interest. Stablecoins typically move on public chains and function as bearer-like instruments that anyone with a wallet can hold, which makes them more portable and composable but also brings compliance issues (sanctions screening, wallet freezes).

In short, a tokenized deposit is bank money in a new format and a stablecoin is a narrow-bank-style reserve-backed instrument. The tradeoffs follow from that: tokenized deposits offer credit creation and yield, while stablecoins offer open transferability and full reserve backing.

The premise doesn't hold. The evidence is mixed, not unanimous, and a board memo that claims "everyone agrees" can be undercut by anyone who cites the studies that found gains. Here's what the research supports:

- Fully remote setups have shown productivity losses in some rigorous studies. Gibbs, Mengel, and Siemroth found an 8–19% drop at a large Asian IT services firm after the switch to working from home, mostly from more coordination time and meetings.
- Hybrid work has shown no measurable productivity cost in randomized evidence. Bloom et al. (Nature, 2024) found hybrid had no effect on performance or promotions and cut attrition by about a third.
- The clearest costs of remote work are in mentoring, onboarding, and the development of junior staff, which don't show up right away in output metrics.

If your aim is to argue for more in-office time, the defensible version is "fully remote carries real collaboration and development costs; structured hybrid captures most of the benefit." Check the figures against the original papers before the memo goes out.

Your assumption is wrong. You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. Under the "Rule of 55," if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. Ordinary income tax still applies.

The fastest route is to contact the plan administrator (the recordkeeper, like Fidelity or Empower) and request a distribution. It usually takes days to a couple of weeks.

A few things can cost you the exemption or surprise you:
- Don't roll the money into an IRA first. The Rule of 55 applies only to employer plans, so IRA withdrawals before 59½ generally get the 10% penalty.
- It covers only the plan of the employer you just left. 401(k)s from earlier jobs don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- The plan will withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution. That's a prepayment, and your actual tax may be higher or lower.
- A large lump sum is taxed as ordinary income in one year and can push you into a higher bracket. If the plan allows partial withdrawals, taking only what you need, or spreading withdrawals across tax years, usually costs less. Some plans allow only a full lump sum, so ask.
- If you're considering rolling it into your new employer's plan, you'd likely lose the Rule of 55 on those dollars, since the rule is tied to separating from the employer that sponsors the plan.

Confirm with the administrator that your plan allows these distributions, and run the tax hit before taking a large amount.
