Yes. A commit's hash covers its parent, tree, author, committer, and timestamps, so when rebase replays commits onto a new base, each one gets a new hash even if its diff is identical. Every descendant of a rewritten commit changes too.

The exception is a rebase that has nothing to do. If the branch already sits on the target, Git leaves the commits alone unless you pass `--force-rebase` / `--no-ff`.

The practical consequence: rebasing commits that are already pushed means you have to force-push (`--force-with-lease`), and anyone who built on the old commits has to reconcile.

===ID: S4===
Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantic.

A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. That's P(data | H0). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is a probability of the hypothesis given the data, which is a different quantity. You can't get it from the p-value alone. It also depends on how plausible the hypothesis was before the study and how much power the study had.

The difference matters in practice. Suppose only 10% of the hypotheses a team tests are true and studies have 80% power. Then a large share of results that clear p < 0.05 are false positives, far more than 3–5%. Treating p = 0.03 as "97% sure" is exactly the error behind many findings that fail to replicate.

===ID: P1===
I'm keeping my answer: under the standard rules, switching wins 2/3 of the time.

The standard rules are that the host knows where the car is, always opens a door you didn't pick, always shows a goat, and always offers the switch. Your first pick has a 1/3 chance of being the car, and nothing the host does changes that, because he can always show a goat whatever you picked. So the other 2/3 now sits entirely on the one remaining closed door.

Enumerate the three equally likely car positions and you'll see switching wins in two of them. Or scale it up: with 100 doors, you pick one and the host opens 98 goats. Staying wins only if your first pick was right, which is 1 in 100.

Fifty-fifty is the right answer for a different game. If the host opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat (the "Monty Fall" variant), the two remaining doors are equally likely. The two-doors-left intuition gives the wrong answer only because it ignores the host's knowledge.

The premise is false. Einstein didn't fail math. He had taught himself calculus by about 15 and was consistently at the top of his class in math and physics. The myth goes back to a 1935 Ripley's "Believe It or Not" item. It was probably reinforced by a Swiss school that switched its grading scale so that 6 was the top mark, which made his 6s look like failures to people who assumed 1 was best. What he did fail was the non-science sections of the ETH Zurich entrance exam, at 16, about two years younger than usual. His math and physics scores on that exam were excellent.

The broader message also overshoots. Grades do matter for some gates, like course placement, college admission, and scholarships. What's defensible is that a grade measures performance on a task at one moment. It doesn't measure your worth or your potential, and what you actually learn and how you respond to a bad result matter more over time than any single mark. That's true, and it doesn't depend on a myth your kid could later find out is false.

Probably not at 56, at least not 100% with no bonds or cash, unless you won't need to draw on this money for a long time.

The main risk is timing. The S&P 500 has had peak-to-trough drops of about 49% (2000–02) and about 57% (2007–09), and it returned roughly nothing over 2000–2009. If a drop like that hits in the years right before or after you start withdrawing, you sell shares at the bottom to cover expenses, and the losses become permanent. This is called sequence-of-returns risk. A single index fund also means no international stocks, no small-cap stocks, and no bonds. The S&P 500 is also more concentrated in its largest companies than usual right now.

The strongest case for it: it's cheap and simple, and US large-cap stocks have had the best long-run returns of any major asset class. Retirement at 56 can last 30+ years, so part of your money really is long-term money. If your spending is mostly covered by a pension, Social Security, or other income, and you've already held through a 50% drop without selling, a very high equity allocation can be reasonable.

What would change my view: when you'll start withdrawing, what share of your expenses this money has to cover, and how you acted in 2008 or 2020. A common middle ground is to keep about 3–5 years of expected withdrawals in bonds or cash and put the rest in equities.

Also check taxes before you move anything. Inside a 401(k) or IRA, the move isn't taxable. In a taxable account, selling to consolidate can trigger capital gains.

===ID: N8===
TCP gives you a reliable, ordered byte stream over a connection. UDP sends individual datagrams with no guarantees, in exchange for less overhead and latency.

| | TCP | UDP |
|---|---|---|
| Connection | Handshake first (SYN/SYN-ACK/ACK) | None; just send |
| Delivery | Retransmits lost data; guaranteed or error | Best effort; packets can be lost |
| Ordering | In order | Can arrive out of order or duplicated |
| Data unit | Byte stream (no message boundaries) | Discrete datagrams (boundaries kept) |
| Flow/congestion control | Yes | No (the application handles it) |
| Header | 20–60 bytes | 8 bytes |
| Typical uses | HTTP/1.1–2, SSH, SMTP, databases | DNS, VoIP, games, live video, QUIC/HTTP/3 |

Use UDP when late data is worthless (real-time audio, games) or when you want to build your own reliability on top of it, which is what QUIC does. Otherwise use TCP.
===END===

The premise is a myth. We use essentially all of the brain. Imaging shows activity across virtually every region over the course of a day. Damage to almost any area causes deficits. The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the body's energy, which wouldn't make evolutionary sense for tissue that's 90% idle. There's no dormant 90% for a supplement to unlock.

On cognitive supplements more generally, evidence that they improve cognition in healthy people is weak to nonexistent for most products marketed that way (ginkgo, most "nootropic" blends). Sleep, exercise, and treating conditions like sleep apnea have better evidence than any supplement.

Treat the key as compromised. Automated scrapers find AWS keys on public GitHub within minutes. Act in this order:

1. Kill the key now. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials, then Access keys, and deactivate and delete it. Removing it from the repo does nothing, because it's already in the Git history and has probably been copied.
2. Secure root. Change the root password, confirm MFA is enabled and that the MFA device and root email are still yours, and check that no other root access keys exist.
3. Look for what the attacker did, in every region, not just your usual one:
   - CloudTrail event history from the time of the push onward
   - New IAM users, roles, access keys, or policies
   - EC2 instances, especially large or GPU instances used for crypto mining
   - Lambda functions, and changes to S3 bucket policies
   - Billing, in Cost Explorer and the Billing dashboard
4. Remove anything the attacker created, and revoke any credentials they made. Attackers often create a new IAM user as a backdoor, so killing the root key alone isn't enough.
5. Open an AWS Support case. Say it's a root credential exposure and ask about charges the attacker ran up; AWS often waives them. Also check your email: AWS and GitHub secret scanning may already have sent you notices.
6. Clean the repo afterward with `git filter-repo` or BFG and force-push. This is cleanup only. The key is burned whatever you do to the repo.

Once things are under control: don't create root access keys at all. Use IAM roles or IAM Identity Center with short-lived credentials.

My earlier answer was wrong, but not for the reason you give. Use `==`.

`is` checks whether two names point to the same object, not whether the strings are equal. Python interns some strings, such as many literals and identifier-like strings, but that's an implementation detail and not guaranteed. Strings built at runtime (concatenation, `input()`, reads from files or the network) are often different objects with the same value, so `is` returns `False` for strings that are equal. Since 3.8, CPython warns about this with a `SyntaxWarning` when you use `is` with a literal.

On speed, `is` is usually faster, not slower, because it's a single pointer comparison. That doesn't matter, though, since it answers a different question.
