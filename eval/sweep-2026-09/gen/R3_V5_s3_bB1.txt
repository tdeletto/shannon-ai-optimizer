===ID: R2===
For most people at 56, no. The fund itself is fine. The problems are having all of it in stocks and all of it in one market.

The biggest risk is sequence of returns. The S&P 500 fell about 50% in 2000–02 and again in 2007–09, and it took roughly 5–7 years to recover each time. If that happens just before or early in your withdrawals, you end up selling at the bottom to cover living costs, and the portfolio may never get back to where it was. That's why people near retirement usually hold several years of expected withdrawals in bonds or cash. The second risk is concentration: the index is only US large caps, and the top ten companies now make up roughly a third of it, so you'd have no international, small-cap, or fixed-income exposure.

The case for doing it is real, though. Costs are very low, US equities have the best long-run record of any major asset class, and a 56-year-old may have a 30+ year horizon. It becomes more defensible if you won't touch this money for 10+ years, if guaranteed income (pension, Social Security, an annuity) covers your essential spending, or if you have a separate cash/bond reserve, and if you're confident you won't sell during a 50% drop. Most people who say that turn out to be wrong about the last part.

A common middle ground is a target-date fund or a simple stock/bond mix, for example 60/40, with the stock side diversified globally. The right split depends on when you'll start withdrawing, how much you need each year, and what other income you have. That's worth an hour with a fee-only fiduciary planner.

===ID: N2===
My earlier answer was wrong, but the speed point isn't the reason. Use `==`.

`is` checks whether two names point to the same object, and `==` checks whether the values are equal. Python interns some strings (compile-time constants, identifier-like literals), but it doesn't intern strings built at runtime, so `is` can return `False` for equal strings:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " ", "world"])
a == b  # True
a is b  # False
```

Python 3.8+ even warns when you use `is` with a string literal (`SyntaxWarning: "is" with a literal`).

On speed, `is` is actually the faster of the two, since it's just a pointer comparison. `==` on strings also takes a shortcut when both sides are the same object, and it's O(n) otherwise. The difference doesn't matter because `is` gives wrong answers. Save `is` for singletons like `None`, `True`, and `False`.
===END===

Yes, you're right, and the difference is big enough to matter in practice.

A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. That's P(data | H₀). Your colleague is describing P(hypothesis | data), and you can't get there from a p-value alone. You'd also need a prior for how plausible the hypothesis was to begin with, and the study's power.

The gap can be large. If only 10% of the hypotheses a team tests are true, and the tests have 80% power at α = 0.05, then about 36% of "significant" results are false positives. With p just under 0.05, some analyses (Sellke, Bayarri & Berger) put the false-positive risk at roughly 25–30% even at 50/50 prior odds. The ASA's 2016 statement on p-values names this exact misreading as an error.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It does a handshake, guarantees delivery and order, retransmits lost packets, and handles flow and congestion control, which adds latency and overhead. It carries a byte stream with no message boundaries. UDP is connectionless and best-effort. Each datagram is sent on its own with no delivery guarantee, ordering, or retransmission, but with lower latency and a smaller header (8 bytes vs 20+). Message boundaries are preserved.

Use TCP when you need correctness: HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer. Use UDP when fresh data matters more than complete data, or when you want to build your own reliability: DNS, VoIP and video calls, games, and QUIC, which HTTP/3 runs on top of.
===END===
