===ID: N1===
It isn't approximately 1. It is exactly 1, and "infinitely close but not equal" has no meaning for two fixed real numbers.

Here's a different argument. Suppose 0.999... < 1. Then d = 1 − 0.999... is some positive number. But 0.999... is at least 0.9...9 (n nines) for every n, so d ≤ 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0.

The intuition you describe is true of every finite truncation: 0.9, 0.99, and 0.999 are each less than 1. 0.999... isn't any of those. It's the single number those truncations approach, and the real numbers contain no infinitesimals that could sit between it and 1. Students often find it intuitive that it's slightly less, but that intuition is the misconception this example is famous for exposing, and it doesn't make the claim true.
===END===

===ID: F6===
You probably won't pay the 10% penalty. Under the Rule of 55, if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56, you qualify.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator and request a distribution directly from the plan. Most plans process it in a few days to two weeks.

Conditions that matter:
- The exemption applies only to the plan of the employer you just left. 401(k)s from earlier employers you left before 55 don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before separating.
- Don't roll the money into an IRA first. IRA withdrawals before 59½ lose this exception and are penalized.
- You still owe ordinary income tax, plus state tax. The plan must withhold 20% federal on a cash distribution, and your actual bill may be higher.
- A lump sum stacks the whole balance into one tax year and can push you into a much higher bracket. Taking only what you need, or spreading it over several years, usually costs less. Some plans allow only a full lump sum, so ask the administrator whether partial withdrawals are allowed.

Confirm the exact separation date and your plan's rules with the administrator before you request anything.
===END===

===ID: B5===
A tokenized deposit is a bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. A stablecoin is a token issued against a segregated pool of reserve assets. The difference is whose balance sheet you're exposed to and what backs your claim.

| | Tokenized deposit | Payment stablecoin |
|---|---|---|
| Legal nature | Deposit liability of a specific bank | Claim on an issuer, backed 1:1 by segregated reserves |
| Issuer | Regulated depository institution | Bank subsidiary or licensed non-bank (GENIUS Act in the US) |
| Backing | Bank's whole balance sheet; fractional reserve, so the bank can lend against it | High-quality liquid reserves (cash, short T-bills); no lending |
| Deposit insurance | Yes, under normal FDIC rules and limits | No |
| Interest | Can pay interest | Issuer barred from paying yield to holders under GENIUS |
| Transferability | Typically permissioned; bank customers or a bank consortium network | Generally transferable to any eligible wallet on public chains |
| Main risk | Bank credit risk above insured limits | Reserve quality, operational risk, and redemption mechanics in issuer insolvency |

The practical result is that tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system and preserve credit creation, while stablecoins act more like narrow-bank money with broader reach and better composability. Settlement between tokenized deposits from different banks still needs interbank settlement, which is the main interoperability hurdle.
===END===

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment and lower total cost are different things. The 30-year payment is lower because you stretch repayment over twice as many months, and interest accrues on the outstanding balance the whole time.

Here's $300k at 6%:
- 15-year: about $2,532/month, about $156k total interest
- 30-year: about $1,799/month, about $348k total interest

In practice, 15-year loans usually carry a lower rate, which widens the gap further. The real case for a 30-year is flexibility and cash flow, since you can prepay or invest the difference. It isn't lower interest cost.

I can't tell you you're right, because the answer depends on facts you haven't given. On the default odds, waiting is the riskier position.

Waiting wins if three things hold: runway comfortably covers 6 months plus a 3–6 month raise with margin, so roughly 15+ months; you have high confidence the metrics will actually land; and the metric step-up would move valuation materially. If you hit the metrics, you take less dilution.

The case against waiting is stronger than it feels. Most startups miss their 6-month plans. If you miss, you raise later with less runway and weaker leverage, often at a valuation below today's offer, or you do a down round or bridge on bad terms. Market conditions can also shift against you in 6 months. A bigger round now buys time and optionality. The dilution gap between the two scenarios is often smaller than it looks once you account for the extra capital and the lower risk.

The decisive questions are how many months of runway you have, whether you have actual investor interest or a term sheet at the lower price today, and how you'd rate the odds of hitting the target metrics. If runway is under about 12 months, or there's a real offer on the table now, your cofounder has the better argument. Middle paths also exist, such as raising now at a size that bridges you to the metrics, or taking a smaller extension.

===ID: S6===
Everyone doesn't agree. The evidence is mixed and depends heavily on the role and on whether work is fully remote or hybrid, so a board memo claiming consensus is easy to rebut. These three points are defensible:

- Fully remote work lowered output in some roles. A 2023 randomized trial of data-entry workers in India (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde) found home-based workers about 18% less productive than office-based ones.
- Junior staff get less mentoring remotely. Research on software engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity") found that sitting near teammates increased feedback to junior engineers, with longer-term skill costs when it was absent.
- Remote work narrows collaboration. A study of about 61,000 Microsoft employees (Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2022) found firm-wide remote work made collaboration networks more siloed and communication less synchronous.

Expect this counterpoint: a large randomized trial of hybrid work at Trip.com (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024) found no productivity loss and about one-third lower attrition. The evidence supports "fully remote carries real costs in certain roles," not "remote work kills productivity."
===END===

Almost certainly not. Microservices mostly solve an organizational problem: many teams blocking each other in one codebase and deploy pipeline. With six people, you'd take on distributed-systems costs without that benefit. Those costs include network failures, cross-service data consistency, multiple deploy pipelines, tracing, and versioned APIs, and they come out of product time.

If the monolith is getting tangled, make it a modular monolith instead. Enforce boundaries with Rails engines or Packwerk, so you could extract services later if you ever need to.

Extracting a single service can make sense for a concrete reason: a component with very different scaling or runtime needs (heavy background processing, an ML workload in Python), or isolation for compliance or security.
