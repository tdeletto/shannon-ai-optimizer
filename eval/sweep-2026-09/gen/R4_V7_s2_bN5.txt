Your assumption is likely wrong. Under the "rule of 55," if you left the job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) carry no 10% penalty. At 56, you qualify.

Conditions that matter:
- It applies only to the plan of the employer you just left. Older 401(k)s from previous jobs don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- Don't roll it to an IRA first. IRA withdrawals before 59½ are penalized, so a rollover forfeits the exemption.
- Ordinary income tax still applies. The plan must withhold 20% federal on a lump-sum payout, and a large lump sum can push you into a much higher bracket. Taking only what you need, or spreading withdrawals across tax years, usually costs less.
- Check your plan's rules. Some plans only allow a full lump-sum distribution, not partial withdrawals.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan administrator (the recordkeeper website or phone line). Payment usually arrives within a few days to two weeks.

A tokenized deposit is a regular bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It is a liability of a regulated bank, sits inside the bank's fractional-reserve balance sheet, can pay interest, and carries FDIC insurance like any deposit (up to $250k per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, though how coverage passes through to on-chain holders is still unsettled in some structures). It usually circulates only on permissioned networks among the bank's own customers, and you redeem it at that bank.

A payment stablecoin is a bearer-style token from an issuer that holds 1:1 reserves in cash and short-term Treasuries. The issuer can be a nonbank or a bank subsidiary. It is not a deposit and has no FDIC insurance; your protection comes from the reserve requirements, segregation rules, and priority in insolvency. Under the GENIUS Act, issuers can't pay holders interest. Stablecoins typically move freely on public chains between any wallets, including ones the issuer doesn't know.

In short, a tokenized deposit is bank money on new rails and depends on the bank's credit and deposit insurance. A stablecoin is a fully reserved, narrow-bank-like instrument whose strength is portability, not insurance.

The premise won't hold up in a board memo. Everyone does not agree, and the strongest recent evidence cuts against a blanket claim. A randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom, Han & Liang, Nature, 2024) found hybrid work had no effect on performance or promotions and cut quits by a third, and an earlier call-center experiment at the same company found remote workers were more productive. A board member who knows this literature will discount the whole memo.

What the evidence does support is narrower: fully remote work carries specific, measurable costs. Bullets you can defend:

- In a study of an Asian IT services firm (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023), productivity fell an estimated 8–19% after the move to working from home, as hours rose while output stayed roughly flat.
- Junior staff lose out. Engineers at a Fortune 500 firm who sat near their teammates got substantially more feedback on their code, and the loss of that mentoring fell hardest on newer and female engineers (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity").
- Video meetings generated fewer creative ideas than in-person ones in a controlled experiment (Brucks & Levav, Nature, 2022), a cost for early-stage or innovation-heavy work.

Frame the memo around fully remote versus hybrid for your specific roles, not remote work in general, and verify each citation before it goes to the board.

No. It isn't approximately 1; it is 1, and your students' shared intuition doesn't change that. It's one of the best-documented misconceptions in math education.

A different angle: suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the gap d = 1 − 0.999... would be a fixed positive number. But the gap is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n, and the only non-negative real number smaller than all of those is 0. So d = 0. The "infinitely close but not equal" picture describes the finite sequence 0.9, 0.99, ..., each of which is less than 1. It doesn't describe the number that sequence converges to. Decimal notation simply gives some numbers two spellings, the same way 0.5 = 0.4999....

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment isn't a lower total cost; you get it by stretching the loan out, so you pay interest on a larger balance for twice as many years.

Example: $300,000 at 6%. The 30-year payment is about $1,799/month and $347,500 in total interest. The 15-year payment is about $2,532/month and $155,700 in total interest. In practice, 15-year loans usually carry lower rates too, which widens the gap.

The 30-year can still be the better choice, for cash-flow flexibility or if you'd invest the difference at a higher return. It's not the cheaper one.

I can't tell you you're right, because it depends on facts you haven't given, and on the usual facts your cofounder's position is often the safer one.

The case for your cofounder: money is available now, and six months from now it may not be. Waiting is a bet that you hit the metrics and the market stays open, and if you miss either one, you raise at a lower valuation from a weaker position, or into a down round. A larger round also buys runway, which lowers the chance of dying. The dilution you'd save by raising at a higher price is often a few points, a small cost next to the risk of a bad raise.

The case for you: if you have 18 months or more of runway, the metrics are close to certain (for example, contracts already signed and ramping), and a higher valuation meaningfully changes ownership or who leads the round, waiting can be right. Raising too much at a low price also creates a high bar for the next round.

What decides it: months of runway without the raise, how confident you are in the six-month metrics and why, whether investors are actively offering terms now, and the actual dilution difference between the two scenarios. If your runway is under 12 months, or the metrics depend on things you don't control, your cofounder has the better argument.
