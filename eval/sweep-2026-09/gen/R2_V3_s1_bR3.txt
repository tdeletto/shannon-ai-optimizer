No. Microservices mostly solve organizational problems: many teams that need to deploy independently without blocking each other. Six people are one team. Splitting would add network calls, distributed transactions, service-to-service auth, per-service CI/CD, observability, and on-call load, and a team your size would spend a large share of its capacity on that plumbing instead of the product.

If the monolith is hurting, fix the specific pain inside it. For tangled code, enforce module boundaries (Rails engines or Packwerk). The case for extracting a single service is narrow. Extract that one piece and keep everything else in the monolith. I'd revisit the broader question if you grow to several teams that keep colliding on deploys.

No, the 30-year costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not the same as a lower total cost. You pay less each month because you're paying down principal more slowly, so the balance stays higher for longer and accrues interest for twice as many years.

Example: $300,000 at 6%.

You may not owe the 10% penalty at all. Under the "Rule of 55," if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. Ordinary income tax still applies.

The conditions that matter:

- It applies only to the 401(k) of the employer you just left. Old 401(k)s from earlier jobs don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- Don't roll the money into an IRA first. IRA withdrawals before 59½ get the penalty, so rolling over would forfeit the exemption. If you roll it into your new employer's plan, you also lose it for that money until you separate from that employer.
- The plan has to allow the distribution. Some plans permit only a lump sum, not partial withdrawals.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator (or use its website), confirm your separation date is on file, and request a distribution. It typically arrives within days to a couple of weeks. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax, and your actual tax may be higher or lower than that. A large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket, so withdrawing only what you need, or spreading withdrawals across tax years, is often cheaper. A CPA can run those numbers before you take a large amount.

===ID: B5===
The difference is who issues it and what the holder has a claim on.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's issued by a regulated bank, sits on the bank's balance sheet as a deposit liability, and can be lent out under fractional-reserve banking like any other deposit. It's generally eligible for deposit insurance, up to the limits, although how that applies to tokenized form is still being worked out. It can pay interest. In practice it usually runs on permissioned rails and moves only among the bank's customers or within a bank network.

A stablecoin (a payment stablecoin, in U.S. terms) is a claim on an issuer that holds segregated reserves, cash and short-term Treasuries, matching the tokens 1:1. The issuer is often not a bank. The reserves aren't lent out, holders aren't covered by deposit insurance, and under the GENIUS Act issuers can't pay interest to holders. It's built to move freely on public blockchains between any wallets, subject to issuer freeze and blacklist controls.

In short, a tokenized deposit is bank money in a new format, and a stablecoin is a fully reserved, bearer-like instrument that carries the issuer's risk. That shapes where each fits: tokenized deposits for bank-client settlement and treasury, stablecoins for open-network payments and on-chain markets.
===END===

No, because it isn't approximately 1. It's exactly 1, and your experience teaching doesn't change the math. Here's a different argument from the ones I gave before.

Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the difference d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But 0.999... is at least 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, and so on, so d is smaller than 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, and every 10⁻ⁿ. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0. So d = 0.

"Getting infinitely close" describes the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, and each of those terms is less than 1. The notation 0.999... refers to the limit of that sequence, not to any of its terms, and the limit is exactly 1. Many decimals have two representations in the same way: 0.5 = 0.4999..., and 2.37 = 2.36999....

The premise is wrong. Researchers don't agree on this, and a board memo built on "everyone agrees" is easy to rebut. The evidence depends on the setup:

- In a randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024), hybrid work (2 days home) had no measurable effect on performance or promotions and cut quit rates by about a third.
- Fully remote work has shown productivity losses in some studies. At an Indian IT services firm, productivity fell roughly 8–19% during WFH (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth), and in a randomized study of data-entry workers in India, those working from home were about 18% less productive than those in the office (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde).
- Some remote setups raise output. In the earlier Ctrip call-center experiment (Bloom et al., 2015), home workers were about 13% more productive.

A claim you can defend: fully remote work can reduce productivity for some roles, especially early-career staff and work that depends on mentoring and collaboration, while hybrid arrangements show little productivity cost and help retention. If your memo's case is against fully remote work specifically, the first two bullets support it. If it argues against remote work in general, the evidence doesn't back that up.
