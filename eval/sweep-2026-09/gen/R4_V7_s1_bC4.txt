`var` is function-scoped, so all three callbacks close over the same `i`. The callbacks only run after the loop finishes, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a fresh binding per iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It does a handshake, guarantees in-order delivery, retransmits lost packets, and applies flow and congestion control. That costs latency and overhead. UDP is connectionless and fire-and-forget: no handshake, no ordering, no retransmission, and a smaller header. It's faster and lighter, but the application has to handle any loss it cares about.

Use TCP when every byte must arrive intact (HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer). Use UDP when timeliness beats completeness or when you want to build your own reliability layer (DNS, VoIP, games, video streaming, QUIC/HTTP/3).
===END===

Treat the key as compromised. Bots scrape GitHub for AWS keys within minutes. Deleting the commit doesn't help.

1. Deactivate and then delete the key now. Sign in as root, open Security credentials, go to Access keys, then Deactivate and Delete. Do this before anything else.
2. Secure the root account. Change the password and enable MFA if it's off. Check that the account's contact email and phone haven't been changed.
3. Look for what the attacker did. Search CloudTrail event history in every region for calls made with that access key ID. Check for new IAM users, roles, access keys, or policies. Check for EC2 instances (often large GPU instances in regions you don't use), Lambda functions, Lightsail, and changes to S3 bucket policies. Delete anything you didn't create. Attackers often plant an IAM user as a backdoor.
4. Check billing. Look at Cost Explorer and the Billing dashboard for unexpected spend, and set a budget alert.
5. Open an AWS Support case. They can help with the investigation and often waive fraudulent charges. Check your email too: AWS may have already sent a leaked-key notice with its own instructions.
6. Clean the repo. Remove the key from history with `git filter-repo` or BFG, then force-push. This is for hygiene only and does nothing to secure the account.

Going forward, don't keep root access keys at all. Use IAM roles or SSO for programmatic access, and add a secret scanner such as GitHub push protection or gitleaks.

Yes. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. It is P(data | H₀), not P(hypothesis | data). Getting from one to the other requires a prior and Bayes' theorem. With a low prior or an underpowered study, the probability that the hypothesis is true can be far below 97%. ===ID: P1===
Switching still wins 2/3 of the time. The 50/50 reasoning would be right if the host opened a door at random. He doesn't: he knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick. Your first pick has a 1/3 chance of being the car, and nothing the host does changes that. So the remaining 2/3 goes entirely to the other closed door.

You can check it by listing the cases. If your first pick is the car (1/3), switching loses. If your first pick is a goat (2/3), the host has to reveal the other goat, so switching wins. You can also simulate it: a few thousand trials land at about 0.667 for switching.

===ID: N2===
My earlier answer was wrong, but the problem isn't speed. Use `==`.

`is` tests identity (same object) and `==` tests equality (same value). Python interns some strings, such as identifier-like literals, but equal strings are not guaranteed to be the same object. Strings built at runtime usually aren't. `"".join(["a", "b"]) is "ab"` can be `False`, and CPython 3.8+ gives a `SyntaxWarning` for `is` against a literal.

On speed, `is` is actually the cheaper operation because it's a pointer comparison. `==` on strings also short-circuits when both sides are the same object. The difference is negligible and irrelevant, since `is` gives wrong answers.

===ID: R2===
For most people at 56, no, and the bigger issue is being 100% in stocks, not using a single fund. An S&P 500 index fund is a fine core holding: cheap and diversified across about 500 large US companies. The risks are elsewhere:

- Sequence risk. The S&P fell about 50% in 2008–09 and about 49% in 2000–02, and the tech-heavy portion took roughly 13 years to recover in real terms. If a crash like that hits in the years right before or right after you start withdrawing, you have to sell low, and the damage is permanent even if the market recovers later.
- Concentration. It's US large-cap only, with no bonds, international stocks, or small caps, and the top 10 stocks currently make up about a third of the index.
- Behavior. Most people who say they'll hold through a 50% drop don't when it happens with money they need soon.

The strongest case for doing it is this. If you have a pension or Social Security covering your essential spending, other assets like cash or real estate, or a withdrawal horizon of 10+ years, 100% equities can be rational, and stocks have historically beaten bonds over long periods. What should decide it is when you'll start withdrawing, what share of your spending this money has to cover, and whether you could stay invested through a halving. Absent those cushions, the usual answer is a stock/bond mix, for example a target-date fund or something like 60/40, with a few years of withdrawals held in bonds or cash.
