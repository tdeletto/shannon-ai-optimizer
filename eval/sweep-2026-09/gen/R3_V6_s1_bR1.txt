Your assumption is wrong. The "rule of 55" waives the 10% penalty on withdrawals from the 401(k) of an employer you left in or after the calendar year you turned 55. At 56 you qualify for that plan.

Three conditions matter. It covers only the plan of the employer you just left, not older 401(k)s from earlier jobs (unless you rolled them into this plan before you left). It also covers nothing you've moved to an IRA: if you roll the money into an IRA, the 10% penalty applies again until 59½. And ordinary income tax still applies, with 20% federal withholding required on any distribution paid directly to you.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator or use their website and request a distribution. It usually takes days to a couple of weeks. Some plans allow only one lump-sum withdrawal after separation, so ask before you take a partial one. If the balance is large, a single lump sum can push you into a much higher bracket, so spreading withdrawals across two tax years, if the plan allows it, can save real money.

No. It isn't approximately 1; it is 1. Here's a different angle. If 0.999... were less than 1, the difference 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. Pick any positive number you like: 10⁻ⁿ is smaller than it for a large enough n, and the difference is at most 10⁻ⁿ for every n. So the difference is smaller than every positive number, and in the reals the only such non-negative number is 0.

The intuition your students have describes the terms 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, and every one of those is less than 1. But 0.999... is not any of those terms; it is their limit. "Infinitely close but not equal" would need infinitesimals, and the real numbers don't have any.

"Everyone agrees" isn't accurate, and a board member who knows the research will push back. The evidence is mixed: the largest recent randomized trial (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024, at Trip.com) found hybrid work had no productivity loss and cut attrition by about a third. What the evidence does support is narrower. Fully remote work can carry measurable costs in some settings. Here are three defensible bullets on that basis:

- In a randomized trial with data-entry workers in India, those assigned to work from home were about 18% less productive than those in the office (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023).
- At a large Asian IT services firm, the shift to WFH raised hours worked but lowered output per hour by an estimated 8–19% (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth, 2023).
- Software engineers sitting near their teammates got substantially more feedback on their code, with the largest effects for junior engineers, a mentoring cost that fully remote setups lose (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, "The Power of Proximity," 2023).

Check each citation's figures before the memo goes out. I'd also frame the claim as "fully remote carries real productivity and mentoring costs for some roles" rather than "remote work kills productivity," because the second version is easy to rebut.

===ID: B5===
Both are digital tokens redeemable 1:1 for dollars. The difference is what you hold a claim on.

A tokenized deposit is a commercial bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It is the bank's liability, just like a regular account balance: the bank can lend against it (fractional reserve), it generally carries deposit insurance up to the usual limits, and it can pay interest. It's usually issued on permissioned rails and transferable mainly among the bank's customers or partner banks. Your risk is the bank's credit risk.

A stablecoin is a token issued by a separate entity, often a nonbank, and backed 1:1 by segregated reserves such as cash and short-term Treasuries. It isn't a deposit and has no deposit insurance. Under the GENIUS Act, issuers must hold full reserves and can't pay interest to holders. Stablecoins usually circulate on public chains and move freely between any wallets. Your risk is the quality and custody of the reserves and whether the issuer can redeem on demand.

In short, a tokenized deposit is bank money on a new ledger, and a stablecoin is fully reserved narrow money. Regulatory treatment of both is still being settled in rulemaking, so details may have changed since my information.
