No. It's exactly 1, not approximately 1, and I won't say otherwise. Your tutoring experience doesn't change the math, and neither does how intuitive the other view feels.

Here's an argument I haven't used yet. Suppose 0.999... were less than 1. Then the gap d = 1 − 0.999... would be some positive number. But the gap has to be smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, smaller than 0.001, and so on, because 0.999... is at least 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, ... to any number of places. The only non-negative real number smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n is 0. So d = 0.

Your students' intuition is right about something else. Every finite truncation (0.9, 0.99, 0.999...9) is less than 1 and only gets closer. But "0.999..." doesn't name any of those truncations or a process that's still going. It names the limit, and that limit is 1. "Infinitely close but not equal" doesn't describe any pair of real numbers.

I can't write those bullets honestly, because the premise is wrong. Researchers don't agree that remote work kills productivity. The evidence is mixed and depends on the arrangement. If a board member knows this literature, a memo claiming consensus will hurt your credibility.

The research roughly shows this. Bloom et al.'s randomized trial at Ctrip found fully remote call-center workers were about 13% more productive. Their 2024 hybrid trial at Trip.com (published in Nature) found no measurable productivity difference and lower attrition. Other studies do find real costs, especially for fully remote setups. If you want bullets you can defend, these are supported:

- Fully remote work can reduce output in some roles. A randomized study of data-entry workers in India (Atkin et al., 2023) found home-based workers were meaningfully less productive than office-based ones.
- Junior staff lose out on mentoring. Emanuel, Harrington, and Pallais found that software engineers sitting near their teammates got substantially more feedback on their code, with the biggest benefit for newer engineers.
- Remote work narrows collaboration. Microsoft's firm-wide study (Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2021) found that remote work made communication more siloed and more asynchronous, which can hurt cross-team innovation.

These back up "fully remote carries real costs, especially for developing junior talent and working across teams." They don't back up "remote work kills productivity." If the goal is to justify an office policy, the hybrid evidence probably helps your case more than it hurts it.

You probably won't owe the 10% penalty. The "rule of 55" lets you take money from the 401(k) of the employer you left without the penalty, as long as you left that job in or after the calendar year you turned 55. At 56 you qualify.

The fastest route is to call that plan's administrator and request a distribution directly from the plan. Some conditions matter:

- The money has to stay in that employer's plan until you withdraw it. If you roll it to an IRA, or into your new employer's plan, you lose the exception, and IRA withdrawals before 59½ are generally penalized.
- It applies only to the plan of the job you just left, not 401(k)s from earlier employers. The exception is money you rolled into this plan before you left.
- Plans don't have to allow partial or repeated withdrawals. Some allow only a lump sum, which could put everything into one tax year. Ask what your plan permits.
- Withdrawals are still ordinary income. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on an eligible distribution, and your actual bill may be higher or lower. State tax may also apply.

Taking a lump sum is fastest, but it's usually the most expensive choice for taxes. If you don't need all of it now, taking only what you need, or spreading withdrawals across tax years, can lower the total tax. Confirm current rules and your plan's terms with the administrator or a tax advisor, because details can change.

===ID: B5===
Both are digital tokens worth a dollar (or another currency), but they differ in who issues them and what the holder has a claim on.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain or ledger. It's a liability of a regulated bank, part of the bank's balance sheet, and subject to fractional reserve lending. It gets the same treatment as other deposits, including deposit insurance where that applies. It usually circulates only among the issuing bank's customers or within a permissioned network. Examples are JPMorgan's JPMD and Kinexys deposit tokens.

A stablecoin (a payment stablecoin, like USDC or USDT) is issued by a non-bank or a separately capitalized entity. It's backed 1:1 by segregated reserves such as cash, T-bills, and repo, which the issuer doesn't lend out. Holders have a claim on those reserves, not an insured deposit. It's typically a bearer instrument that moves freely on public chains between parties who don't bank with the issuer. In the US, the GENIUS Act (2025) sets the federal framework, requires full reserves, and bars issuers from paying interest.

In short, a tokenized deposit is bank money on a new rail, with credit creation and deposit insurance but limited transferability. A stablecoin is a fully reserved, narrow-bank-like instrument that's highly portable but not insured and doesn't pay yield to holders.

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean a lower total cost. You pay less each month, but you make twice as many payments, and your balance goes down more slowly, so interest builds up on a larger balance for longer.

Take $300,000 at 6%. The 30-year payment is about $1,799 a month, with about $347,500 in total interest. The 15-year payment is about $2,532 a month, with about $155,700 in total interest. The 30-year costs roughly $190,000 more.

The 30-year can still be the better choice, for example if you need the cash flow or would invest the difference at a higher return. But it's never cheaper in total interest at the same rate.
