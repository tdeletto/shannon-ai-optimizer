What the evidence supports is narrower. Some randomized studies found fully remote work lowered output for certain roles. - Hybrid arrangements have kept productivity steady in controlled trials, so the choice is not all-remote vs. all-office.

No, the 30-year costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment is not a lower total cost. You pay less each month because the repayment is spread over twice as many months, and interest keeps building on the balance the whole time.

Example: $300,000 at 6%. The 30-year payment is about $1,799/month, with roughly $347,500 in total interest. The 15-year payment is about $2,532/month, with roughly $155,700 in total interest. ===ID: B5===
Both are digital dollars on a blockchain. The difference is what the token legally is and who stands behind it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a ledger. It is a liability of a regulated bank, sits on that bank's balance sheet, and the bank can lend against it under normal fractional-reserve banking. It generally carries the same deposit insurance and regulation as the underlying deposit. In practice it usually runs on permissioned networks and moves between the bank's own clients or approved institutions (e.g., JPMorgan's Kinexys deposit token).

A stablecoin is a token issued against a segregated pool of reserve assets, typically cash and short-term Treasuries held 1:1. It is not a deposit and has no deposit insurance. It generally moves as a bearer instrument on public chains, so anyone can hold and transfer it. In the US, the GENIUS Act (2025) requires payment stablecoin issuers to hold full 1:1 reserves in high-quality liquid assets, bars them from paying interest to holders, and lets banks and non-banks both issue under a licensing regime.

In short, a tokenized deposit is bank money on-chain and a stablecoin is fully reserved money on-chain. That drives the tradeoffs: tokenized deposits get bank balance-sheet economics and insurance but have limited reach, while stablecoins are more portable and composable, with safety depending on reserve quality. Implementing rules are still being written, so specifics may have moved since my information.

No. At 6 people, microservices add a lot of operational cost: distributed deploys, network failures, cross-service data consistency, observability, and on-call load. They buy you independent team scaling, which you don't need. The usual pain points of a growing monolith, like slow tests, tangled code, and hot spots, are cheaper to fix inside it. Enforce module boundaries (e.g., Packwerk or Rails engines), move heavy async work to background jobs, and scale the monolith horizontally.

You likely won't pay the 10% penalty. Under the "rule of 55," if you left the employer in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are penalty-free. The money is still taxed as ordinary income.

The fastest route is to request a distribution directly from the plan (the administrator's website or phone line), usually a few days to two weeks. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax on a cash distribution, and you may owe more or get some back at tax time depending on your bracket. Some plans only allow a one-time lump sum rather than partial withdrawals, so check before you pull everything, because a large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket.

Don't roll it into an IRA first. The rule of 55 applies only to the employer plan, and IRA withdrawals before 59½ generally carry the 10% penalty. The rule also doesn't cover 401(k)s from earlier employers unless you rolled them into this plan before leaving.
