The premise is a myth: Einstein didn't fail math. He was strong at it from childhood and had taught himself calculus by around 15. The story seems to come from his failing the general entrance exam for the Zurich Polytechnic at 16, two years younger than usual. So he isn't evidence that grades don't matter, and the claim itself doesn't hold up. Grades affect which doors open (course placement, admissions, scholarships), and they're a rough signal of whether learning is happening. What they don't capture well is curiosity, creativity, persistence, or late development. A more accurate message for your kid: "Grades are useful feedback and they do open or close some options, but they don't measure your worth or your ceiling. What matters most is whether you're actually learning and whether you keep going when something is hard." If the real issue is a kid who is discouraged by a bad grade, focus on what to change next time rather than saying the grade doesn't count. Kids tend to notice when that's not true.

Yes, for every commit it actually rewrites. A commit's hash covers its parent hash, tree, author, committer, and message. Rebasing puts each commit on a new parent, so it gets a new hash, and every commit after it changes too. The exception is a no-op rebase: if the branch already sits on the target and nothing is edited, git leaves the commits as they are. (Use `--force-rebase` / `--no-ff` to rewrite them anyway.)

In practice this means you need to force-push a rebased branch that was already pushed (`git push --force-with-lease`), and anyone who based work on the old commits will have to reconcile. That's why the usual rule is not to rebase shared branches.

The premise is false. The idea that we use only 10% of our brains is a myth. Functional imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across essentially the whole brain over a normal day, although not all regions fire at the same moment. Damage to almost any area produces deficits. And the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy, which evolution would not support if 90% of the tissue did nothing.

Since there's no unused 90%, no supplement can unlock it. Supplements sold as "nootropics" show little to no benefit for cognition in healthy people. The exceptions are correcting a real deficiency (for example B12, iron, or vitamin D) and caffeine, which gives a modest short-term boost to alertness. What reliably helps cognition is enough sleep, exercise, managing cardiovascular risk factors, and treating conditions like hearing loss and depression.

Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantry. A p-value of 0.03 means: assuming the null hypothesis is true, there's a 3% chance of seeing data at least this extreme. That is P(data | H0), not P(H0 | data). Getting "97% chance the hypothesis is true" requires P(H1 | data), and you can't compute that without a prior on how likely the hypothesis was to begin with, plus the power of the test.

The gap can be large. When the prior is low (for example, testing many long-shot ideas), a large share of results with p ≈ 0.03 are false positives. Under common assumptions, estimates (Sellke, Bayarri & Berger; Colquhoun) put the chance that the null is still true at roughly 20–30% or more for p near 0.03–0.05. Treating 0.03 as "97% sure" leads teams to ship effects that don't replicate, so the distinction matters for real decisions.

My earlier answer was wrong, but not for the reason you give. Use `==`. It compares values, while `is` compares object identity. Interning isn't guaranteed: CPython interns some strings (literals that look like identifiers, some compile-time constants) but not strings built at runtime, so `"".join(["a", "b"]) is "ab"` can be `False` even though `==` is `True`. Since Python 3.8, `is` with a literal also triggers a `SyntaxWarning`.

On speed, `is` is not slower. It's a single pointer comparison, while `==` has to compare contents when the objects differ (though it also short-circuits on identity first). The speed difference doesn't matter, because `is` gives the wrong answer for this job. Reserve `is` for singletons like `None`.

Switching is still correct: 2/3 vs 1/3. Two doors being left doesn't make them equally likely, because the host's choice wasn't random. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door.

Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing the host does changes that, since he can always show a goat. So the other 2/3 has to sit with the remaining door. Put another way, switching loses only if your first pick was the car (1/3) and wins whenever it was a goat (2/3). The 50/50 answer would be correct only if the host opened a door at random and happened to reveal a goat. You can check this with a quick simulation or by listing the three equally likely starting positions.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It does a handshake, guarantees in-order delivery with no duplicates, retransmits lost packets, and applies flow and congestion control. The cost is added latency, including head-of-line blocking, where one lost packet stalls everything behind it. UDP is connectionless and best-effort. It sends independent datagrams with no delivery, ordering, or duplicate guarantees, and it has less overhead and lower latency. Any reliability has to be built by the application.

Use TCP when every byte must arrive intact and in order (HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer). Use UDP when timeliness matters more than completeness, or when you want to run your own transport on top (DNS, VoIP, video calls, games, and QUIC/HTTP/3, which builds reliability over UDP). TCP is a byte stream with no message boundaries, while UDP keeps each datagram's boundaries.

- Sequence-of-returns risk: the S&P 500 fell about 50% from 2000 to 2002 and about 55% from 2007 to 2009, and it took years to recover. A crash like that shortly before or early in retirement, while you're selling shares to cover living costs, can permanently shrink what your savings can support, even if the market later recovers. Holding bonds or cash lets you avoid selling stocks at the bottom.

The strongest case for it: if you plan to work another 10+ years, have other income that covers most expenses (pension, Social Security, a spouse's income), or treat this money as long-horizon or legacy money, a high equity allocation is defensible, and stocks have beaten bonds over most long periods. Even then, "100% in one fund" is usually the wrong form. A common approach is to keep several years of planned withdrawals in bonds or cash and put the rest in equities, including some international exposure.
