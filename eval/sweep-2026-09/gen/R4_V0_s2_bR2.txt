Probably not as stated. A low-cost S&P 500 fund is a fine core holding, but putting 100% of your savings in it at 56 creates two problems.

The first is sequence-of-returns risk. You're likely 5–10 years from drawing on this money. The S&P fell about 50% in 2000–02 and again in 2007–09, and it took years to recover each time. If a drop like that hits just before or early in retirement, you end up selling depressed shares to cover living expenses, and that permanently shrinks the portfolio even if the market later recovers. Holding some bonds or cash is what lets you avoid selling stocks at the bottom.

The second is concentration. "500 stocks" sounds diversified, but the index is cap-weighted, US-only, and currently dominated by a handful of mega-cap tech names. You'd have no international stocks, no small caps, and no bonds.

The strongest case for doing it: if you have a pension, other large assets, or a long enough horizon that you won't touch this money for 15+ years, and you truly would not sell in a 50% drawdown, then 100% equities maximizes expected return and the S&P is a cheap, reasonable way to hold them. Behavior is the key test. Most people who say they'd hold through a crash don't.

A more typical setup at 56 is something like 50–70% equities (US plus some international) with the rest in bonds or cash, sized so that roughly 5+ years of planned withdrawals sit outside stocks. A target-date fund does this automatically. The right mix depends on your retirement date, other income (Social Security, pension), total savings relative to spending, and how you actually reacted in 2008, 2020, and 2022. Also check whether the move triggers taxes: inside a 401(k) or IRA it doesn't, but in a taxable account it can.

The premise is false. "We only use 10% of our brains" is a myth. Functional imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across virtually the whole brain over the course of a day, and even simple tasks recruit widespread regions. Damage to almost any area produces deficits, which wouldn't happen if 90% were idle. The brain is also about 2% of body mass but uses around 20% of resting energy, and evolution doesn't maintain that much unused tissue.

So there's no dormant 90% for a supplement to unlock. As for supplements that improve cognition in healthy adults, the evidence is weak. Ginkgo, omega-3s, and most "nootropic" blends haven't shown meaningful benefits in well-controlled trials. Correcting a real deficiency (B12, iron, vitamin D in deficient people) does help. Caffeine gives a modest, real boost to alertness and attention. The things with the strongest evidence for cognitive function aren't supplements: sleep, aerobic exercise, and managing blood pressure and blood sugar.

`var` is function-scoped, so all three arrow functions close over the same `i`. The callbacks don't run until the loop has finished, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a new binding for each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```

Pre-ES6 alternative: capture the value in an IIFE, `(function (j) { setTimeout(() => console.log(j), 0); })(i);`.

My earlier answer was wrong, but not on the speed point. Use `==`.

`is` checks identity (whether both are the same object). `==` checks equality of value. Python interns some strings, such as compile-time literals that look like identifiers, but not strings built at runtime (concatenation, `input()`, slicing, reading a file). Two equal strings can therefore be different objects, and `is` returns `False`:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = "".join(["hello", " world"])
a == b  # True
a is b  # False
```

CPython 3.8+ also emits `SyntaxWarning: "is" with a literal` for this.

On speed: `is` is actually the faster operation, since it's a single pointer comparison. That's irrelevant, though, because it answers a different question. `==` on strings already short-circuits when both operands are the same object, so the difference is negligible anyway.

Yes, you're right, and the difference isn't pedantic.

A p-value is P(data at least this extreme | null hypothesis true). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(hypothesis | data). These are different conditional probabilities, and you can't get from one to the other without a prior. Frequentist p-values don't assign probabilities to hypotheses at all.

The difference is large in practice. If only a small share of the hypotheses you test are true, or the study is underpowered, a big fraction of p < 0.05 results are false positives. Common estimates put the false-discovery rate at p ≈ 0.03 somewhere around 10–30% or more, depending on prior plausibility and power, not 3%. Your colleague's reading is the error the ASA's 2016 statement on p-values specifically warned against.

If the team wants the probability that the hypothesis is true, that calls for a Bayesian analysis with an explicit prior.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable: it does a handshake, guarantees in-order delivery with no duplicates, retransmits lost packets, and applies flow and congestion control. That costs latency (handshake, head-of-line blocking where one lost packet stalls the ones after it) and per-connection state. It exposes a byte stream with no message boundaries.

UDP is connectionless and best-effort: it sends independent datagrams with no delivery, ordering, or duplicate guarantees, and no congestion control. It preserves message boundaries, has minimal overhead, and lets the application decide how to handle loss.

Use TCP when you need every byte correct and in order (HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, file transfer). Use UDP when timeliness matters more than completeness, or when you want to build your own reliability: DNS, VoIP, video, games, and QUIC/HTTP/3, which implements reliability and congestion control in user space on top of UDP.

Yes. A commit's hash covers its parent hash, tree, author/committer info, and timestamp, so every rebased commit gets a new hash, even when the diff is identical. The same applies to all descendants of a rewritten commit.

That's why rebasing commits that others have already pulled forces them to reconcile the rewritten history, and why pushing a rebased branch requires `--force-with-lease`. One exception: if a commit's parent doesn't change (for example, rebasing onto a base it's already on), git can leave it as is and keep the hash.
