Yes, you're right. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd see data at least this extreme 3% of the time. That's P(data | null), not P(hypothesis | data). Turning it into a probability that the hypothesis is true requires the prior plausibility of the hypothesis and the study's power. With a long-shot hypothesis or an underpowered study, a result at p = 0.03 can still leave the hypothesis more likely false than true. That's also why this isn't pedantry: treating p as "97% sure" is the reasoning behind a lot of results that don't replicate.

For most people at 56, no. The main risk is sequence of returns. The S&P 500 fell about 49% from 2000 to 2002 and about 57% from 2007 to 2009, and it took roughly 13 years from the 2000 peak to recover in real terms. A drop like that in the years just before or after you retire, when you have to sell shares to cover spending, can permanently shrink what the portfolio can pay you. An S&P fund also holds only US large caps, with no bonds, no cash buffer, and no international or small-cap exposure.

The best case for doing it: you may be investing for 30 or more years, equities have historically beaten bonds over long periods, and a low-cost index fund is far better than stock-picking. If a pension, Social Security, or other assets already cover your essential spending, you can afford to hold much more in stocks.

A common alternative is to keep a few years of expected withdrawals in bonds or cash and put the rest in broad equities, or to use a target-date fund. How you should split it depends on when you'll start drawing down, your other income, and whether you could stay invested through a 50% drop without selling. If you're moving money inside a 401(k) or IRA, the move itself won't trigger taxes.

My earlier answer was wrong, but not because of speed. Use `==`. It compares values. `is` checks whether two names point to the same object. Python interns only some strings (mainly compile-time literals that look like identifiers), and strings built at runtime usually aren't interned:

```python
a = "hello world"
b = " ".join(["hello", "world"])
a == b  # True
a is b  # False
```

On speed, `is` isn't slower. It's a single pointer comparison, so it's marginally faster, but that doesn't matter when it can return the wrong answer. Python 3.8+ warns about `is` with a literal (SyntaxWarning in 3.8–3.11) for this reason.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It does a handshake before sending, guarantees in-order delivery, retransmits lost packets, and adds flow and congestion control. That costs extra latency and overhead. UDP is connectionless and sends independent datagrams with no delivery, ordering, or duplicate guarantees. It has less overhead and lower latency, and your app handles any reliability it needs. Use TCP when every byte has to arrive intact (HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases). Use UDP when timeliness matters more than completeness, or when you want to build your own reliability layer (DNS, VoIP, video, games, QUIC/HTTP/3).
===END===

Treat the key as compromised now. Bots scan GitHub for AWS keys within minutes, so deleting the commit won't help.

1. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials, and deactivate the access key, then delete it. Root shouldn't have access keys at all, so don't replace it.
2. Change the root password and make sure MFA is on.
3. Check for anything an attacker created, in every region: IAM users, roles, access keys, and policies; EC2 instances (look especially for GPU instances used for crypto mining); Lambda functions; and changes to S3 bucket policies. CloudTrail Event history shows API calls made with that key ID.
4. Check Billing and Cost Explorer for sudden spikes.
5. Open an AWS Support case. They handle exposed keys and may be able to adjust charges from the abuse. Also follow up on any AWS abuse or health notification you get about the key.
6. Remove the key from the repo's history (git filter-repo or BFG) and force-push. This is cleanup only; step 1 is what actually protects the account.

For anything that needs programmatic access, use IAM roles or scoped IAM users instead of root.
