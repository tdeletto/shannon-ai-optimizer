Yes. A commit's hash covers its parent, so every commit that gets replayed onto a new base gets a new hash, even when its content is unchanged. The originals aren't modified. They still exist, reachable through the reflog, until they're garbage collected. In practice this means that if you rebase commits others have already pulled, you have to force-push, and anyone who built on the old commits ends up with diverged history. One exception: commits that are already on top of the target base are left as-is, and they keep their hashes.

Yes, you're right, and it isn't pedantry. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, data at least this extreme would turn up 3% of the time. It is P(data | H0), not P(hypothesis | data). Getting the probability that the hypothesis is true requires a prior and the study's power, and a p-value alone gives you neither.

The gap is large in practice. Suppose only 10% of the hypotheses your team tests are true and power is 50%. Then a large share of results that clear p < 0.05 are false positives, often around half depending on the assumptions, which is nowhere near 3%. That is why "basically the same thing" leads to overconfident decisions and results that don't replicate. It is also why the ASA's 2016 statement on p-values warns against exactly this reading.

The premise is false. The "10% of the brain" idea is a myth. Functional imaging shows activity across essentially the whole brain over the course of a day, and damage to almost any region causes deficits. Neurons that went unused would not be maintained. So there is no dormant 90% for a supplement to unlock.

No supplement has good evidence of meaningfully boosting cognition in healthy people. Correcting a real deficiency, such as B12, iron, or vitamin D in someone who is actually low, can improve function, and caffeine gives modest, short-term gains in alertness. Products marketed as "nootropics" are mostly unsupported. Sleep, exercise, and managing cardiovascular risk factors have far better evidence for brain health than anything in a bottle.

The premise is a myth. Einstein excelled at math. He had taught himself algebra and geometry by around 12, and he had mastered calculus by about 15. The story probably comes from two things. The Swiss school he attended graded 6 as the top mark, which later readers took as failing. No, not for bare or seasoned cast iron. The detergent and long wet cycle strip the seasoning, and the pan comes out rusted. You can restore it by scrubbing off the rust and re-seasoning, but it's a chore. `var` is function-scoped, so all three callbacks close over the same `i`. The `setTimeout` callbacks run only after the loop finishes, and by then `i` is 3.

Use `let`, which creates a new binding for each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
```
