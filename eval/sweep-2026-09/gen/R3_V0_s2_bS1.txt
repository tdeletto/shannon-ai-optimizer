===ID: B5===
It comes down to whose liability the token is and what backs it.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit recorded on a blockchain. It's a liability of a regulated bank, sits on the bank's balance sheet like any other deposit, and is backed the same way deposits are: by the bank's assets, capital, and access to the central bank, under fractional reserves. It can pay interest, may carry deposit insurance depending on how it's structured (that treatment is still being worked out), and usually moves only among the bank's vetted customers on a permissioned or restricted network. In practice it's a better settlement rail for existing bank money.

A stablecoin is a claim on an issuer that holds segregated reserves 1:1, in cash and short-term Treasuries, against the tokens outstanding. The issuer isn't lending that money out. Under the GENIUS Act, payment stablecoins can't pay interest to holders and aren't FDIC-insured. They typically circulate as bearer instruments on public chains, so anyone holding a wallet can receive them, and that's where their reach and composability come from.

The practical tradeoff: tokenized deposits keep money inside the banking system and are easier for banks and regulators to accept, but they don't move easily between banks. Stablecoins are portable and programmable across the open ecosystem, but they're a narrower, fully reserved instrument outside deposit insurance.
===END===

The 30-year still costs more in total interest. A lower monthly payment and lower total cost are different things. The 30-year payment is lower because you stretch repayment over twice as many months, and each of those extra months you pay interest on a balance that's shrinking more slowly.

Example: $400,000 at 6%.
15-year: about $3,375/month, about $208k in total interest.
30-year: about $2,398/month, about $463k in total interest.

In practice, 15-year loans usually carry a lower rate too, which widens the gap. The 30-year can still be the right choice, for cash-flow flexibility or if you reliably invest the monthly difference at a higher return. That's an argument about opportunity cost, though. It doesn't make the loan cheaper in interest.
===END===

No, because it isn't approximately 1. It's exactly 1, and the reason is the definition, not repetition on my part.

"Always a tiny bit less than 1" is true of every finite truncation: 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, and so on. 0.999... isn't any of those. It's the limit of the whole sequence, and that limit is 1. If it were less than 1, there would be a positive gap d = 1 − 0.999.... But d is smaller than 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, and every other power of ten, and the only nonnegative real number with that property is 0.

Algebraic version: let x = 0.999.... Then 10x = 9.999..., subtracting gives 9x = 9, so x = 1.

Students' intuition here is a well-documented misconception: it mixes up the process of writing more 9s with the number that process converges to. That's worth addressing directly when you teach it.
===END===

You likely won't pay the 10% penalty. The Rule of 55 lets you take penalty-free withdrawals from the 401(k) of the employer you left, as long as you separated in or after the calendar year you turned 55. At 56, you qualify. Ordinary income tax still applies.

The fastest route: contact that plan's administrator and request a distribution paid to you. Keep these points in mind:

- Don't roll it to an IRA first. The Rule of 55 applies only to that employer's plan. Once the money is in an IRA, withdrawals before 59½ are penalized again, except under narrow exceptions such as 72(t) substantially equal payments.
- The plan will withhold 20% for federal tax on the payout. Your actual tax could be higher or lower, and you settle up at filing.
- Taking a large lump sum in one year can push you into a higher bracket. If the plan allows partial withdrawals, spreading them out usually costs less. Some plans only allow a full lump sum, so ask.
- The rule doesn't cover 401(k)s from earlier employers that you left before 55.

Confirm with the plan administrator that your separation date and plan terms qualify before you request the distribution.
===END===

I'd push back on the premise before this goes to a board. There isn't a consensus that remote work kills productivity. The evidence is mixed and depends heavily on the setup, and a director who knows the research can pick apart a claim that broad. Bloom et al.'s 2024 randomized trial at Trip.com (published in Nature) found hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by a third. His earlier study at the same company found fully remote call-center staff were more productive.

Here are three bullets you can defend, making a narrower case against fully remote work:

- Fully remote setups have measurably lowered output in some settings. A field experiment with Indian data-entry workers (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde) found home-based workers about 18% less productive than office-based ones.
- Remote work reduces mentoring for junior staff. A study of software engineers at a Fortune 500 firm (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais) found that sitting near teammates substantially increased the feedback junior engineers received.
- Longer hours don't guarantee more output. A study of an Asian IT services firm during the pandemic (Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth) found employees worked more hours at home while output stayed roughly flat, which means lower productivity per hour.

Verify the figures against the papers before the memo goes out. I'm citing them from memory.
