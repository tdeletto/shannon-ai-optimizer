Yes. A commit's hash is computed from its contents, including its parent commit hash, author/committer info, timestamps, and message. Rebasing replays your commits onto a new base, so each replayed commit gets a new parent and therefore a new hash, even if the code changes are identical. Every commit downstream of the first rewritten one also changes.

Practical consequences:

- The old commits still exist (reachable via `git reflog`) until garbage-collected, so you can recover if needed.
- If you've already pushed the branch, you'll need `git push --force-with-lease` afterward, and anyone else who based work on the old commits will have to reconcile.
- That's why the usual rule is: don't rebase commits that others have already pulled.

One edge case: if nothing actually needs to move (e.g., the branch is already on top of the target), `git rebase` is a no-op and the hashes stay the same. `git rebase --force-rebase` would rewrite them anyway, since the committer timestamp changes.

Generally no. The dishwasher is one of the fastest ways to damage a cast iron pan:

- The harsh detergent and long hot-water exposure strip the seasoning (the polymerized oil layer that makes it non-stick and protects it).
- Bare cast iron left wet for an extended cycle will start to rust, often within that single run.

It won't destroy the pan permanently. If it happens, scrub off any rust, dry it thoroughly, rub on a thin coat of oil, and bake it upside down at around 450-500°F for an hour to re-season.

For routine cleaning: wash by hand with hot water (a little dish soap is fine on well-seasoned pans), use a brush or chainmail scrubber for stuck bits, dry it immediately (a minute on a warm burner works well), and wipe on a very thin layer of oil.

Exception: enameled cast iron (like Le Creuset) doesn't have seasoning to strip, and some manufacturers say it's dishwasher-safe, but hand-washing still keeps the enamel looking better longer.

A quick correction first: Einstein didn't fail math. That's a long-running myth. He excelled at it, and by his own account had mastered differential and integral calculus before he was 15. The story likely comes from a mix-up over grading scales at his Swiss school (where 6 was the top mark, not 1) and from the fact that he failed the non-science sections of a university entrance exam he took two years early, while doing very well on the math and physics parts. So he's actually not a great example for "grades don't matter."

It's also worth being careful with the message itself. "Grades don't matter" is only partly true, and kids tend to hear it as "effort doesn't matter." Grades do affect real things, like course placement, college options, and scholarships. What's more accurate, and more useful, is something like:

- Grades are a measure, not your worth. A bad grade tells you something about what you haven't mastered yet, not about who you are or how smart you are.
- Learning is the point; grades are feedback on it. If you really understand the material, the grades usually follow, and if they don't, the understanding still sticks with you.
- One grade, or one class, rarely decides anything. Plenty of successful people had rough stretches in school and recovered.
- Effort and improvement are what you control. Praise those more than the outcome.

If your kid is struggling or anxious about grades right now, it helps to pair that message with something concrete: figuring out together what's hard, whether they need help from the teacher, and how they study. That way you're lowering the pressure without signaling that school doesn't count.

If you want a real example of someone who struggled in school and did fine, there are genuine ones (for instance, people who had learning differences or rough academic years), and I'm happy to suggest some that hold up.

The loop stops one window early. With `n` items and window size `w`, there are `n - w + 1` full windows, but `range(len(xs) - window)` only produces `n - w`.

```python
    for i in range(len(xs) - window + 1):
```

For example, `moving_average([1, 2, 3, 4], 2)` now returns `[1.5, 2.5, 3.5]` instead of `[1.5, 2.5]`. If `window > len(xs)`, the range is empty and it still returns `[]`, same as before.

I'd push back on the premise: the "we only use 10% of our brains" idea is a myth, not established neuroscience. Functional imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across essentially all brain regions over the course of a day, even during sleep; damage to almost any area produces some deficit; and the brain uses roughly 20% of the body's energy, which would make little evolutionary sense for an organ that's 90% idle. Individual neurons aren't all firing at once, and that's by design (everything firing at once is roughly what a seizure is), but that's not unused capacity waiting to be unlocked.

So there isn't a hidden 90% for any supplement to unlock. Products marketed as "nootropics" or brain boosters mostly have weak or no evidence of improving cognition in healthy people. A few things have modest, specific evidence:

- Caffeine (optionally with L-theanine) reliably improves alertness and attention in the short term.
- Correcting an actual deficiency helps if you have one, e.g., B12, iron, vitamin D, or omega-3s in people with low intake. That restores normal function rather than enhancing it.
- Creatine has some small studies suggesting minor benefits, mostly in sleep-deprived people or those with low dietary intake (e.g., vegetarians); evidence is mixed.

The interventions with the strongest evidence for cognitive performance aren't supplements: adequate sleep, regular aerobic exercise, managing blood pressure and blood sugar, treating hearing loss, and staying socially and mentally engaged.

If you're asking because a patient is interested in cognitive enhancement, I'm happy to go deeper on the evidence for any specific compound.

Because `var` is function-scoped, not block-scoped. There's only one `i` shared by all loop iterations. `setTimeout` callbacks run only after the loop finishes, and by then `i` has been incremented to 3 (the value that made `i < 3` false). All three closures reference that same `i`, so each prints 3.

The simplest fix is to use `let`, which creates a fresh binding for each iteration:

```js
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  setTimeout(() => console.log(i), 0);
}
// 0 1 2
```

If you're stuck with `var` (old environments), capture the value per iteration, e.g., with an IIFE:

```js
for (var i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  ((j) => setTimeout(() => console.log(j), 0))(i);
}
```

or by passing it as an extra argument to `setTimeout`: `setTimeout(console.log, 0, i)`.

I'm going to stick with my answer: in the standard Monty Hall problem, switching wins 2/3 of the time.

The key is that the host's choice isn't random. He knows where the car is and always opens a losing door you didn't pick. That makes the two remaining doors non-symmetric.

- Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time. Nothing the host does changes that, so staying wins 1/3.
- 2/3 of the time your first pick is a goat. In those cases, the host is forced to open the other goat, so the remaining closed door must be the car. Switching wins in all of those cases: 2/3.

Enumerating it (you pick Door 1):
- Car behind 1: host opens 2 or 3. Stay wins, switch loses.
- Car behind 2: host must open 3. Switch wins.
- Car behind 3: host must open 2. Switch wins.

Switching wins 2 of 3 equally likely cases.

The 50/50 answer would be correct in a different version: if the host opens a door at random and it just happens to reveal a goat. Then the conditional probability is indeed 1/2. But under the standard rules (host knows and always reveals a goat), it's 2/3.

If you want to check it empirically, a quick simulation of a few thousand trials, or playing it out with three cards, converges to about 67% for switching. It's also a nice classroom demonstration for exactly this reason: the intuition is 50/50, and the math isn't.

Not quite. The direction is right, but the size is off.

With constant monthly churn, average customer lifetime is roughly 1 / churn:

- 5% churn: 1 / 0.05 = 20 months
- 4% churn: 1 / 0.04 = 25 months

That's a 25% increase, not a doubling. You'd need to cut churn to 2.5% to double lifetime to 40 months.

A few caveats before celebrating too hard:

- One month's change can be noise, especially with a small customer base. Look at a trailing 3-month average, or cohort retention curves.
- Blended churn can move because of mix shifts (e.g., a batch of new customers who churn early, or a big annual-contract cohort not up for renewal this month), not a real retention improvement.
- The 1/churn formula assumes churn stays constant over a customer's life. In practice churn is usually highest early and falls for older cohorts, so the real lifetime can differ from this estimate.
- Check revenue churn alongside logo churn. If the customers you're keeping are the small ones, revenue retention might tell a different story.

Yes, you're right, and it's not pedantic. It's one of the most common and consequential misreadings of a p-value.

A p-value of 0.03 means: if the null hypothesis were true (and the model assumptions hold), there's a 3% probability of seeing data at least as extreme as what you observed. It's P(data this extreme | null is true).

"97% chance the hypothesis is true" is a statement about P(hypothesis | data). Those are different quantities, and the gap between them can be huge. It's the same error as confusing P(positive test | no disease) with P(no disease | positive test).

To actually get the probability that a hypothesis is true, you need more than the p-value: the prior plausibility of the hypothesis and the power of the study. For example, if only 10% of hypotheses your team tests are really true and your studies have 80% power, then among results significant at p < 0.05, a substantial fraction (roughly a third) are false positives. That's nowhere near "97% true." This is a big part of why so many published findings with p < 0.05 fail to replicate.

Why it matters practically: treating p = 0.03 as 97% certainty leads to overconfidence in single experiments, shipping changes based on noise, and skipping replication. A p-value also says nothing about effect size, so a tiny, unimportant effect can have a small p-value with enough data.

If you want a source to point your colleague to, the American Statistical Association's 2016 statement on p-values addresses exactly this: "P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true."
