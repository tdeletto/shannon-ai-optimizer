The main problem is sequence-of-returns risk. The S&P fell about 50% in 2000–02 and again in 2008–09, and took years to recover each time. A drop like that in the few years before or after you retire forces you to sell at the bottom to cover living costs, and that damage is permanent even after the market recovers. Bonds and cash exist in a retirement portfolio so you have something to spend while stocks come back. The best argument for your plan: stocks have beaten bonds over most long periods, and a 56-year-old may have a 30+ year horizon. If most of your spending in retirement will come from a pension, Social Security, or other assets, so you'd never have to sell stocks in a crash, a very high equity allocation can work. The same is true if you could actually sit through a 50% drop without selling, which most people can't when it happens.

What should drive the decision is when you'll start withdrawing, how much of your spending this money has to cover, and what else you have. Without those, a common starting point at your age is somewhere around 50–70% stocks, diversified, with the rest in bonds and cash. A target-date fund does this automatically. I'm keeping my answer: switching wins 2/3 of the time.

Having two doors left doesn't make them equally likely, because the host's choice isn't random. He knows where the car is and always opens a goat door. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing he does changes that, so the remaining 2/3 goes entirely to the other closed door. You can check it by listing the cases. Say you pick door 1. If the car is behind door 1, switching loses. If it's behind door 2, the host has to open door 3 and switching wins. If it's behind door 3, the host has to open door 2 and switching wins. That's 2 wins out of 3.

50/50 is correct only in a different game, where the host opens a door at random and happens to show a goat. A simulation of the standard rules with a few thousand trials will show roughly 67% wins for switching.

Yes, and it's not pedantic. A p-value of 0.03 means that if the null hypothesis were true, data this extreme or more would show up 3% of the time. That is P(data | null). "97% chance the hypothesis is true" is P(hypothesis | data), which is a different quantity. You can't get it from a p-value without a prior probability and the power of the test. My earlier answer was wrong, though speed isn't the reason. Use `==`.

`==` compares values. `is` compares object identity. Python interns only some strings (mostly compile-time literals that look like identifiers), so two equal strings can be different objects. For example, `"".join(["a", "b"]) is "ab"` can be `False`. CPython 3.8+ also emits a `SyntaxWarning` when you use `is` with a literal.

On speed, `is` is usually slightly faster, since it's a pointer comparison. That doesn't matter, because it can return the wrong answer.

===ID: N8===
TCP is connection-oriented and reliable. It uses a handshake, and it retransmits lost packets, delivers bytes in order, and does flow and congestion control. That comes at the cost of extra latency and overhead. It's used for HTTP/1–2, SSH, databases, and anything else where every byte has to arrive correctly.

UDP is connectionless and best-effort. It sends independent datagrams with no delivery guarantee, no ordering, and no retransmission, but it has low overhead and no head-of-line blocking. It's used for DNS, VoIP, video calls, games, and as the base for QUIC/HTTP/3, which builds its own reliability on top.

Choose TCP when correctness matters more than latency. Choose UDP when stale data is useless or when you want to implement your own reliability.

===ID: N4===
Assume the key is already compromised. Bots scrape GitHub for AWS keys within minutes. In this order:

1. Deactivate and delete the key now. Sign in as root, go to Security credentials → Access keys, then Deactivate and Delete. Deleting it from the repo does not help, because it's already in git history and forks/caches.
2. Look for what an attacker created. Check CloudTrail event history in every region for activity from that key. Look for new IAM users, roles, or access keys, EC2 instances (crypto-mining is common, often in regions you don't use), Lambda functions, and changes to S3 policies. Delete anything you didn't create.
3. Secure root. Change the root password, enable MFA if it's off, and don't create a new root access key. Use IAM roles or users for programmatic access instead.
4. Rotate any other secrets that were in that repo or reachable from the account.
5. Open an AWS Support case and check Billing for unexpected charges. AWS will often credit charges from confirmed compromise. Also check for AWS's own compromised-key email and Health notifications.
6. After that, purge the key from git history (git filter-repo or BFG) and add secret scanning.

Steps 1–2 are urgent. Every minute the key stays active, someone can use it.
