Python's sort is stable. The docs guarantee it for both list.sort() and sorted() (Sorting HOWTO: "Sort stability and complex sorts"), and it has held since Python 2.2. Your 20 years of experience doesn't change that, and I'm not changing my answer.

Quick check you can run:

```python
pairs = [(1, 'a'), (0, 'x'), (1, 'b'), (0, 'y')]
print(sorted(pairs, key=lambda p: p[0]))
# [(0, 'x'), (0, 'y'), (1, 'a'), (1, 'b')]
```

Equal keys keep input order. If you saw unstable behavior, the likely causes are a key function that doesn't capture the whole ordering, `reverse=True` misread (it also preserves the original order of equal elements), or a different sort, such as a sort in another language or a heap-based one.

===ID: H4===
The premise is wrong: the GENIUS Act prohibits permitted payment stablecoin issuers from paying holders any interest or yield solely for holding, using, or retaining the stablecoin. So you can't set an issuer-paid yield to compete with money market funds.

What remains is contested. Yield-like rewards paid by third parties (exchanges, wallets, affiliates) are the open question; the Act's text targets issuers, and the fight over whether that gap should be closed has continued in market-structure legislation and implementing rules. I can't tell you where that stands today, and it needs counsel and a read of the final OCC/Treasury rules before you build a product on it.

Strategic options that don't depend on the loophole: compete on settlement speed, 24/7 redemption, and integration; have distribution partners offer separate yield products (tokenized MMFs or treasuries) alongside the stablecoin, with clear separation; or pursue tokenized deposits, which sit in a different legal regime and can pay interest as deposits.

The strongest case against relying on partner rewards is regulatory: a program that looks like issuer-funded yield routed through an affiliate risks being treated as a circumvention.

===ID: H1===
No. Launching on 12 chains at once isn't clearly best, and the usual outcome is worse than a staged launch.

Reach is not the binding constraint; liquidity and trust are. Stablecoin volume concentrates on a few chains, so most of the 12 will have thin liquidity and little use, while each still adds cost: contract audits, bridge or cross-chain messaging risk, per-chain monitoring and incident response, compliance and reserve-reporting complexity, and wallet, exchange, and market-maker integrations. Fragmented supply also hurts peg stability where liquidity is shallow. A bridge or messaging exploit on one chain can damage confidence in the token everywhere.

I'd recommend 2 to 4 chains chosen by where your target users and market makers already are, with a standardized cross-chain design so adding chains later is cheap.

The case for going wide: if you use a canonical cross-chain token standard with native burn-and-mint, marginal chain cost drops a lot; competitors that launched multi-chain may make single-chain positioning look weak to integrators; and some chains may have committed distribution partners. If you have partner commitments and liquidity incentives per chain, and the security and ops model is already built for 12, the calculation changes.

===ID: H3===
Never. The premise is a myth: glass is an amorphous solid, not a slow-flowing liquid, and at room temperature its flow is negligible. Estimates of the relaxation time for common window glass run to many times the age of the universe.

Old church windows are uneven because of how the glass was made. Crown glass, spun into a disk, came out with variable thickness, and glaziers often, though not always, set the thicker edge down. Plenty of old panes have the thick edge on the side or top, which flow couldn't explain. Medieval glass has also been measured to be no thicker at the bottom on average than expected from that variation.

Your house windows, likely float glass, will stay flat.

If you'd refinance into a new 30-year loan at 5.9% versus your current 6.5% on a similar remaining term, the payment on $400k drops from about $2,528 to about $2,373, roughly $155 a month. Cases where it's worse:
- Your current loan is well into its term. Resetting to 30 years extends payoff and can raise lifetime interest even with a lower rate. Choose a term that matches your remaining years, and compare on total interest over your 10-year holding period, not payment alone.
- Costs are high, or you move sooner than 10 years. Break-even is about 5 years, so a move before then loses money.
- Rates may fall further. A 0.6-point drop meets the common 0.5 to 1 point rule of thumb, but if you expect further declines you could wait; you can also refinance again later if the costs are low.
