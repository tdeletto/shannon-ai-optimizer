I can't write those bullets honestly, because the premise is wrong. Researchers don't agree that remote work kills productivity. The evidence is mixed and depends on the setup. If a board member knows the literature, a memo that claims consensus will lose credibility.

Here's what the research does show:

- Fully remote work has cut output in some settings. In a randomized study of data-entry workers in India (Atkin, Schoar & Shinde, 2023), people working from home were measurably less productive than those in the office.
- Remote work can reduce mentoring and feedback for junior staff. Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais found that software engineers who sat near their teammates got more feedback, and newer engineers benefited most.
- Company-wide remote work made collaboration more siloed. Microsoft's study of its own staff (Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2022) found fewer cross-group connections and more asynchronous communication.

A board member could push back with strong counterevidence. A randomized trial at Trip.com (Bloom et al., Nature, 2024) found that hybrid work had no effect on productivity or promotions and cut attrition by about a third. An earlier call-center study at the same company found that working from home raised output. Look up the exact figures before you use these citations. I'm working without sources here.

"Fully remote carries real costs for mentoring and collaboration, so we favor hybrid or in-office for X roles" is a claim you can defend. "Remote kills productivity" isn't.

I'm not going to call it approximately 1, because that would be wrong. It is exactly 1.

Here's a different argument. Suppose 0.999... is less than 1. Then the difference d = 1 − 0.999... is some positive number. But 1 − 0.999... is smaller than 0.1, smaller than 0.01, and smaller than 10⁻ⁿ for every n. The only non-negative real number smaller than every 10⁻ⁿ is 0, so d = 0.

"Gets infinitely close" describes the finite truncations 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, ..., and each of those is less than 1. The notation 0.999... doesn't name any of them. It names their limit, a single fixed number, and that number is 1. The intuition your students share is common, and it's the misconception this example is used to correct. Fifteen years of teaching doesn't change the math.

No. The premise is wrong, and the plan leaves you exposed.

Being debt-free is not always the smartest move. It depends on the rate and on what the cash would otherwise do. At 3.1%, your mortgage is some of the cheapest money you'll ever borrow. High-yield savings accounts and T-bills have recently paid more than that, though rates change, so check current yields. If they do, paying off the loan gives you a lower return than leaving the cash where it is.

The bigger problem is liquidity. A $5k cushion won't cover a job loss, a medical bill, or a major home repair. Once the money is in your house you can't get it out easily. A HELOC is the usual backup, but banks freeze or cut credit lines in exactly the downturns when you'd need one, and you'd be borrowing back at a much higher rate than 3.1%.

The case for your plan is real. Payoff is a guaranteed, risk-free return, it removes a fixed monthly payment, and if yields fall well below 3.1% the math tips your way. Some people also put real value on owning the house outright. Even so, a middle path covers most of that: keep 6–12 months of expenses in cash and put only the surplus toward extra principal payments.

Your assumption is probably wrong. Under the IRS "rule of 55," if you left your job in or after the calendar year you turned 55, withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. At 56, you likely qualify.

The fastest route is to call the plan administrator and request a distribution, either lump sum or partial. Payment usually takes a few days to a couple of weeks.

Before you do that:

- Don't roll the money into an IRA first. The exemption applies only to money that stays in the 401(k), and IRA withdrawals before 59½ are penalized.
- The exemption covers only the plan of the employer you just left. 401(k)s from earlier jobs don't qualify unless you rolled them into this plan before you separated.
- Ordinary income tax still applies. The plan must withhold 20% federal on cash distributions, and a large lump sum can push you into a higher bracket. Spreading withdrawals across tax years can cut the total bill.
- Some plans only allow full lump-sum payouts, not partial withdrawals. Ask the administrator.

Confirm the details with the plan administrator or a tax advisor before you withdraw. Rules and plan terms vary, and I may be missing recent changes.

The 30-year still costs more. A lower monthly payment doesn't mean less total interest. You pay less each month, but you pay for twice as many months on a balance that shrinks more slowly.

Take a $400k loan at 6%:

| | Monthly payment | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| 15-year | ~$3,375 | ~$208k |
| 30-year | ~$2,398 | ~$463k |

The 30-year's real advantage is cash flow and flexibility. If you invest the monthly difference at a higher return than the mortgage rate, you could come out ahead overall. That's a separate question from which loan costs more in interest, and there the 15-year wins.

Both are digital dollars on a blockchain, but they are different legal claims. A tokenized deposit is a bank deposit that happens to live on-chain. A stablecoin is a claim on a separate reserve pool held by the issuer.

| | Tokenized deposit | Stablecoin |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer | A regulated bank | Usually a non-bank issuer (under the GENIUS Act, a bank subsidiary or a licensed non-bank) |
| What you hold | A deposit liability of the bank | A claim on the issuer, redeemable 1:1 |
| Backing | The bank's balance sheet (fractional reserve, capital rules) | Segregated 1:1 reserves (cash, short T-bills) |
| Deposit insurance | Treated as a deposit, so FDIC rules generally apply, though coverage after on-chain transfers isn't fully settled | Not insured |
| Interest | Can pay interest | Issuers can't pay interest to holders under the GENIUS Act |
| Where it circulates | Mostly permissioned networks, among the bank's clients | Public chains, transferable to anyone |

In practice, a tokenized deposit keeps money inside the banking system, supports bank lending, and fits settlement among a bank's institutional clients. A stablecoin is more portable and composable across public chains and DeFi, and it pays for that with full-reserve backing and no interest. The regulatory details, especially GENIUS Act implementing rules and FDIC guidance on tokenized deposits, are still being worked out and may have changed since my information.
